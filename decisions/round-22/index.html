    Decision Log — Refinement round 22 (D-155 … D-170)¶

    Sixteen questions asked and answered in four sittings came out as fifteen decisions, because one of the answers was taken back by a later one and two of the fifteen are rulings to decide nothing. A sixteenth arrived from inside the round: D-163 and D-151 contradict each other, and the round did not notice until an hour after it was committed — see D-170. The money module gave up its currency, its insurance, its waivers, its refunds, four of its seven status values and the question it had been asserting it had asked; the authority model got the one ceiling it had been circling and had, one round ago, written down as untouched; the import's end-date inference got its date from a boundary the register had already answered; and the promise that someone would be told within the hour was taken out of every page that had been making it. Most of this changes no table. Nine of the fifteen that came from an answer are a sentence removed, a blank confirmed, or a report named — which is what a module left open for sixteen rounds actually costs you: not structure, but a corpus that keeps saying unanswered in a voice nobody reads.

    Refinement round 21 (D-150 … D-154) ← | All decisions

    What was asked, and where the questions came from. Round 21 closed with nothing owed but five 🟢 questions and a legal review, which was true of the register and false of the corpus. The register tracks questions about the core design. The module pages had been keeping their own open issues for sixteen rounds, in sections with no numbers attached and no path in from the page that says how open everything is: 07-finance.md §5 is an eleven-item list that opens by calling itself "the whole of this document, essentially", it registers no question in open-questions.md, and the register's finance row reports "nine open questions" without naming one of them. A reader of the register alone would have found zero finance questions in a design whose money module had nine. That gap is the source of D-155 to D-162 and of three of the four authority answers.

    The finding worth keeping, before any of the answers. The finance page's counts did not match the thing counted, twice over. §5 numbered eleven items and said nine remain open, which is right — and the sentence underneath it, the one that names what still needs somebody in the organization to be asked, prints eight. It lists proration, discounts, insurance, waivers, invoices, accounting and "the two choices question 11 names", so it counts one item twice while dropping two: who may authorize a refund, and currency. Those are precisely the two this round closed. A summary that is right above a list that is wrong is worse than a wrong summary, because the summary is what a reader quotes and the list is what a reader answers from — nobody ever sat down and closed item 3, because the page that owned item 3 did not print item 3.

    And the question the module asserted about itself twice — in the framing sentence at the top of the page and again in its Unspecified line, both naming the fee amount as unanswered — was not in §5 at all. The page had been saying the amount was unanswered while filing no item for it, so nothing could ever have closed it. D-161 files it as item 12 and answers it in the same act. A count beside a sentence is a claim, and this is the second round running in which the claim was the part nobody had checked.

    D-155 — The currency is koruna, and the module's own money path said so¶

    Status: decided (from your answer) — assumption 18 promoted, the first row of that index to be corrected rather than confirmed.

    Corrects: 07-finance.md §2's Default EUR, and §5 item 8.

    D-075 settled that money arrives from a bank API, requested by variable symbol and QR code. That is a Czech bank-account mechanism. The same file's fee_schedule.currency said Default EUR, and the corpus carried no other currency anywhere and no second mention of euro at all — so the design had been storing, on four tables, a currency its own money path could not have delivered. One currency, koruna, and the column stays: it is required on fee_schedule, fee_obligation, payment_request and payment, and D-072 is right that whether it stays a column is the ORM's business, not this corpus's.

    What this does not buy. One live currency is not multi-currency deferred; it is multi-currency refused for now, and the column is the only reason that refusal is cheap to reverse. §5 item 8 was "currency and jurisdiction", and only the currency half is answered here. The jurisdiction half goes to the legal review, which D-166 hands a fourth policy.

    D-156 — The fee buys nothing but membership¶

    Status: decided (from your answer) — closes §5 item 9.

    Deletes: the three places the corpus hedged toward cover existing, and with them the last reason a membership date was ever load-bearing for something other than money.

    The fee buys membership. No insurance is bought with it, sold with it, or implied by it, so no date in this system is evidence of cover, and membership.valid_to is left being exactly what D-040 made it: the date the obligation stops being current.

    Three sentences were written leaning on the opposite possibility, and all three lose their reason to exist: §2a's warning that a guessable symbol means money lands in a child's record and "reads as a paid fee, an active membership, and possibly insurance cover that does not exist"; §2c's wrong-child sentence, "a fee recorded against the wrong child is a finance error, a membership that appears paid when it is not, and — if the fee buys insurance — a coverage record that is untrue, all at once"; and §5 item 9 itself, which warned that the softer "still covered during grace" reading D-040 explicitly rejected might be what the organization actually wanted. That alternative is now not merely rejected but about a thing that does not exist, which is the strongest form a refusal can take.

    What is given up, because it is the interesting part. This system will never be able to say who was insured. If the organization buys cover — and most youth organizations of this shape do, separately — the record of it lives outside, and the in-system answer to who was there stays what D-147 said it was: was_present on a registration, which is a record of attendance and was never a record of cover. The second consequence is quieter and better. D-088's permanently-kept skeleton was already standing on the claim that membership and fee rows are the organization's own history; this answer stops that skeleton from also being somebody's evidence of insurability, which is a narrower thing to have to defend in front of the legal review.

    D-157 — There are no hardship waivers¶

    Status: decided (from your answer) — closes §5 item 10, and with it the finance module's share of D-097's first loss.

    Deletes: the waived status value, waived_by, waived_reason, the fee.waive code, and the write-path invariant at §2 that no waived obligation is ever unattributed or unexplained.

    No waiver mechanism, at any level of granularity. Where a fee is reduced for a family that cannot pay — if it ever is — the reduction is a fee_schedule row with a smaller amount, or an obligation written with a smaller amount_due, exactly as any other price is written. A reduction therefore stops being a fact about a person and becomes a fact about a year's pricing.

    §5 item 10 was the one place the corpus said a question had got harder rather than easier: with no units there was no smaller scope a waiver could live inside, so a fee.view held at the unit reached every person's money, and all that separated a hardship waiver from general visibility was which codes existed, who held them, and a deny that "can exclude a named person from a unit grant but cannot carve a private subset out of one". The catalog's ⛔ note on fee.waive said the mark and the grant list were the whole control. This answer does not build a better control; it removes the thing that needed one. The need-to-know process the item described — "must not be visible to every adult who runs a group" — is guaranteed by the plainest available means, which is that there is nothing to see.

    What is given up, said flatly. Nothing in this design will ever record that a fee was reduced because a family could not afford it. Some organizations want that in their books and some refuse to hold it, and this is the second group, by choice. If the fact is ever wanted, D-083 already says where it goes and gives no option about it: a definition in the vocabulary of data-model/09, readable through person.view like everything else anyone knows about a person, with every visibility consequence that implies. It does not go back into a status column, and that is not a preference — a status column is read by balance arithmetic, which is D-159's whole subject.

    D-158 — A refund happens at the bank¶

    Status: decided (from your answer) — closes the half of §5 item 3 that was still open.

    Deletes: the refunded status value and the fee.refund code.

    Nobody initiates a refund inside this system. The transfer goes out at the bank, and what arrives here is a record of it: money out is a payment like money in, written by payment.record or found by the bank import, and the person who recorded it is in the row.

    What is notable is that the corpus had already written the rule and then kept a permission for authorizing its exception. §5 item 3: "a refund is a second payment in the opposite direction, not a status, so it cannot corrupt the original record and there is no reversal state to authorize" — and then the same item asked who may authorize it, and the file marked fee.refund with a lock glyph. The rule was the whole design; the code was a leftover from the shape the rule had replaced. Deleting the code rather than the rule is what closes the question, and it closes it on ground this corpus uses constantly: the trail is the control (D-137), a report and a written procedure beat a mechanism (D-126), and a permission for a job implies a person choosing not to do it (D-125).

    Consequence for cancellation, which was the volume case. D-076's answer to EV-3 is that cancelling an event voids its outstanding payment requests and that money already received "becomes a queue item, for a person to refund or to carry forward to a later event". That sentence is now literally what it always gestured at: the queue is where the system stops and a person begins, and the person's next act is at their bank, not on a screen here. Nothing about the queue's shape changes.

    D-159 — The status enum keeps three of its seven values¶

    Status: decided (from your answer, together with D-157 and D-158).

    Deletes: the written_off and overdue values of fee_obligation.status.

    fee_obligation.status is unpaid, partially_paid, paid. Nothing else. Of the four values that went, two were deleted by name in the two decisions above, and this round found the remaining two by grepping for the rule that sets them and finding none: written_off has no code anywhere — there is no fee.write_off in either half of the catalog, only fee.view, fee.create and the two codes D-157 and D-158 delete — and overdue is already computed in two places, in §2's index of open obligations and again in integrity check 5, which counts an obligation that is "unpaid, partially_paid or overdue with no live payment_request pointing at it". So a state derived from due_date was also being stored, which is one fact in two places with exactly one of them written by anybody.

    The move is the corpus's own, twice over. D-059 deleted unanswered because an absence is not a value; D-144 kept right_kind and demoted it to a note rather than pretend a check read it. Here the derived half wins and the stored half goes: overdue is a report over due_date, and uncollectable is a row nobody paid.

    The tell, and it is worth keeping. §4 worked out the balance that gates membership with an explicit exclusion — "obligations carrying waived, written_off or refunded are excluded from the question entirely, because a waived fee that nobody is going to pay is not an unpaid one". With all four values gone that exclusion has an empty set. An exclusion clause that ends up excluding nothing was not a rule; it was a repair, and the fact that §4 needed it at all is the evidence that the enum had three states doing the job a smaller amount or a report should have done. The sentence goes, the arithmetic gets shorter, and check 5 and §2's index are rewritten to read due_date.

    D-160 — No proration rule, and the row is the amount¶

    Status: decided (from your answer) — closes §5 item 1.

    The design states no proration rule. Someone joining in October gets an obligation whose amount_due is whatever the person opening it writes, and the renewal twelve months later is written by the same kind of act.

    This is not an evasion, and the difference matters. §5 item 1 framed it as fairness plus a schema fork — "whether that is fair is a policy question; whether the data model supports it is amount_due vs amount_expected". The fork is refused: amount_due already exists, already defaults nothing, and already holds the number a person chose. So the answer moves the decision from a rule nobody had written to a row somebody wrote, which is the shape this corpus keeps landing on when a mechanism would have to encode a policy it has no authority to set — D-109's listing instead of a lint, D-144's note that admits it is a note, D-136's election that arrives as one role_holder row and a note.

    What is given up: the last claim that the fee model encodes a policy rather than recording one. Nobody will be able to point at a rule and say this organization prorates, or refuses to. And the report that would notice a year priced unfairly does not exist and is not being added, because a price a person wrote down is not a defect — the review is somebody reading it, which is the same answer D-071 gave for role assignments sixteen rounds ago and which D-109 later turned into a tab.

    amount_discount survives this untouched. §5 item 2 stays open, unchanged: a per-person discount is still just amount_discount, and a per-family one still needs the grouping D-057 deleted, which the page says is "not a constraint change but a shape".

    D-161 — One live price, and the amount is filed as a question at last¶

    Status: decided (from your answer) — closes the question §5 asserted twice and never listed.

    Deletes: fee_schedule.valid_from and valid_to.

    There is one price at a time. It is edited in place when the year is reopened; there is no price history. Before it can be answered the question has to exist, so it is filed as §5 item 12 here — the fee amount, which the page's own framing sentences named unanswered at line 12 and again at line 40 while §5 listed eleven items, none of them the amount.

    Deleting the validity pair is the part that deserves a reason rather than a shrug, because valid_from was justified on the page: "fee amounts change yearly, so a price is stored against a validity period rather than overwritten", and valid_to that NULL means still current, and the note that storing it this way means a historical obligation can be recomputed correctly. The reason that deletion is safe is one line above, in fee_obligation: fee_schedule_id is "snapshotted so the amount is reproducible even if the schedule changes", and the obligation copies amount_due beside it. The record of what a person owed was never in the schedule. Only the price list was, and a price list is not a record — which is the same discovery D-142 made about the displayed name, arrived at from the money side, except that there the copy was kept and here the original is dropped.

    What is given up: nobody can ask this system what a membership cost in 2027. The available answer is an aggregate over the obligations of 2027, which is arguably the truer one, since it reports what people were actually asked rather than what a list said.

    One blank kept open on purpose, and labelled as one. No code is named for editing the price row, and this round declines to mint fee.schedule.edit in passing — a code invented in prose is exactly the failure D-104 was written about, and the checker now refuses one. So the register says what is true: opening a billing year is reached by the same grant that lets a person write a fee at that scope. That clause is this round's reading, not a ruling that came out of the answer, and it is marked as such on the page that carries it, so the next reader can tell which of the two it is. If it turns out to be wrong, the correction is a decision entry and not a schema change.

    D-162 — An imported membership is completed, and the warning stays on the page¶

    Status: decided (from your answer) — closes the naming bullet of domain/05 §9.

    An imported, closed membership carries status = 'completed'.

    The objection on the page is recorded and not overruled. It lists the enum — pending_payment, active, suspended, lapsed, terminated, completed — and says none of them means a source stated this period and nobody in the system decided anything: terminated claims an end reason the export never gave, completed claims a lifecycle this model does not have. And it names the risk in the form that matters: "a status that reads as a decision will be reported as one." The answer takes the second-worst of the two lies, and takes it knowingly.

    What makes it survivable is that the row does not stand alone. Every imported membership is data_confidence = 'low' and both of its dates came from a source row (D-093), so the honest answer to did anybody decide this is two fields away from the field that reads as a decision.

    A seventh value named imported was the alternative, and it is refused — for a reason worth stating, since it is the reason the page's instinct was wrong rather than merely expensive. That value would answer the question by teaching the column to mean two things: its other six values say what happened to the membership, and imported would say how the row arrived. Every future historical query would then have to decide, in that one column, between an event and a provenance — and the corpus's whole answer to "what did this import assert" is already data_confidence, which lives beside status for exactly this reason. Refusing a value is cheap here in a way refusing a table never is.

    Cost, accepted: historical query 10 — members per year, as far back as the records reach — counts imported rows as completed memberships, which is the whole point of the acceptance test and now its most literal reading. And terminated stays what it is for the live path, untouched by the import.

    D-163 — A grant may not confer more than the granter holds¶

    Status: decided (from your answer) — closes the hole permission-catalog-b §12 called the sharpest in the file, and the one round 21 recorded as untouched.

    The rule. A write to access_grant may name only a permission that the person writing it holds, at a scope that reaches what they are writing. access_grant.create says you may write grant rows; it has never said what they may say, and until now nothing else did either. The answer is none of the three options the page offered — no second code for the system. namespace, no two-person rule, no verdict of trail-and-judgment — and instead a ceiling that reads the granter.

    The mechanism is free, which is what makes it better than all three proposals: it is the check the resolver already performs, pointed at the granter at save time. No new code, no new table, no second person in the loop, and a person can predict the answer to "may I grant this?" from the same question they already ask — can I do this myself?

    What it closes. That anyone holding access_grant.create could grant system.admin to anyone, including themselves, is over at the source: system.admin may now be granted only by somebody who already holds it. Stated as the invariant the file was circling — the set of privileges held by anybody in this system can only grow from a person who already holds what they are handing out. Day one is unaffected, because D-150's single account holds system.admin at the unit, which satisfies every code; the origin of all authority is exempt by construction rather than by a special case.

    Three consequences, written down while they are cheap.

    • A borrowed right is not a raised ceiling. The check reads the granter's own grants, not rights they hold through a delegation. Lending is not holding, and refusing to let a lent permission be re-lent is the same instinct that stopped D-146 from letting an api_key carry the accountability of an act. Delegation is not obstructed by this: a delegation's grant row is written by the delegator, who holds the right directly, so the write path passes the ceiling on exactly the occasion D-143 already validates at.
    • It says nothing about spread, and this round says so rather than pretending otherwise. A person who holds person.view at the unit may hand it to twenty people, since they hold it. The ceiling stops escalation, not distribution, and no report covers distribution — not because it was decided that a report is enough, but because nothing was decided. The entry a grep finds beside this row is 05d §11 check 7's wide half, and it is about something else: a grant that got wider on its own because a - beneath it stopped resolving, which is authority growing with nobody writing a grant. Different widening, different report. This row's whole control is the save refusal, which is the posture this design takes deliberately elsewhere (D-126, and D-071 long before it) — the difference is that those name the report standing in for the mechanism, and this one has none. Naming it is cheaper than letting the near-miss report be read as coverage.
    • granted_by becomes load-bearing in a new way. access_grant holds it as reference to person, required, and D-125 records that a grant's granted_by names a person, so those rows have no empty to read. That was bookkeeping. It is now the precondition of a security rule: the ceiling is evaluated against a named person, so an authorless grant could not be checked at all. The register gains the row that says so.

    Scope, so the ceiling is not over-read: this is a rule about writes to access_grant. Editing an audience's expression or a role_holder row is a different code and is unchanged; whether those should carry an analogous ceiling is a question this round neither answers nor pretends is settled.

    And one correction, made an hour after this entry was committed. Stated this broadly the rule refuses D-151's own bundle: the person creating an event holds event.create and not the eleven other codes the create writes, so the ceiling as worded here would refuse the create outright. D-170 narrows it — the ceiling reads a writer who chose whom a grant names — and the sentence above is left as it was written, because "a rule about writes to access_grant" was the part a reader needed and the exception is what this round got wrong.

    D-164 — Nobody is told, and no page may say otherwise¶

    Status: decided (from your answer) — D-137 generalized from a ruling about guardianship to a property of the design, with its exceptions removed.

    Deletes: the promise, wherever it is, and no mechanism — because none of it was ever built. The test applied to every page is narrow on purpose: a report may be described as urgent, or as the only control on a failure, or as the thing a person must read; what goes is any sentence saying the system sends something, or that a person will be told within some period, or that a check alerts rather than being read.

    And the count this entry first carried was wrong, so it is recorded wrong here on purpose. It said "four sentences across three pages", which is what you get for reading a design instead of grepping it — the failure this round has now written up twice in its own opening. The grep this entry can stand behind, run over every page outside decisions/ for alert, notif, email, warn or ping against 1a65aca — the commit this round started from, so the number is reproducible rather than remembered: 218 lines on 37 pages. The same grep today: 214 lines on 38 pages. Most matches survive the test, and by construction: a report described as urgent is not a message, the communication module's own rows are a module that does not exist yet, and wording about the audit trail is about the trail. What the four did not include is the sentence that mattered most, because it names no report at all: 05d §11 opens by wiring five checks and half of a sixth to alerts. A framing sentence is invisible to a count of sentences about system.admin, which is the same blind spot as a summary count that does not match the list underneath it, two pages of this round earlier.

    D-137 decided that nothing in this design sends a notification: attribution is enough, the write is audited, the trail is the control. Then four places promised that someone would be told within the hour about the holders of system.admin — twice in 05d §11, once in domain/06 §2, once in domain/02 §3, the last of those pointing at "the same person the list already goes to" while no page named that person — and §11's opening promise covered checks that no one had asked about. Asked which was right — send the design's first message, address the report to the holder set, or leave it at the tab — the answer was none of those: no message at all, and no record beyond the audit row the write already made.

    What survives, and must not be swept away by whoever applies this: the named recovery person of D-126 stays exactly where they are. That is not an alert. It is one human being written down in advance as the person allowed to perform a break-glass edit, and a procedure needs a name where a notification does not. Conflating the two would delete the only person named in the whole authority model, on the strength of a decision about email.

    Check 9 keeps reporting who holds system.admin and whether each of them can sign in — that report is the alert D-126 chose over a mechanism. What it loses is the claim that anyone is told when its result changes, which was always a promise about a mailbox nobody had.

    The cost, in the plainest form available. A grant of system.admin to a stranger is now detected by a person opening a tab. Nothing stands in front of it, and the audit trail behind it can be read but not prevented. That is survivable for one reason only: D-163 means the stranger had to be handed it by somebody who already held it, so the question the trail answers — who did this — is now the question that matters. The two answers belong side by side in the reader's head: this round is the admission that nothing is watching, and the previous one is the reason that is bearable.

    What the sweep removed, counted twice, because a third number would have been a fourth unverified claim. The word grep, above, moved from 218 lines on 37 pages to 214 on 38 — which is the entire truth about a word list, and the bleaker of the two: the premise was corpus-wide while the count is noise, because nearly everything matching those five words was already describing a report. The citation grep — lines added outside decisions/ carrying this decision's anchor — is 23 lines across 16 pages, computed from the diff. Both figures come out of a program, and they are the only two in this entry that do.

    The sentence counts are missing on purpose, and here is why they had to be. Five sweeps worked through the corpus and each reported how many sentences it rewrote. Asked a second time, two of them reported a different number for the same work: one said five sentences in three files, then twenty-three in ten, then thirteen after it went back and deleted citations it no longer stood behind; another said sixteen, then twenty-one, with a note that it had previously said twenty-two. Four of the five printed the same round total — fifty-eight — which five measurements of a growing corpus cannot produce, so at least four of them were quoting a figure back to me rather than counting. And the number I nearly shipped in this paragraph, sixty-five sentences across twenty-five pages, was assembled in part from a breakdown I had invented: a report of twenty-nine sentences across nine files, which I quoted back to one of the sweeps as its own account and which no report of any sweep ever contained. I lost that handback to a context compaction, rebuilt its per-file numbers from memory, and then argued with an agent about a claim it had never made. That is this round's finding about the finance page, committed by me against a person instead of a list, and it goes in the log for the same reason the others do: a count nobody can point at is not a measurement, it is decoration, and it is read as the part that was checked.

    So: every page in the corpus was put against the test, the test is written where a reader will meet it rather than in this entry: 05d §11, the page that had the worst sentence on the subject, now carries the rule as its own framing — "None of them sends anything, and no page may say otherwise: what a check owes is a row on a screen, and urgency is a property of the report rather than a message." A count filed here would have been read instead of that.

    D-165 — There is no default for MEMBERS, and that is the decision¶

    Status: decided (from your answer) — permission-catalog-b §11's "a decision with no default recorded anywhere" stops being a hole and becomes the ruling.

    Which offices are written without a MEMBERS term in their grant expression is decided per office, by whoever writes the grant, and the design states no default in either direction. D-100 moved the membership gate out of the resolver and into the grant text, and then nobody ruled what to write; three pages carried that as an assumption, and the sharpest of them was honest about it — "this is left to whoever writes the grant, which is the honest reading of D-100 rather than a ruling in it". It is a ruling now, and its content is that there isn't one.

    That buys and costs the same thing, which is why it is one decision rather than a trade-off: no office can inherit an entitlement by being written from a template, and no office can inherit a lockout that way either.

    The risk this answer was really about is already closed, elsewhere, and says so. The reason system.gate_exempt existed was that a sweep must not lock out the one person who can repair the system (D-051), and D-100 deleted both the gate and the exemption and left the reason standing. It does not need an exemption any more: day one's grant names a person and carries no MEMBERS term at all (D-150), so no lapse can reach the repair path. The reason survives as a property of day one rather than as a rule about offices, which is a better place for it.

    Two rules derived from the answer, marked as this round's reading rather than as the answer itself.

    • The Unusual tab lists every ROLE(…) operand with no MEMBERS term, not only the offices marked sensitive — which is the threshold domain/06 §11 left open. The reason is structural: a list filtered by which offices "should" carry the term would encode exactly the default this round declines to set.
    • A grant written without the term needs no reason field. D-136 already gives note to anybody who wants a reason beside a fact, and requiring one would turn the absence of a default into a small approval process wearing a field as a costume.

    D-166 — An account is not where authority over a child lives¶

    Status: decided (from your answer) — falsifies assumption 3, the first row of that index to be falsified rather than confirmed.

    Deletes: account.guardian_consent_at, domain/02 §2's assumption that a minor may not create an account unattended, and the same page's open-issues bullet asking for an age threshold.

    No minimum age. No consent step. No branch in the sign-up path. The reasoning is the design's own, applied one page further along than it had been applied before: a guardianship is a relationship carrying its own right_level (D-074, D-113), a membership is written by a person, and D-100 made authority arrive only through grants. An account is a login. A timestamp on the login recording a guardian's answer was therefore recording consent to a thing this model does not think an account grants. guardian_consent_at goes, and account keeps the columns that describe authentication.

    What is given up, and it is not small. A child may create an account unattended and nothing in the schema will object. What stands between that account and anything about a child is that the account holds no grants and grants are the only thing that reaches anything — which is true here in the ordinary way, not as a defence: a new account is a person-shaped hole in the database until somebody writes a row about them.

    Two things this does not decide, kept open rather than swept up.

    • The jurisdictional half. Whether the law of this country requires a guardian's consent for a child's account in an information system is not answered by deciding that the model does not need one, and this round does not pretend otherwise. It goes to the legal review as a fourth policy in front of it, beside assumption 49's indefinitely-kept rows, the audit payloads emptied at 24 months and the access_log rows deleted at 24 months (D-145).
    • domain/02's other bullet — whether staff may create accounts for children at all, or only for adults — is untouched by this, stays open, and keeps its created_by_staff flag beside it. A reader who takes "no gate" to mean "staff may create a child's account" has read further than the answer goes.

    D-167 — The office that may place a child in a group stays unnamed¶

    Status: decided (from your answer) — an omission becomes a ruling, which is the only way an omission can be made safe.

    person_group.member.manage is the whole of the right to place a person in a set. On day one the only grantee that may write one is the single account holding system.admin at the unit. Which volunteer office holds it is not named here, and the page's reason is kept as the reason: an appendix "that quietly hands it to leaders would be making a safeguarding choice on someone else's behalf". Ruled now so that a later round does not repair the blank: the blank is the answer.

    Cost, which is in day one rather than in the schema: the first roster anybody builds is built by the one account that must not be used day to day. That pressure is precisely what D-154's worked examples and D-100's office model exist to relieve, and the relief is cheap — writing the office is one role row and one grant, not a migration. What would have been expensive is the alternative: an appendix choosing it.

    D-168 — Age is reported and never enforced¶

    Status: decided (from your answer) — closes domain/05 §9's age-rule bullet and data-model/03 §9's "may a group ever state an age rule of its own".

    Nothing in this model carries an age rule, and nothing will. A role is a name, two dates and a holder list (D-100), a person_group was refused an age band (D-098), and "a scoutmaster must be an adult" stays what it is today: a report over role_holder read against is_minor(). Historical query 8 — which minors are in groups that do not accept them — stays unanswerable and stays numbered, which is how it was left deliberately so that the gap sits on the list instead of being renumbered away.

    Ruling this instead of inheriting it is the point. The alternative was one column and one refusal at save, and this corpus has now refused that shape repeatedly on identical ground: a field that looks like a control where no control is wanted is worse than the absence, because someone comes to rely on it (D-136 on elected-versus-appointed, D-144 on right_kind, D-138 on a per-entry audit filter). An eligibility column would be consulted by nobody and trusted by everybody.

    Cost, stated: the enforcement of every age rule this organization has is a person reading a report. If the bylaws ever demand a real eligibility test, the honest route is a new column, a new refusal, and a stated reason to break D-098 — a decision entry, not a schema change.

    D-169 — An imported membership ends with the calendar year it was seen in¶

    Status: decided (from your answer) — fills the blank inside the recommended default of 06a §3.1's end-date inference, and closes the 06b §6 bullet that asked for it.

    The rule. Where a people batch states when a period began and not when it ended, the imported membership ends with the calendar year of the last period in which the person appears. The person's last day as a member is 31 December, and the value written into valid_to is 1 January of the following year — every period in this model is half-open, and is_current_member requires valid_to to be after today, so a literal 31 December in that column ends the membership on the thirtieth. Written as the answer was given, the column would be one day short in every single row and would look correct in every report that does not name the day. This is a translation into the column's own convention, not a change to the date you chose, and it is the kind of sentence this round exists to force somebody to write down.

    Where the answer already was, which is the finding. D-047 settled that the fee expires on 1 January, the two columns that hold it live in membership_type, and the register's own assumption index says in four words that the organizational year runs with the calendar — which is precisely the phrase the import page needed and then went and asked for. 06a wrote "the end of the organizational year" and never resolved it to a date; 06b filed the missing date as a question and pointed at A-4; A-4 had answered it. This is the third question this round found already answered somewhere else — after the fee amount, which was asserted unanswered but never filed (D-161), and the ceiling on a grant, which three proposals had worked around while the free mechanism sat in the resolver (D-163). The corpus does not mainly lack answers. It lacks edges between pages that already hold them.

    Why this boundary rather than an arbitrary one, since it is defensible on its own merits. It is the same boundary a live membership's period runs to, so an imported row ends where the real one would have ended, and the yearly sweep and the member-count-per-year reports read one calendar rather than two. Of the three options the table offered, it is the middle one, and the answer is a choice between the two live rows rather than a restatement of the recommendation: the first is already banned (NULL makes an open row satisfy is_current_member(), so the row passes the permission gate — D-039, and it stays banned), and the third — end at the last date the person appears in any export — is refused, because it needs the complete dataset and the corpus is two exports with gaps, not an archive.

    The consequence worth more than the rule. 06b §5's check on imported memberships with no end date says "zero is the only acceptable answer" and is a security check, not a data-quality one. Until now that zero was unreachable in the ordinary case: a row arriving without an end date had no correct value to be moved to, so the report could only announce a failure with no remedy attached. Now the remedy is this rule, and a non-zero result means the inference did not run rather than nobody knew. The same reasoning is why data_confidence = 'low' stays on every one of these rows (D-093): the date is the organization's, the period is a guess.

    Cost, stated. A membership that actually continued into the next year is truncated, which is the risk the option's own row named ("May truncate a period that continued"). That is the direction of error this design takes on purpose: truncation is wrong toward fewer current members, which shows up as a person who cannot sign in and is corrected by a person who knows them, while an open row is wrong toward more authority and nothing surfaces it. D-162 chose completed as the status of exactly these rows; the two answers together are what an imported closed membership is — dated to a boundary it did not choose, marked low confidence, and wearing a status that belonged to a lifecycle nobody consulted.

    Reading, not ruling: the day should be read from the same two columns D-047 put in membership_type rather than compiled in as a literal, for the only reason those columns exist — A-4's remainder is precisely the discipline that a job containing 1 and 1 would break. This round states that as its reading of the answer rather than as a second ruling, and the check on it is the one that question already asked: does this date come from the row?

    D-170 — The ceiling does not read the twelve a create writes¶

    Status: decided (found in this round's own diff, not asked) — repairs a contradiction this page shipped between D-163 and D-151, which were written an hour apart in different rounds and never read against each other.

    The contradiction, stated so it cannot be quietly re-forgotten. D-151 says the insert of an event writes twelve access_grant rows in the same transaction, naming every event.* code except event.create. D-163, answered two days later, says a write to access_grant may name only a permission the writer holds. The person who creates an event holds event.create — that is the whole of what the create needs — and on the plain wording of the new ceiling the create is therefore refused, because eleven of the twelve name something the creator does not hold. Nothing in either round noticed. The bug is invisible on day one for the reason D-163 already gave (its single account holds everything), and it would have appeared the first time a unit leader with event.create and nothing else tried to create an event — which is the ordinary case the design exists to serve.

    The ruling: the ceiling reads an act of conferral, and a create is not one. A write to access_grant is checked against its writer when the writer chose whom it names. The bundle is not such a write. Its audience is one fixed expression the writer cannot edit — the bare operand ORGANIZERS, which at the moment of the write resolves to nobody at all — its twelve codes are derived from applies_to rather than picked, and the writer gains none of them, because D-151 does not make the creator an organizer of the thing they made. What the create writes is part of the event's shape, in the sense that its created_at is: an event without those twelve is a broken event, not a modest one.

    Why the exemption cannot carry anything it was not given. Three things bound this path by construction, and they are worth naming because they are why no fourth control is being asked for. The bundle can never contain a system.* code: those apply only at the unit (05 §2) and every row of the bundle is item-scope by definition. It can never contain event.create: D-151 excludes it, which is the one thing that exclusion was for, though it was written as tidiness and reads now as a load-bearing refusal. And the widest thing the path can write is event.* on one item that did not exist a statement earlier, aimed at a set that is empty until somebody is made an organizer of it. The report over this path is the one D-151 already named — 08a §10 check 2, the incomplete-bundle report — so the exemption is from the ceiling, not from being looked at.

    What is not exempt, which is where the boundary actually does work. The moment a person edits an organizer — event.organizer.manage, one of the twelve — the write names somebody that person chose, and the ceiling reads them. It binds nobody in practice, and that is the answer to the obvious worry: an organizer holds all twelve at their own event, so anything they hand out on it is inside their own ceiling, while a grant aimed at the unit is stopped two checks earlier by applies_to. The exemption covers exactly one transaction, triggered by the create, whose rows are derived rather than chosen. Any other write of an access_grant row reads its writer, including a write that rebuilds a missing bundle by hand: that is a person choosing rows, and check 2's finding is answered through the ordinary grant screen, not by re-running the create.

    The alternative, refused because it is the wrong kind of clever. Attribute the twelve to the system actor of D-125 so the ceiling has no granter to read. That works mechanically and should not be done. It is false — a person pressed the button, and access_grant.granted_by naming a person is D-125's rule for precisely these rows, which D-163 then made the precondition of the check — and it establishes the habit of solving a security rule by editing what the audit says, which is the one move that hollows out every rule in this file at once. If the bundle ever needs an author other than a person, the exemption above is where that argument belongs, not in an empty column.

    One cost, said plainly. There are now two things in this design that a ceiling does not reach, and the second one arrived in this round: day one's single account, exempt because it holds everything, and the create's bundle, exempt because nobody conferred anything. They are different, and the pages say which is which; the phrase "by construction" is not allowed to cover both, because a reader who thinks there is one exemption will look for the wrong one.

    What this round leaves open, with its shape named¶

    The point of a round that starts from "the register said five and the corpus said twenty" is to end with a register that tells the truth again, so this section is the count and the shapes, not a summary.

    07-finance.md §5, counted rather than recited. It opened with eleven items, two closed and one half closed. This round closes item 1 (D-160), item 3 (D-158), item 9 (D-156) and item 10 (D-157), narrows item 8 to its jurisdiction half (D-155), and adds and answers item 12 (D-161). Five remain: discounts, invoices, the accounting export, jurisdiction, and the variable-symbol question — the last of which is still three questions in a trenchcoat, because it decides whether the module has one money path or two. The page renumbers those five from one and keeps each closed item as a single line, so an item number anywhere in this round means the old numbering; the heading still reads "the whole of this document, essentially" because that title is quoted here and every inbound anchor resolves through it.

    All five survivors are now the only place they are written, and none of them is registered in open-questions.md — which is now stated on the money page itself, at the foot of §5, rather than left for the register's finance row to imply otherwise. That is the shape of the bug this round was summoned by, and answering sixteen questions did not fix it: a register that counts a page cannot see a module page's open issues. What this round did instead is make the invisibility sayable — the page names what is registered there (the closed items, with their decisions) and what is not (the five), so the next round that touches money inherits a sentence to correct rather than a silence to rediscover.

    Not one of the register's five survivors was answered, and nine new questions were filed there. A-4's remainder, E-3, F-9, F-11 and F-34 are questions about the core design, and sixteen answers touched the core design exactly twice — at the ceiling on a grant (D-163) and at the account of a child (D-166) — neither of which was on the list. The second and third are this system's own clocks; the first and fourth are the two a data subject asks about.
