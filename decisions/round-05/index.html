    Decision Log — Refinement round 5 — the events module (D-058 … D-071)¶

    Events designed end to end, plus the withdrawal of per-resource admin permissions and of anything that forced an assignment to end.

    Refinement round 4 (D-047 … D-057) ← | All decisions | Refinement round 6 — the no-SQL round (D-072 … D-081) →

    D-058 — An event is a dated occasion owned by exactly one unit¶

    Status: decided (from your answer) Date: refinement round 5

    Ownership reversed by D-102; the dated-occasion half stands. An event is still an occasion with dates rather than a recurring template, and still has exactly one set of dates. There is simply no unit for it to belong to, and the people connected to it are named by selectors that bind to it.

    Decision. event.org_unit_id is NOT NULL — exactly one owning unit. A weekly meeting group is not an event; it is unit_participation. Events are the things people register for: camps, trips, hikes, jamborees.

    Why. Two answers, and the second is the structural one.

    • On ownership: "One owning unit." A joint camp between two packs is Pack A's event that Pack B's children register for. Pack B is a guest, and its access is written explicitly — an audience grant, a named person, at the level that fits. Nobody arbitrates "whose event is this?", and one unit remains the author of the record.
    • On recurrence: "No recurrence — meetings are participation." A Thursday group meeting forty times a year is already recorded as "this child takes part in this Thursday group." Forty event rows would say what one participation row says, with forty capacities, forty organizer rosters, and forty chances to be wrong.

    The ownership rule is forced by D-049, not merely preferred by it. If an event could belong to two units, the root's inability to read a sub-unit's events would have an exception shaped exactly like a joint camp, with no principled place to stop. One owner keeps the rule total.

    What this forecloses. There is no event_series, no RFC 5545 rule, no occurrence table, and no event_unit join table. Each would require every downstream table — registrations, capacity, presence, organizers, questions — to carry a second possible home.

    Consequence worth stating. "Was Jan at the meeting on 14 March?" is not answerable from the events module. That is presence tracking for a standing group, which is a different feature. The boundary is deliberate; the confusion between the two is the thing to watch for.

    See: ../domain/07-events.md §1–2, ../data-model/08-events.md §2.

    D-059 — "Unanswered" is the absence of a row; a registration stores two values¶

    Status: decided (from your answer) Date: refinement round 5

    Decision. event_registration.response has two values — 'attending' and 'not_attending'. 'unanswered' is written nowhere. It is the absence of a registration row. "Who has not replied yet?" is a subtraction: (people who could contribute to this event) MINUS (people with a registration).

    Why. Your answer: "Absent row — two stored values."

    The framing that settled it was your own correction: registration is "positive with additional fields, or negative (or none)". "None" is not a third stored value — it is the lack of a record, and storing it would mean writing rows for people who have not acted.

    Three reasons absence beats a stored value, and the third is decisive:

    Absent row Stored 'unanswered'
    Rows written before anyone acts None One per person asked
    "Asked and silent" vs "never asked" Distinguishable — the eligible set is a query Indistinguishable without an invite table
    "Who has not replied?" A subtraction against an audience Requires enumerating the audience into rows

    The third is the real argument. The eligible set is an audience — a rule the resolver evaluates (D-042) such as unit:7:participants or all_members. Enumerating it into 'unanswered' rows would mean re-enumerating every time a child joins the pack, and the stored rows would drift out of agreement with the rule that generated them.

    Returning someone to unanswered removes the row (DELETE, recorded in audit_log). That is the honest act — it leaves the least behind, and "no row" then means the same thing whether the person never answered or their answer was withdrawn.

    Consequence. unit_participation uses the same reasoning for the same reason, and this decision makes the pattern explicit: where a state is the absence of a fact, store the absence. Compare D-002 for the temporal case and D-053 for the definitional one.

    See: ../data-model/08-events.md §4, ../domain/07-events.md §3.

    D-060 — Eligibility is capability; there is no separate rule engine¶

    Status: decided (from your answer) Date: refinement round 5 Resolves: the deferral in D-006

    Decision. Registration requires contribute capability on that event. Nothing else. There is no eligibility rule table, no age gate, no prerequisite system. event.register_self is already mapped to contribute in permission_level.

    Amended by D-107 in round 12, in the direction of this entry's purpose rather than of its letter. The words "no age gate" were doing two jobs in that paragraph. As "the system applies no eligibility test of its own; there is no rule table; nothing is evaluated outside can()" — they stand, and that is the decision this entry was recording. As "nobody may write an age test in this model" — they are now false, and were only ever true because of a sentence in domain/06 §6 that round 12 deleted: "an attribute is not a set of people … age is not." OLDER(n) and YOUNGER(n) are seeded operands now, so event.register_self may be granted to MEMBERS * YOUNGER(15), and the age ceiling this entry explicitly declined to enforce — the one EV-1 went on to record as unenforced — is statable. What has not moved is who decides. A gate is a grant somebody wrote, shown by the preview, attributed in the audit trail; it is not a conclusion the system draws from a birth date. This entry's real subject was always that, and it is why the entry is amended rather than superseded.

    Why. Your answer: "I feel like the people with 'contribute' permissions (capabilities) on that item should be allowed to register."

    This dissolves D-006's deferral rather than satisfying it. D-006 said "eligibility ... will be a separate concept when events are specified." Your answer says the separate concept was never needed: the capability ladder already is one.

    view        may SEE the event
contribute  may REGISTER for it
manage      may ORGANIZE it
admin       may control it

    Why audiences make this sufficient rather than merely simple. Registering 200 children individually is 200 grant rows — but opening an event to members is one, which the shape of the row made visible at the time:

    INSERT INTO access_grant (scope_type, scope_id, audience_id, level)
VALUES ('event', :camp_id,
        (SELECT id FROM audience WHERE code = 'all_members'), 'contribute');

    The requirement now, without the statement: an access_grant may address an audience instead of a person, so "everyone who is a current member may take part in this event" is one row rather than one per member — 05-authorization §8.

    An age-gated camp is a camp opened to unit:<id>:participants for a unit whose participants are that age. This is the case audiences (D-042) were built for, and events are where the investment pays off.

    What this does not do, stated plainly. (Round 12: this paragraph describes a choice now, not a limitation — YOUNGER(n) is seeded, so an organizer who wants a ceiling grants event.register_self to MEMBERS * YOUNGER(15) and sees whom it reaches before saving. What still does not exist is a test the system runs by itself. D-107.) It does not enforce an age ceiling on an open event. If a nine-year-old holds contribute on a camp for teenagers, they can register. The design decides eligibility by who may register, not by inspecting the person — the check is the grant. Whether that is sufficient is carried in ../open-questions.md rather than hidden.

    Organizers are not bound by it. Anyone holding event.manage_participants may register anyone, and the registration records source = 'staff'. That is the permission's purpose: the leader who knows there is a spare bed is trusted, and the act is attributed.

    See: ../domain/07-events.md §4, data-model/05 §7.

    D-061 — Event answers are read by that event's organizers¶

    Status: decided (from your answer) Date: refinement round 5

    Decision. Answers to event_question are readable by the organizers of that event, with no additional permission. You confirmed the consequence explicitly: the event form is the route by which a camp leader learns a child has a nut allergy.

    Why, and the part that must be recorded. This is only coherent — and only defensible — because the dependent.* namespace is unreachable by unit leaders. The permission catalog states it outright:

    "Resolved only against guardianship_permission, never against roles. A unit leader does not get these by virtue of leading the unit — that is a safeguarding property."

    So a camp leader cannot reach dependent.medical.* at all. Not by leading the pack, not by running the camp, not by being an organizer. There is no permission that changes it. Which means an allergy recorded on a camp form is the only way the people running the camp will know.

    This is a widening, and it is recorded as one. Access to a child's health information moves from "few people, via a guardianship-scoped permission granted by the guardian" to "every organizer of any event the child registers for." That is a coherent position — the parent tells the people present, per event, and the medical file stays closed — but it is not an accident of where the gate was placed, and it must not be "fixed" later by tightening the wrong thing.

    Two consequences that follow.

    1. Only preference-level detail belongs on a form. Nothing in the schema distinguishes "vegetarian" from "coeliac" — both are data_type = 'text' in event_answer.value. The discipline is in the form label, so it is guidance, not a constraint. A CHECK cannot tell them apart, and a keyword list would fail on the first unusual phrasing.
    2. Event answers are not a medical record. They are operational detail for one event, read by that event's organizers, and ending with the event. The two stores are not duplicates of each other and should not be merged or synchronised.

    Open: the retention policy. A dietary answer citing a medical condition is health data with a retention obligation, and there is no policy yet.

    See: ../data-model/08-events.md §7, ../domain/07a-capacity-presence-and-forms.md §12, appendix/permission-catalog.md §9.

    D-062 — Organizers are appointed on the event, not granted¶

    Status: decided (from your answer) Date: refinement round 5

    Decision. Events have an event_organizer roster, and it is the domain answer to "who is running this?" — labelled, ordered, attributed. It is not an access_grant, and it does not by itself confer authority to act on the event.

    Why. Your answer: "Roster only; grants stay separate."

    The tempting alternative, and why it fails. Write an access_grant at manage whenever an organizer is added, so "the organizers" is a query over grants and the two can never disagree. It fails on three counts:

    Grant at manage event_organizer
    A cook's label No room for organizer_role = 'cook' organizer_role, free text
    Display order Not expressible sort_order, is_primary
    "Who is running this camp?" Read the permission table Read the roster
    Removing the cook Revokes their ability to see the event Removes them from the list

    That last row is the practical one: "they are no longer cooking" does not mean "they may no longer see the camp."

    event_organizer is the event-scoped analogue of position_assignment — an appointment on the thing being run. Like a position it may carry a label and an order; like a position it is not a permission bundle. organizer_role is free text, not a CHECK: every organization labels these differently, and a controlled vocabulary here would be a guess of the kind D-054 removed for unit types.

    It has no valid_to, deliberately. A roster is not a period: you are on the list or you are not. An organizer who drops out is removed (audited), because "was Jan an organizer of that camp?" must have one answer, not a set of intervals.

    How authority actually reaches an organizer is D-063 — the unit's roles reach its events.

    See: ../data-model/08-events.md §3, ../domain/07-events.md §5.

    D-063 — An event is an ARTIFACT of its unit, not a child of it¶

    Status: decided (from your answer, as a consequence you did not have to state) Date: refinement round 5

    Superseded by D-102. The argument's shape survives — an item's people are named, never inherited from a container — but with no unit and no owner the question 'whose event is this?' has no column left to answer it from.

    Amends: nothing. Extends: D-031

    The problem. The resolver matches scope exactly. There is therefore no path from a unit_leader role held at ('org_unit', 7) to a check made at ('event', 42) — even when unit 7 owns event 42:

    unit_leader ── holds role at ──► org_unit 7 (the pack)
                                     │
                                     │  ✗ NO PATH
                                     ▼
event 42 "Summer camp" ◄─ owned by ──┘    resolver checks scope exactly

    The pack's leader — the person whose job is running the pack's camp — does not pass can(leader, 'event.view', 'event', 42). Nothing is broken; the answer is simply no. But the module cannot ship with that gap, because it would mean the leader must be granted every event their own unit creates.

    Decision. A permission is satisfied at ('event', E) if it is satisfied at the event's owning unit, ('org_unit', E.org_unit_id), and that unit is the event's owner. One wrapper function, can_event(), calling can() twice.

    Why this is not inheritance downward, and why that distinction is the whole point.

    Inheritance (rejected, D-015/D-031) Artifact rule (chosen)
    What a leader gains Authority in the unit's sub-units too Authority over the unit's own events
    Blast radius Grows on every restructure Fixed — one owner, pinned by D-049
    Reverses D-015, D-031 Nothing

    An event is not a child of its unit; it is a thing the unit made. The unit's authority already covers the unit. Asking "may this person do this in Pack 7?" is asking the right question about the right scope — and it is a question the resolver already answers correctly.

    Restructuring the hierarchy still changes nothing about who may do what. Adding a sub-unit still grants nobody anything. D-015 and D-031 stand untouched, and the rule is exactly as narrow as the case requires.

    Why a wrapper function rather than a change inside can(). Putting this clause inside the resolver would make every permission check — on people, memberships, units, fees — evaluate an event-shaped rule. Keeping it in can_event() means the exception is visible, named, and confined to the module that needs it.

    And it is a two-way door. can_event() calls can() twice and adds no authority of its own; nothing is written to access_grant to make it work. If the organization decides that leaders should not automatically run every event their unit creates, deleting the second can() call is the entire change, and it needs no data migration.

    The root is unaffected. An organization-scoped registrar still cannot see a pack's camp — events are precisely the sub-unit records D-049 protects. The fix, if the root wants it, is one scope_exposure row naming one assignment.

    See: ../domain/07-events.md §6, ../data-model/08a-event-guards-and-checks.md §8.

    D-064 — There is no event status; visibility is the permission model¶

    Status: decided (from your answer) Date: refinement round 5

    Decision. event has no status column. Draft is not a state: a new event has no grants beyond its organizers' own authority, so nobody else can see it, and publishing is an access_grant, not an UPDATE. completed is derived (ends_at < now()). Cancellation is a recorded fact — cancelled_at, cancelled_by, cancel_reason.

    Why. Your answer, which is the right instinct: "Isn't the draft solved by not giving anyone else permissions, but the organizer?" It is, and it is better than a status enum for a reason worth stating:

    A status column and a permission system that both claim to answer "who may see this?" will eventually disagree — and the one that gets enforced is whoever wrote the last IF.

    With no status column there is nothing to disagree. The questions a draft flag would answer are all answered by grants:

    Question Answer
    "Is this ready to be seen?" Has anyone been granted view?
    "Who can see it before it is announced?" Its organizers, and anyone already granted
    "How do I hide it again?" Revoke the grant — revoked_at set, the row kept

    Where the logic deliberately diverges: cancellation. If cancelling were done by revoking grants, the event would vanish for exactly the people who need to know it is off — the registrants. They would see nothing at all: not "cancelled", just gone. So cancellation is a fact, the event stays visible, and it is marked.

    Draft and cancellation therefore use different mechanisms on purpose, because they have different audiences: draft is about who may discover an event, cancellation is about who must be told.

    A cancelled event stops accepting registrations (the window closes implicitly) and keeps every registration row — those rows are the record that the camp was planned and who was coming, which is precisely what is needed to notify and refund.

    See: ../domain/07a-capacity-presence-and-forms.md §10, ../data-model/08-events.md §2.

    D-065 — Capacity is a waitlist threshold, and organizers may exceed it¶

    Status: decided (from your answer) Date: refinement round 5

    Decision. Registering for a full event succeeds and produces a waitlisted row — it is never refused. Organizers may register past capacity outright, and the overrun is permitted, attributed and reported rather than prevented. Promotion from the waitlist is a deliberate act by an organizer, never automatic.

    Why. Two answers, and they only look contradictory:

    • "Waitlist" — the thirteenth caller for a twelve-bed camp is not turned away, they are written down.
    • "Organizers might exceed, also they might choose to include people from the waiting list" — a leader who knows there are two spare tents should not be blocked by a number they cannot edit mid-camp.

    The two coexist because the waitlist is a SELF-SERVICE outcome. The trigger applies it only when source = 'self'. A staff registration is admitted regardless. So:

    Path At a full event
    Self-service Waitlisted, ordered
    Staff / organizer Admitted, the overrun reported
    Promotion Deliberate — an organizer picks who comes off the waitlist
    Capacity change An UPDATE — visible and attributed

    Capacity is therefore three things at once, on purpose: a rule for self-service, a number organizers can exceed, and a report. data-model/08 §10 check 5 produces rows and that is not a failure — an event that overruns every year has a wrong capacity number, and the fix is the number, not refusing people.

    Promotion is not automatic because a place freeing up is not the same as it being right to give it to the next person — the organizer may know the next child cannot come on those dates. What is automatic is one narrow release rule, recorded as such in D-066.

    See: ../data-model/08-events.md §5, ../domain/07a-capacity-presence-and-forms.md §8.

    D-066 — Payment confirms the place, and the release deadline is the one automatic transition¶

    Status: decided (from your answer) Date: refinement round 5

    Decision. An event may have a fee (event.fee_schedule_id), and payment confirms the place. Registering creates a fee_obligation; a paid obligation sets is_confirmed = true via confirm_event_place(). Events without a fee confirm immediately, and most events have no fee — you were explicit that payment "does not have to be at all events."

    Why. Your answer: "Payment confirms the place, also the payment does not have to be at all events."

    The second half is the part that shapes the schema: fee_schedule_id is nullable, and NULL is the common case. A free hike confirms on registration, and there is no "confirmation" state with no meaning.

    The accepted cost, recorded rather than hidden. This design has spent real effort avoiding automatic state changes (D-023, D-040), and payment-confirms-the-place reintroduces one: an unpaid registration at a full event releases its place to the waitlist at waitlist_deadline. It is accepted because it is a deadline, not a sweep — narrowly scoped, opt-in per event, and visible in what it produces. The predicate as decided:

    -- Only at a full event, only with a fee configured, only past the deadline,
-- only unpaid, only self-service, never a cancelled event.
e.fee_schedule_id IS NOT NULL
AND e.waitlist_deadline IS NOT NULL
AND e.waitlist_deadline < now()
AND r.source = 'self' AND r.is_confirmed = false
AND e.cancelled_at IS NULL

    The requirement now, in words: release happens only at a full event that has a fee, a deadline that has passed, an unpaid self-service registration, and is not cancelled — six conditions, each of which is load-bearing, and the list is the rule rather than an illustration of it. Stated in 08-events §5.

    waitlist_deadline is NULL by default, so an event that does not opt in never runs this rule at all. The alternative — no deadline — means a place is held forever by someone who never paid, which is worse.

    A function, not a trigger. confirm_event_place() is called by the finance module and is not a trigger on fee_obligation.status, because a trigger would fire for membership fees and event fees alike and "the fee is paid" means something different in each case: a membership activates (D-040), an event place confirms. Two transitions, two owners.

    See: ../data-model/08-events.md §6, ../domain/07a-capacity-presence-and-forms.md §11.

    D-067 — Presence is a three-valued fact on the registration¶

    Status: decided (from your answer) Date: refinement round 5

    Decision. event_registration.was_present is a nullable boolean, marked by an organizer with presence_marked_by / presence_marked_at.

    Value Meaning
    NULL Not yet marked — no register was taken
    true Turned up
    false Marked absent — a register was taken and they were not there

    Why three-valued rather than a boolean defaulting to false. "We did not take a register" and "we took a register and they were not there" are different facts, and only the second is a safeguarding signal. A boolean defaulting to false would assert absence where the organization only failed to look.

    Why it matters more than it looks. For a camp, the end-of-week presence record is the record of who was actually in the organization's care. That is the row that matters if the fee buys insurance (finance question 9 — "member on the date of the incident"), and it is the row that cannot be reconstructed afterwards from registrations.

    Presence is not registration, and the distinction is enforced: a not_attending registration with was_present set is contradictory and reported (data-model/08 §10 check 8).

    A walk-in is not representable, deliberately. A sibling who turns up on the day was not in the organization's care — no guardian recorded, no name on the roster — and must not silently appear to have been. Recording them means registering them, which means the organizers have a name and a guardian. That is the correct friction.

    Note the contrast with D-059. There, absence of a row was the right encoding for unanswered. Here, a stored NULL is the right encoding for not marked. The difference: "unanswered" is the absence of a fact about the person, while "not marked" is a fact about the organization's incomplete work — and the work being incomplete is itself worth recording, because it is a task still outstanding.

    See: ../data-model/08-events.md §4, ../domain/07a-capacity-presence-and-forms.md §9.

    D-068 — There are no per-resource admin permissions; roles are the bundle¶

    Status: decided (from your answer) — reverses D-007 Date: refinement round 5

    Your answer. Asked whether person.admin should subsume person.merge and person.redact — the standing case against prefix subsumption, since both are irreversible and both are privacy incidents if granted wrongly — you answered: "Only the system should have admin permissions, not the individual items."

    The phrase needed disambiguating, because "individual items" names two different things in this schema: the per-resource permission (person.admin, over the whole resource) and the item-scoped grant (access_grant over one event). You confirmed the first reading.

    Decision. The sixteen <resource>.admin permission codes are deleted — the ten documented ones (person.admin, account.admin, membership.admin, unit_participation.admin, org_unit.admin, position.admin, guardianship.admin, audit.admin, event.admin, audience.admin) and the six sitting in deferred namespaces (document, fee, payment, report, consent, message). So is the resolver's prefix-match branch. What remains:

    • system.admin is the only permission in the system that means everything, and it is reached only through a role assignment (system namespace — never an item grant, never guardianship). Unchanged.
    • "Someone who can do everything about events" is a role the organization composes, bundling the thirteen event.* codes. Not a permission the code invents for them.
    • access_level.admin (rank 40) is untouched. This is a different thing that shares a name, and the collision is the most likely place for a later reader to go wrong. The level answers "how much authority does this grant carry" — view < contribute < manage < admin, used by permission_level to decide whether a contribute-level grant satisfies event.register_self. The permission code answered "does this capability imply that one". The ladder survives; only the wildcard code dies.

    Why this is coherent with the rest of the design rather than an exception to it. D-052 already settled that roles are runtime data and the seed set is not a specification — the organization composes permission groups at runtime. A per-resource admin code is the code composing a permission group on the organization's behalf, in advance, for a bundle nobody asked for. Deleting it makes the two mechanisms agree: the code implements capabilities, the organization groups them. It is the same division D-055 drew when it refused to prune the catalog — a large menu of narrow things, composed by whoever needs them.

    The cost is real, and it is the cost D-007 existed to prevent. Stated in D-007's own words: without the wildcard, every new narrow permission requires updating every role that should already have implied it — and someone will miss one, producing a permission that silently is not granted. That failure mode is back. It is accepted because it fails closed: someone cannot do a thing they were arguably meant to be able to do, and they say so, rather than doing a thing nobody meant them to be able to do. D-007 traded a loud inconvenience for a quiet hazard. The reversal takes the opposite side of the same trade.

    What replaces the protection — since the wildcard is gone, reachability needs a check of its own. Integrity check 18 lists permissions that no role grants, which is the query that surfaces a permission silently orphaned by the absence of a wildcard (numbering follows the existing list in ../data-model/05 §11. It was written as an executable query, and this is it as decided:

    -- 18. ORPHANED PERMISSIONS — granted by no role at all. With no .admin wildcard
--     (D-068), a new permission nobody bundles is silently unusable. This report is
--     the safety net the wildcard used to be.
SELECT p.code, p.resource
  FROM permission p
 WHERE p.is_assignable
   AND NOT EXISTS (SELECT 1 FROM role_permission rp WHERE rp.permission_id = p.id)
 ORDER BY p.resource, p.code;

    The requirement now: every assignable permission that no role bundles is reported, which is the net the .admin wildcard used to be; the check is item 18 of 05-authorization §11, and like the other eighteen it is now something to be verified rather than something that runs.

    What happens to the F-4 question that prompted this. It does not dissolve — it moves, and becomes visible. A role bundling all eleven person.* permissions still reaches person.merge and person.redact, because those are ordinary codes in the bundle. The difference is where the decision lives: previously a resolver rule made the implication, invisible in every role editor and true for every resource whether or not anyone wanted it. Now the implication appears as the contents of a role the organization wrote, reviewable line by line in role_permission, dated, and alarmable. role.edit on that role is already audited for exactly this reason (D-052). The guidance therefore moves from the schema to the seed: a role bundling a resource including its irreversible codes should be named for what it is, and the seed roles in §11 are written so that no seeded role mixes person.merge/person.redact with routine person.edit. That is now expressible as a report rather than a caveat.

    Two survivors, and why neither is an exception. Both look like wildcards and neither is the thing being deleted:

    • dependent.admin stays. It is not a prefix-match artefact — it is a code a staff member chooses and stores, one row in guardianship_permission, granting the full set for one child. A wildcard that must be deliberately written is what this decision endorses, not what it removes. It also has a property no role wildcard can have: an explicit is_granted = false row overrides it, so a court-ordered restriction survives a broad grant. Deleting dependent.admin would make the common restriction easier to express, not harder — listing nineteen narrow rows per child where a court case needs two. The distinction to keep: person.admin implied capabilities nobody wrote down; dependent.admin is a shorthand for capabilities somebody did write down, in a specific row, about a specific child.
    • system.admin stays, for the reasons already in §7 — it is the break-glass capability, concentrating the most dangerous thing in one alarmable holder rather than scattering equivalents. It was never a prefix-match beneficiary; it is checked outright.

    A third thing called "admin", which also stays. dependent.profile.admin, dependent.medical.admin, dependent.consent.admin are not wildcards at all — they are actions meaning "edit" inside a sub-resource, paired against .view. Nothing is implied by them. Three unrelated meanings now share one word in this schema — the access level (rank 40), the deleted resource wildcard, and an edit action — and any of the three may be what a reader means. When one of them comes up in conversation, say which.

    Migration. Deleting role_permission rows for the sixteen codes; no other data moves. Any role that held only person.admin becomes an empty role, which is correct and visible rather than quietly over-privileged — and role.delete still refuses to remove a role that was ever assigned (D-052), so the empty shell survives as evidence of what someone once meant. Re-deriving it means naming the bundle, which is the point.

    Two-way door? No. Re-adding a wildcard later is mechanically cheap and semantically loud — it re-creates the implication silently for resources added after the day you added it. Treat it as a fresh decision, not a revert.

    See: ../appendix/permission-catalog.md §1, ../data-model/05-authorization.md §2, ../domain/06-authorization-model.md §4.

    D-069 — Guardianship ends when a person ends it; the system takes no position on majority¶

    Status: decided (from your answer) — reverses D-024 Date: refinement round 5

    Your answer. Asked what the system does when a guarded child turns 18 — review queue, auto-expire, or nothing — you answered "Nothing at all."

    Decision. No job, no review queue, no computed candidate date, no flag. A guardianship row is ended by a person ending it (guardianship.end), or by import, and the system does not notice birthdays in this connection at all.

    Why this is better than the queue D-024 proposed, and where D-024 went wrong. D-024 reasoned from "fail open rather than silently strip access" and picked the gentler of two automatic behaviours. Both were automatic. The queue was not a refusal to act; it was a deferral of the action, and it smuggled in a claim the system cannot support: that majority is a date the system knows. It is not. D-024's own rationale said thresholds vary by jurisdiction — and then computed a date anyway. A review queue is not a neutral container for a wrong date; it is a worklist of cases staff are told to decide, and the item on it asserts something the organization has not decided.

    The deeper problem with the queue: it inverts who decides. Under D-024 the system raises the question at 18 whether or not anyone wanted it asked, in every case, for every family — including the ones where the adult child asks their parent to keep handling their medical correspondence. The queue's default is that the arrangement is suspect. That is the wrong default for a relationship the organization did not create.

    What "nothing" costs, stated honestly. Nothing is tracked, so "who is still listed as my guardian, and since when?" has no automatic answer. It has a manual one: guardianship carries valid_from/valid_to, so the question is a query on a person's rows and the answer includes the date each was ended. What is lost is a prompt, not a record — which is the same trade the module makes everywhere. See the pattern below.

    This is the latest in a consistent line of refusals, and the reasoning is identical each time. Across five rounds the design has allowed itself exactly two automatic transitions — the yearly fee sweep (D-040) and the event waitlist release (D-066) — and has refused, among others, automatic membership lapse states (D-023), event status (D-064), and recurrence (D-058). Guardianship expiry is the next one refused. The pattern: an automatic transition is cheap to design and expensive to defend, because it acts on every case including the ones it was not designed for. A nightly job that ends guardianships at 18 fails closed and strips a legally valid arrangement overnight; a queue fails open and adds a standing assertion the org never made. The third option — a person who knows the family ends the row — is the only one that can also decide not to.

    Consequence to carry: nothing here weakens the exit path, so the exit path must exist. With no automatic end, guardianship.end is the only door, and it must be reachable by someone. Two things follow, both worth stating because they are now load-bearing in a way they were not when a job existed to help:

    1. The adult child must be able to end it about themselves. They are the subject of the row and currently hold no permission over it — they are not a guardian of themselves. This needs a route, and it is now the module's sharpest open item.
    2. A stale row is invisible by design, so the query, not the job, is the control. A periodic human review is a good idea and is explicitly not a scheduled system behaviour.

    Follow-up raised: E-2 is closed and replaced by E-5 — "how does an adult end a guardianship over themselves?" That question existed before this decision and was masked by a review queue that would have surfaced it. It is now unavoidable.

    See: ../data-model/04-relationships.md §2, ../domain/02-actors.md §5.

    D-070 — An event has no age ceiling; the grant is the rule¶

    Status: decided (from your answer) Date: refinement round 5

    Your answer. Asked about the one gap in eligibility is capability (D-060) — an event opened to all_members can be registered for by a nine-year-old even if the camp was planned for teenagers — you answered "Leave it — the grant is the rule."

    Decision. No min_age/max_age on event. No age check in place_event_registration(). Age restriction is expressed entirely by choosing the audience the event is opened to.

    Why the option was tempting, and why refusing it is right. The offered design was the cheap one: a column pair plus one branch, reported rather than blocking, matching position.min_age. It would have cost little and reversed nothing. It is refused on consistency grounds, which are the only grounds worth having here. A condition evaluated at registration time that the capability model does not know about is the first rule-engine rule. Not because age is special — because the moment one exists, the second is cheap, and within two years the honest answer to "who can register for this?" is "check the grants, and also the age columns, and also whatever else we added there." D-060 dissolved a deferred question by noticing the capability model already answered it; a condition living beside it reopens the question by insisting the capability model is not enough.

    The residual risk, stated rather than waved away. A leader registers a child for an event the child is too young for, and nothing in the system objects. Accepted, on three conditions that are all already true in the schema:

    1. It is attributable. Staff-driven registrations carry source = 'staff' and a created_by, so the judgement is recorded as belonging to a named person.
    2. It is visible. §9's roster query lists registrants with birth dates, so age is on the page a leader looks at while confirming.
    3. It is the normal case correctly handled already. A genuinely age-bounded event is opened to the unit whose members are that age, and that is how a section-based organization works today. What is not covered is one narrow shape — an event open to everyone that should be restricted — and it is narrow enough that a column pair would be a permanent mechanism serving an exception.

    If it is ever revisited, the trigger is specific: the shape recurring often enough that leaders routinely open the wrong event to the wrong people. Then the right answer is probably still not columns on event but an audience that encodes the age band — all_members is a rule the resolver evaluates, and so could members_aged_13_plus be. That keeps eligibility in one place, which is the property being protected here. Do not reach for a condition on the row when the mechanism for evaluating membership of a set already exists.

    See: ../domain/07-events.md §4, ../data-model/08a-event-guards-and-checks.md §9.

    D-071 — Nothing forces a role assignment to end; the gate and the report are the control¶

    Status: decided (from your answer) — overrides the recommendation in F-3 Date: refinement round 5

    Your answer. Asked whether valid_to should be mandatory on high-authority role assignments — the classic volunteer who was never removed — you answered "Nothing — rely on the gate."

    The docs had recommended the other way. F-3 said yes, at the application layer, and argued the membership gate made an open-ended assignment more dangerous, not less. You disagree, and the disagreement is worth recording because the recommendation was not obviously wrong.

    Decision. role_assignment.valid_to stays nullable with no application-layer requirement and no per-role policy. The controls are the membership gate (D-039) and a report. No new constraint anywhere.

    Why the recommendation was wrong, in one place. F-3 said the gate makes open-ended assignments more dangerous. It is the other way round: the gate already does what an expiry date would do, for exactly the population an expiry date would target, and it does it without inventing a date nobody chose. A forced valid_to is an guess about when authority should lapse, written by whoever made the assignment — usually inherited from a form default. The gate instead ties expiry to a fact the organization already maintains and renews on purpose: whether this person is a member. When the fee lapses, gated roles stop granting. The expiry date was always going to be a worse approximation of that.

    What the gate does not cover — the honest residue. unit_leader and assistant_leader have requires_membership = false by explicit decision (a volunteer whose own fee lapses still runs the Thursday meeting), so a lapsed leader keeps leading. Also any role held by a non-member, and anything carrying system.gate_exempt. For those, the answer is a periodic human review over open-ended assignments, and D-068 now makes that query sharper, since a role's full reach is visible in role_permission with no wildcard hiding implications in the resolver.

    Note the dependency this decision creates. D-053 put all the weight on role_assignment.valid_to as the only thing that ends authority, and warned that the open-ended assignment is the classic access failure. This decision declines to add a guard rail at precisely that point, so the two together should be read as one load-bearing pair: authority ends only when somebody ends it, and the only defence left is that somebody looks. The report is not a nice-to-have here; it is the entire control.

    See: ../data-model/05-authorization.md §4, ../open-questions.md F-3.
