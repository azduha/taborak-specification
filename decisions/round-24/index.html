    Decision Log — Refinement round 24 (D-193 … D-205)¶

    This is the last round the register asks for. Open Questions opened it with three questions on the page — A-4's remainder, F-11 and K-10 — and it closes with none. The rest came from the module pages' own ## Open issues lists, which is where the last answerable questions actually lived — a register that is down to three rows is not a design with three questions left, it is a design whose remaining questions have stopped being filed in one place; and D-205 deletes the thing the register said this repository still owed, on the ground that the review it pointed at is not going to happen and a design should stop writing cheques it has decided not to cash.

    Three answers changed shape while they were being given. A question about whether one role may read one record came back as a requirement that permissions depend on each other, which is a rule about the whole vocabulary and is answered as one (D-194). A question about whether the calendar is real came back as two calendars rather than one — the membership year January to December, everything else September to August — which is a finding and not an answer (D-193). And a question about a short payment came back as "attach any payment with matching VS", which does not settle a shortfall so much as delete the condition that made a shortfall a non-match (D-196). One of those three turned around inside its own round: invoices are compulsory, which is a fact about the country this organization is in rather than about this design, and it arrives one answer after the money codes were promoted — so D-197 deletes one of the codes D-195 had just registered.

    Nothing in this round was asked that a previous round had answered — which is the discipline round 23 promised after it spent a question on something D-146 had already settled. The item that came closest is D-199, the accounting export, which is the outbound direction D-146 and D-164 already refused in general and which the finance page had kept asking as its own ever since it was written. It is closed as a consequence of those decisions rather than as a new one, and it mints no rule about actors.

    Refinement round 23 (D-171 … D-192 ←) | All decisions

    Where the questions came from, page by page, so a reader can check that nothing was invented. The register's whole live list: A-4's remainder, F-11, K-10. Then, from the module pages: 07-finance §5 — its accounting direction, its jurisdiction half, and its "a payment that does not fit"; 03 §9 — the organizational-calendar bullet and the outstanding_balance bullet; domain/03 §9 — the twins; domain/04 §8 — a group's previous name; 04 §7 — custody_arrangement; 07a §13 — its one unsettled item, whether the audience editor should warn about a bound operand, which came back as a question about the rule itself and is answered keep the rule, change nothing (D-203); and 06b §6 with assumption 49 — the legal basis, which turns out to be the sharpest question in the round and is closed by D-205 refusing the review it assumes. One decision comes from no page at all: that invoices are compulsory (D-197) arrived as a fact about the country, volunteered while a different question was being answered, and it is the only entry here that reversed a decision rather than closing a question.

    What this round does not do. It designs nothing that was undesigned. Two of its answers are refusals to design (D-202, D-203) and one is a deferral with a date on it rather than a decision (D-199). Its schema effect is two columns and one permission code deleted, and no column added.

    And an empty register is not an empty corpus, which this round says out loud rather than implying. What it closed was what the register carried, plus the module questions that pointed at a register row; the module pages keep their own ## Open issues lists — sixteen pages carry one, a hundred bullets between them — and anything in such a list that this round did not answer stays open and keeps asking. 05d §13 and catalog B §12 hold the longest of those lists, and neither was this round's to answer. The register being at zero therefore means one thing precisely: nobody is waiting on an answer from outside the design. It does not mean the design has no open questions, and a reader who concludes that from a zero has misread what the number was ever for. Where a module list still asks something, the asking page is the register for it — that is the arrangement the register's own header now states.

    D-193 — The membership year is January to December, the program year is September to August, and only one of them is stored¶

    Status: decided (from your answer) — closes A-4's remainder, the last row of § A and the oldest open question in the register, and answers the first bullet of 03 §9 and of domain/05 §9.

    The answer, as given. "The membership is always jan–dec, while rest of the activities (such as person_group) will be from sep–aug, however, those are not hardcoded anywhere anyway."

    The rule. There are two years in this organization and they do not agree. The membership year runs 1 January to 31 December, and it is stored — expiry_month, expiry_day and renewal_grace_days on the single membership_type row, which is the way D-047 and D-048 left them and the reason both numbers can move with an UPDATE. The program year — a group's term, a camp season, the collections — runs September to August, and it is stored nowhere at all: not a column, not a constant, not a rule, not a check. A person_group gets the valid_from and valid_to a person types (D-098), and those two dates happen to fall in September and August the way they happen to fall anywhere else.

    Why the "not hardcoded anyway" half is the answer, and not a caveat. It is the whole answer. The question on the register was whether 1 January is this organization's date or a date a database liked, and the answer is that it is a real date for a real thing — the fee, the membership, the renewal — and that the things which run on the other calendar were never dated by the design in the first place. So there is nothing to change and nothing to add, and what the register was missing was not a number but the fact that its own question assumed one year where the organization has two. D-169 stands untouched for the same reason: it rules the membership half, and the membership half is the calendar year. Had the answer been "the year turns in September", that decision's stored valid_to of 1 January would have had to be restated as an organizational year, and every inferred historical end date with it.

    What this changes. Nothing in a table. Three pages gain the two-calendar fact where a reader would otherwise assume one: A-4's row closes as confirmed rather than as answered, 03 §9 keeps the discipline half of the question — do these values come from the row? — because that half was never about the date at all, and domain/05 §6 says plainly that a renewal date in November and a term sheet in September are the same organization keeping two books.

    What it costs, said plainly. A report that asks "how many members did the 2026 year have" and a report that asks "who was in a group in the 2026 year" answer about overlapping-but-different periods, and no column anywhere says which of the two a reader meant. That is the price of a program year with no representation, and it is the price this design has been paying since D-097 stopped dating bodies at all. The alternative was a calendar table or a second set of columns on a group, which would have made September a fact the model asserts about groups that hold no dates in common — the same invention D-095 refused for money and restrictions.

    The discipline that outlives the question. Both numbers are read from the row or they are wrong: no job, no front end and no report may hold the literal 60 or the literal 1. That sentence was already the surviving half of A-4, and this entry keeps it, because D-186 moved this table's defence onto the calendar the row carries and a compiled-in number is the one way to lose it.

    D-194 — Nothing is implied by a permission: the editor offers the companion read, and the database stores two rows¶

    Status: decided (from your answer) — closes F-11, and rules the thing F-11's answer opened.

    The answer, as given. "The permissions should have dependency, so let's implement that. Some grants (write) do not make sense without others (read). I want to add a permission dependency where necessary (either by auto-inheritance, or by forcing the other ones to be present for the new one to be added)." Asked which of the two, the answer was neither: editor convenience only, and not at save.

    The rule. A permission implies nothing. person.merge does not carry person.view, the resolver never reads a dependency, and a grant answers the one code it names — which is D-099 unchanged, D-068 unchanged, and the deleted prefix match still deleted. What is added is one screen behaviour: where a code is a write and the same resource has a read, the editor that saves the grant offers to save the read grant beside it. Taking the offer writes two rows, each naming one code, each with its own grantee, scope and period, each with its own granted_by and its own audit row. Declining it writes one.

    Where the pairing lives, and why not in a table. Next to the code, in program text, on D-177's side of the line: the rule the corpus adopted is that a list becomes a row when the organization may decide it, and no organization gets to decide whether reading is a different act from writing. The pairing is also almost derivable from what is already registered — permission.right_kind says read or write and the code's first segment names the row — so the offer is <resource>.view whenever that code exists, and nothing at all when it does not. Four cases are named rather than derived, because their read is not the same resource's .view: person.merge and person.unmerge offer person.view; payment.match offers payment.view and fee.view; event.presence.mark offers event.answer.view beside the roster it marks; membership.request.approve offers membership.view. No table, no column, no migration, and no gate reads any of it.

    Why not auto-inheritance, which was the other half of the offer. Because an implied permission confers what no row names, and three rulings say that cannot happen. D-163's granter's ceiling reads a grant and, since D-188, nothing else — a grant that also carried its read implicitly would give away a code the granter may not hold, and the check would not see it happen. D-110's record is the expression, and an implied code is not in it. And the question these documents get asked — what may this person do? — is answered today by listing rows, which is the property D-151 spent twelve rows per event to keep. The catalog already suspected this before it had a rule: membership.terminate carries the sentence "it must not be implied by membership.lapse", and until this entry that prohibition rested on nobody having proposed implication. Now it rests on a ruling.

    Why not a save-time refusal either, which is the option that would have worked. It was offered, with a strictness question attached, and the answer was not at save. It is worth recording what was declined, because it was the version that would have closed F-11 outright: a rule refusing an allow for a write code whose grantee holds no live allow for its read. It would have had a real hole in it — the grantee of the two rows is rarely the same row twice, and deciding whether two expressions reach the same people is the containment question D-105 refused to make computable — but the reason it is not here is that it was not chosen, not that it could not be built.

    What this changes, and what it does not reach. Catalog B §11 writes its worked-example identity reviewer with person.view beside person.merge, and says in one sentence that the author chose it and no rule put it there — because an installation copies the example, and a reviewer who cannot open either record spends the first week asking somebody else to look. That is the practical half of F-11 and it is answered there, not in the model. What the offer does not reach: an api_key holder writing a grant, a migration, and the twelve rows an event create writes. Those write what they write, and a reviewer office built by one of them can hold person.merge and no read. Nothing refuses it, nothing lists it, and no report will ever show it — which is the cost of "editor convenience", and it is on the page rather than in a footnote.

    D-195 — The money codes are shipped codes: catalog A grows a money section, and catalog B §10 loses three rows¶

    Status: decided (from your answer) — closes K-10, and the decision that makes the finance module's money path point at something registered.

    The rule. Money codes are codes. They are documented in catalog A the way every shipped code is documented — a row in the Code | Namespace | Sens. | Grants table, and behind it a permission row carrying the applies_to the grant save checks and the right_kind that is read or write because that column is a guardian's answer and not a hint — and they are deleted from catalog B §10, which is the page that calls them "placeholders for features you have not specified". §10 keeps what is genuinely unbuilt: document, consent, message, partner, and the whole report row. The sentence in its payment row asking whether the row is still honest as a deferred namespace is deleted with the row, because it was not.

    Which codes. Six: fee.view, fee.create, payment.view, payment.record, payment.reverse, payment.match. invoice.issue was promoted by this answer and deleted by the next one (D-197), so the section documents six and not the eight this question was asked with. That ordering is recorded rather than tidied, because it is the clearest evidence in this round that the module was designed ahead of the answers.

    And where the promotion stops: not one report code ships, including the money one. report.finance.view was in the list K-10 named, and it stays in §10, because promoting it would have answered a different question by accident. 05d's rules list, §12 states that reporting ships coarse on purpose — only report.view and report.export exist — that whether reporting is per-report or one permission with row-level filtering is still open, and that an installation must not ship both shapes; pick one when reporting is designed. Catalog B §12 carries the same question as its own open item. K-10 asked whether the finance module's codes are still deferred; it did not ask whether reporting's shape is decided, and it is not. So the money rows are gated by fee.view and payment.view, which is the shape this vocabulary already has — a code per row-set — and the report that reads them across subjects waits for the shape question like every other report does. 07-finance §5 loses report.finance.view from the list of codes it calls its own, and gains the reason: the module can spend six codes and not the seventh, and the seventh is not this module's to decide. The catalogue question is unanswered by this entry and stays where it is filed — which is what makes D-138's one-code-for-the-whole-trail ruling the only report-shaped decision in the corpus, rather than one of two contradictory ones.

    What changes. A grant over money has something to be checked against besides a worked example: applies_to says which scope shape each code may sit on, and the grant save refuses one that does not match. Catalog B §11's treasurer office — the one worked example that spends money, and the only one — stops holding six codes that named no register row, which is what made §10 dishonest rather than merely optimistic. It gained payment.match and is now seven rows, six of them registered, and the page's own sentence saying the office "cannot be written today" retires with them. 07-finance §5 loses the paragraph that said its codes are registered nowhere. And scripts/check-registries.py, which reads the registries out of the pages, now finds money codes in the same register as everything else — a code-shaped money token in prose is news from here on, and an unregistered one is a finding.

    What it costs. Six rows nobody wrote before this round, each with an applies_to somebody has to get right, and a section in an appendix that an installation may never grant any of — the disposition D-055 chose says an unused row costs nothing, and this entry spends it on the record rather than on a placeholder.

    D-196 — The variable symbol is the whole match: what arrives under its own symbol is attached in full, and the difference is shown¶

    Status: decided (from your answer) — answers item 3 of 07-finance §5, and narrows what "money that cannot be matched" means since D-180.

    The answer, as given. "Attach any payment with matching VS, show the difference in the UI (under/overpayment)." Asked what that means for the case the module calls the normal one — one transfer of 1 100 Kč against one child's symbol, on a camp ask of 550, with a second child who also has an ask — the answer was attach it all, show the over.

    The rule. A bank row is attached automatically when its variable symbol resolves to a live request, and it is attached in full. The amount is not a condition of the match and never becomes one; it is information, and the information is shown. system:bank-pull writes the payment, the link, and the allocation that carries the whole amount — the rows are the fetch's own, exactly as D-182 ruled, because resolving a symbol is not a human act. What stops a match is the symbol and nothing else: no symbol, a symbol no request carries, or a symbol whose request has been voided. Those rows are still §2b's, and D-180's no-queue answer is unchanged about them.

    The three consequences, in the order they hurt.

    1. partially_paid becomes reachable by itself. It used to be a value a row could only reach through a hand allocation. Now 500 Kč against a 550 ask attaches, the obligation's amount_paid reads 500, the status arithmetic reads partially_paid, and the balance is 50 Kč (D-159 keeps its three values and due_date stays the only place lateness lives). No tolerance is introduced anywhere: 4,90 against 5,00 is a shortfall like any other, and a threshold would be a number nobody can defend and everyone would have to remember.
    2. An obligation can be paid too much. The 1 100 transfer lands entirely on one child's obligation, which then reads 550 over. status still reads paid — it is arithmetic about how much arrived, and there is no fourth value and no credit concept. The surplus is not a new kind of money: it is an allocation a person moves with payment.match, whose split outcome already exists for exactly this shape.
    3. The membership gate does not move. A fee is settled when no obligation attached to it has a positive balance, so a short payment settles nothing and a long one settles everything. That is the same test D-157 and D-159 left, read through the one definition in §3 — and, from D-200, read through no column at all.

    What this costs, and it is the interesting part. The family case is now mis-attributed by the automatic half, silently and on the module's own normal path: the child whose symbol was used looks paid to the sum of 1 100, the sibling looks unpaid, and nobody is told because D-164 forbids telling. This is the shape D-076 refused an identity fallback over — "a parent with two children pays one amount against one symbol is the normal case, not the edge case, and a fee recorded against the wrong child is a finance error and a membership that appears paid when it is not" — and the refusal stands, because it was about resolving a failed symbol by guessing a payer, and here nothing is guessed: the payer's own bank carried the symbol the organization printed. What changed is which mistake the system makes on its own. It no longer leaves the family's money with no subject, which was the old failure and a slower one; it now puts all of it somewhere visible and wrong, and the thing that catches it is a person reading an obligation that says +550, or the family being asked. 07-finance §2b states this as the trade and does not soften it.

    "Shown" is the word this entry has to be exact about, because the answer said show the difference in the UI and this design cannot promise what that sounds like. Nothing is sent and nobody is told: D-164 forbids the sentence, and the overview is plain that notifications stay genuinely undesigned rather than deferred — so there is nothing in this design that could tell a treasurer about an overpayment, even if a page wanted to. There is also no screen that lists these rows, and none was promised: reporting ships coarse, with report.view and report.export and no per-module report code, and the code that would have gated a money exception report is the one D-195 left deferred on purpose. What "shown" therefore buys, in full: the payment row holds the whole amount, the allocation holds the whole amount, the obligation's computed balance reads +550 through the one definition D-200 leaves, so any screen that reads a balance reads the difference; and the two allocations sit side by side in the list somebody opens when they hold payment.match and are moving money by hand. What closes the gap between visible and seen is a person who opens that list, and nothing else — no flag, no exception queue, no job. This round chose the silent mis-attribution over the queued one; it should be read as knowing what it bought.

    What this changes elsewhere. §2a step 6 becomes the whole rule instead of a conditional with an amount in it; §2b's list of reasons a transaction has no match loses "the amount is less than asked, or more", which was a fifth of that list and the largest of its entries; the unmatched population narrows to money with a missing, unknown or dead symbol, which makes D-180's cost paragraph — a camp transfer that sits unattributed forever with no list anywhere — true of fewer rows and no longer true of a family's part-payment; and §4a's check that counts a payment whose allocations do not account for its amount has to say for the first time that an unmatched payment has no allocations on purpose, or it reports the designed absence as a breach.

    D-197 — An invoice is compulsory, so nobody issues one: invoice.issue is deleted, and the ask's write writes it¶

    Status: decided (from your answer) — amends D-187, and deletes one of the codes D-195 registered one answer earlier.

    The answer, as given. "Invoices are compulsory."

    The rule. Every payment_request has an invoice over it. Always, in every installation, without anybody being asked, because the country this organization is in requires the document. The invoice row is written by the same write that creates its request — one transaction, one actor, issued_at and issued_by read off that write, one audit payload covering both rows. invoice.issue is deleted. There is no act left for it to authorize: no screen offers a choice about producing an invoice, no row exists in a state of awaiting one, and a permission nobody can decline is not a permission.

    This reverses the round that added the table, and says so. D-187 is titled "An invoice is a document a payer asks for", and its rule was that the row exists only because a payer asked — the module's own line is "A request is not an invoice… An invoice is a separate row over the same ask, written only because a payer asked for it." That clause is deleted, with the sentence two lines below it that depended on it: "Most obligations have no invoice at all, and no report is planned over the requests that have none." The set of requests without an invoice is now empty by construction, which retires the alternative that entry left on the page — "if invoices are asked for often enough that forgetting one is a real failure, the fix is a report over the requests that have no invoice, not a status on the request" — because there is nothing left to report. What survives untouched is the part that was not about the asking: the request still does not become an invoice, the two rows keep their own numbers, and the sharpest line in D-187 still reads exactly as it was written — a symbol resolves a payment to an ask, a number identifies a document to a tax authority, and nothing promises the two agree. D-187 even named this answer in advance: "If this country turns out to require invoices of somebody, the answer is this table, not a renaming." It turns out to. The table is the answer. The renaming did not happen.

    One sentence in D-187 changes meaning rather than disappearing. Its list of what a payment_request lacks — "no tax identity, no legally-required sequence, no delivery record" — keeps two of its three clauses and loses the middle one. The invoice's sequence is now legally required: number is unique and never reused, which was hygiene when an invoice was a courtesy and is a legal fact now that one is a document. The request itself still has no tax identity and no delivery record, and that is still true for the same reason D-164 gives — nothing here delivers anything.

    The queue argument gets stronger, and keeps its shape. D-187 refused a requested status because a column meaning somebody owes this person a document, with nobody assigned to it and nothing counting it, is D-180's queue wearing a coat. Compulsory invoicing does not add the column and does not need one: it deletes the debt. The invoice's rule of "no state" stands word for word, now for the stronger reason that there is no moment at which a document could be missing.

    What happens to a wrong invoice. D-183's rule unchanged, extended by one row: the ask is voided and reissued, and the voided request keeps its invoice — "a voided request keeps its invoice. Both rows are permanent" is still true and now fires on every reissue instead of almost never. The reissued ask gets a new invoice with a fresh number from the same sequence, and the old number stays retired, because a number a tax authority has seen cannot be handed to a second document.

    Which write, exactly, since the code is gone. fee.create. The money family this round registers has one code that writes the asking side of the model — an obligation and the request over it — and the invoice joins that same write rather than getting a code of its own, so the module has no money row whose write is ungated.

    What this costs, and it is a real loss rather than a rounding. D-187 bought a separation on purpose and named it: invoice.issue was "deliberately separate from payment.match so that the person who can settle a transfer is not automatically the person who can sign a tax document", and catalog B repeated it — "the person who may type in a number is not automatically the person who may produce a document with a legal shape." That separation is gone. Whoever holds fee.create now produces tax documents as a side effect of recording what somebody owes, and no code, no check and no report distinguishes the two acts any more. The control left is the one D-188 left standing everywhere else in this vocabulary: fee.create is rare because somebody granted it, once, in the open — and if that turns out not to be enough, the repair is a sentence about who may hold fee.create, not the code back. There is also the ordinary cost of automation: the sequence advances for documents nobody handed to anybody, and a request voided five minutes after it was written leaves a voided invoice behind forever in a table that, unlike the audit trail, has no end date at all.

    D-198 — An invoice's issuer is configuration; its payer's identification is already in the database¶

    Status: decided (from your answer) — closes the jurisdiction-and-paperwork half of item 2 of 07-finance §5.

    The answer, as given. "There MAY be an external system added in later versions, however it will not be designed in this documentation (as already stated), please close this now." — asked directly, in the same batch, who the invoice says it is from and what happens after it is issued: configuration, like the login methods, and anything beyond issuing the document is discharged outside the system.

    The rule, in three parts.

    1. The issuer is deployment configuration. The organization's name, address, IČO, DIČ and bank account are not in a table. There is no unit row to hold them (D-097 deleted the unit as a row), there is no organization table, and adding one to print a header would be the same invention D-177 refused for login methods — a value with one legal value, one installation, and no screen that writes it. It arrives the way the SMTP relay and the bank's API credentials arrive: as configuration the deployment supplies, named nowhere in the schema.
    2. The payer's identification is already modelled, and it is rows. An invoice needs the payer's name and address, and for a business payer their IČO and DIČ. domain/02 already has the shape: person_identifier is a (scheme, value) pair, unique among current identifiers, and IČO and DIČ are schemes, not columns. Nothing is added for the invoice; the invoice reads rows that exist for a completely different reason and were designed before this question was asked.
    3. Everything after issuing happens outside. The invoice exists as a row here. Getting it to the payer, filing it, reporting it, and reconciling it with an accountant are acts this system does not perform, does not record having performed, and does not have a column claiming it performed. D-164 already forbids the first of those sentences being written as if the system did it; this entry forbids the schema implying it.

    What this changes. 07-finance closes its jurisdiction item as answered by refusing the question's premise: the module had been asking which jurisdiction's rules it should encode, and the answer is that it encodes none — it produces a row whose number is never reused, and the thing that turns that row into a filing is outside the corpus. The one addition is a sentence wherever an invoice is described, naming configuration as the source of the header, so an implementer does not put the organization's IČO in a settings table that does not exist.

    What it costs. An installation with two legal entities cannot have them both, because the issuer is one set of configuration values and there is no row to choose between. That is a consequence of D-097 that this answer makes concrete for the first time: the design supports one organization, has supported one organization since round 10, and now says so at the one place where being two organizations would have shown up in the data.

    D-199 — Nothing leaves this system to an accounting system, and an external one is a later version's design¶

    Status: decided (from your answer) — closes the accounting-direction item of 07-finance §5, the finance module's own version of F-27, and mints no new rule.

    The answer, as given. "There MAY be an external system added in later versions, however it will not be designed in this documentation (as already stated), please close this now."

    The rule. No export, no feed, no integration, no ledger. This system does not send anything to an accounting package: not the invoices, not the payments, not a periodic journal, not a CSV of the day's bank rows. Everything the money model asserts lives in its seven tables and is read out of them by the reports a person reaches with report.view or by a person with report.export. An external accounting system may appear in a later version of this product; when it does, it will be designed then, in a document that is not this one. This corpus contains no placeholder for it — no export status, no external_reference column, no system:accounting actor label, no deferred namespace, no open question.

    Why this closes rather than defers, when "MAY be added later" is not a no. Because the question on the page was not "will there ever be one" — it was "what does this design owe it now", and the answer is nothing. Leaving the item open would keep the finance module describing a hole that has been answered, which is the failure mode this round exists to close. The corpus has an exact precedent for this shape of closure: D-146 answered an outbound direction by refusing to design either side of it, and D-164 then swept every sentence that implied the system sends things. The actor-label list stays exactly where 00-conventions §7a left it — the five it lists, system:renewal-sweep, system:payload-reduction, system:log-reduction, system:bank-pull and migration:<id>, of which the third is already the one that deletes — and no accounting label joins them, which is the concrete thing this deferral costs: nothing. An integration added later is an integration that will need its own label, and needing one is not a reason to reserve it now.

    One cost is worth naming, because it is a cost of the tooling and not of the design. scripts/check-registries.py reads three registers out of these pages — permission codes, deferred namespaces, and the dead names in removed-concepts.md — and it has no register of things the design has refused to add. A label that was considered and declined is therefore invisible to the gate: the only thing standing between this deferral and a future system:accounting appearing in a page is this paragraph and the two module sentences beside it. It is not baselined, because a baseline is for findings the corpus has read and kept, and there is no finding to keep — the name appears in the corpus solely to say it does not exist, and the pages that name it were reworded to say "no sixth actor label" instead, so the checker stays at zero and cannot be the thing that remembers.

    D-200 — membership.outstanding_balance is deleted, and the balance is the one definition it was copied from¶

    Status: decided (from your answer) — the recommendation 07-finance §3 has been making since round 6 is taken, and it closes that section, 03 §9's balance bullet, and the second half of 03 §9's imported-row note.

    The answer, as given. "Delete the column."

    The rule. The column is gone. There is one definition of what is owed, in 07-finance §3, and it reads fee_obligation and payment_allocation; every screen, every report, every gate and every API response that shows a balance reads that definition. The view it was going to be copied into is not created either — the definition is the view, and a second name for it is a second thing to get wrong.

    Why the recommendation was already the right one and why it needed an answer anyway. §3's own sentence — a wrong balance suspends the wrong child — was written in round 6 and has survived nine rounds with the column in place, because deleting a column is a decision somebody has to take and a document can only recommend it. The column's defence was never accuracy; it was that a membership screen wants a number without joining four tables. That defence is now answered the way it should have been answered in round 6: a request memoizes (D-114), so the join is not the expensive thing it was assumed to be when the column was drawn.

    The NULL that was load-bearing, and where it goes now. Assumption 53 recorded that an imported membership's balance is NULL and not zero, because zero asserts the organization settled a debt it never saw, and D-093 made imported rows believe things. With no column, that distinction does not disappear — it moves into the absence of rows. An imported membership has no fee_obligation underneath it, and the balance definition reading "no obligations" must keep reading it as nothing is known, not as nothing is owed, in exactly the places assumption 53 listed: the gate, the arrears report, and any screen that would otherwise print 0 Kč. The activation gate's behaviour for such a row is unchanged and is now written down rather than inferred: it asks whether any attached obligation has a positive balance, an imported membership has none, so the test passes vacuously — which is correct for the gate (nobody is being suspended for a debt nobody computed) and is also what makes the import check flag an obligation-less imported membership as something to look at. Deleting the column removes a number that could lie; it does not remove the underlying absence, and it must not be allowed to remove the sentence about it.

    What this changes. The field table in 03-membership §3 loses the row, including its honest warning that the column was kept "only until that decision is taken" — this is that decision. §3 loses its three-option table and keeps its definition. Every mention of the column across the corpus, in the ERD appendices, in the glossary and in the membership lifecycle prose, becomes a pointer to the one definition. And the column enters the dead-name register, so a future page that writes outstanding_balance is named by the checker rather than caught by a reader.

    D-201 — A group keeps no previous name: this is now a ruling, and not an absence¶

    Status: decided (from your answer) — closes domain/04 §8, which was that page's last open question.

    The answer, as given. "No, prose is enough."

    The rule. A person_group has one name, it is editable in place, and no previous name is stored anywhere — no previous_name, no name-history table, no name_changed_at. A group renamed because it took a new patron, or inherited an older troop's name, carries the fact in prose the organization writes where it wants it: on the group's own description, in its roster's header, in the annual report. Nothing in the schema knows the group ever had another name.

    Why this is written as a decision when it changes nothing in a table. Because the page filed it as an open question, and an open question is a promise that the answer is not yet known. It is known. And this is the second time this round closes a question by saying the words are enough rather than by adding a column: D-173 ruled that two groups being the same group is said and not stored, and wrote the reason this entry inherits — the choice is recorded "in the page that held the question, rather than left as the silence of a table that has no column for it". A previous name and a continuity claim are the same kind of fact about the same table, and it would be incoherent to store one and decline the other. domain/04 §8 keeps its own sentence that if renaming turns out to be common the answer is a decision, not a column somebody adds quietly — that sentence is now the answer rather than the contingency.

    What it costs, including a sharp edge the question had not mentioned. The audit trail was the fallback and it is shorter than the organization's memory of its own troops: an edit writes the old string into an audit_log payload, and payloads are emptied at 24 months, so a group renamed more than two years ago has no name trail anywhere in this system. Worse, and this is the part nobody had said out loud: a group's name is unique across person_group, role and audience together, so renaming a group frees its old name to be taken by a new group, a role, or an audience — after which a retired name is not even a stale pointer to the group that wore it, it is a name that may mean something else entirely. The prose that carries the memory has to name the group in words a reader resolves for themselves, and the design accepts that.

    D-202 — There is no twin-and-sibling procedure, and the collision pair stays a person's judgement¶

    Status: decided (from your answer) — closes domain/03 §9, the last open item on that page.

    The answer, as given. "No procedure."

    The rule. Nothing in this design documents how to tell two children apart, and nothing automates it. There is no disambiguation checklist, no screen that pairs candidates, no check that flags a suspicious merge, no report of "persons sharing a normalised name and a birth date", and no rule about siblings anywhere. What exists is what already existed: D-089's collision path, where a sign-up that matches an existing person files a claim and creates nothing, and D-176's gate, where an account may not reach active while an unresolved identity_hypothesis names its person on either side of the pair. A person then reads two records and decides, holding person.merge.

    The recommendation this retires, and why it was already true. The page recommended "yes, and never merge them automatically". The second half of that recommendation describes a design nobody proposed: there is no job, no match pass and no import that merges anybody — match_job writes a hypothesis, proposed_by says which of match_job, person or staff raised it (D-192), and every merge is a person's write behind a code. So the only live part of the question was the procedure, and the answer is that the procedure is the reviewer.

    The anti-signal that was put to you, and declined. The question came with a fact attached: a guardianship row already records which adults reach which children, so "these two are reached through the same guardian" is a fact this model stores, and a reviewer could be shown it. Declined. Nothing reads the shared-guardian fact as a hint, no screen surfaces it beside a merge, and the design keeps its position that a guardianship row is about authority over a child and not about kinship — which is the same reason there is no sibling edge. Recording the refusal matters because it is the cheapest possible improvement to the risk this entry accepts, and a future reader should find it already considered rather than not thought of.

    What it costs, stated as the failure rather than as an abstraction. Two children, same surname, same birth date, one form filled in by the same parent on the same evening: the sign-up collides, the gate holds the account, and the thing that stops the wrong merge is the attention of whoever reviews the claim. If they merge anyway, one row carries two children — one fee obligation covering a child who is not the one who came to camp, one attendance mark belonging to the other, one set of attribute answers where a diagnosis for one child is now held about both, and D-140's erasure emptying answers about a person who is two people. The undo exists — person.unmerge is why person.merge was ever safe to hand out — but it is safe only while the field conflicts are still legible, and a merge that resolves cleanly leaves nothing that reads as a mistake. Nothing was added to guard against that, by this answer.

    D-203 — A bound operand stays illegal at the unit, and the reason is the granter's ceiling¶

    Status: decided (from your answer) — closes the last unsettled bullet of 07a §13 as declined: no editor warning, no new label, no change to the rule.

    The question as asked, and the question that came back. The page asked whether the audience editor should warn that an audience naming PLACED cannot be aimed at a unit-scope grant, since the refusal currently lands later, when somebody saves the grant. Your answer was a question about the rule underneath it: "If I put those on the system, doesn't it still work when it resolves against a specific item? If I set up an audience organizers defined as ORGANIZERS, then when granted the permission event.edit, all events' organizers can edit them."

    The answer to that question is yes — and it already happens, without anyone writing the grant you are imagining. An audience named organizers whose whole expression is the bare operand ORGANIZERS is a legal row, event.edit aimed at it is a legal grant, and it works on every event in the system — because D-151 writes it once per event, twelve codes beside every event, each an item-scope grant pointing at that audience. At check time the resolver is handed an item, so a bound operand has something to bind against (05c); the expression never needed the grant to know the item, the grant is what has to be per-item. What stays illegal is the single unit-scope row naming that audience — which is check 3 of the seven the grant save runs, reading selector_type.binds_to_item against grant_scope_type (05 §6, domain/06a).

    Why the rule is kept now that it has been examined, which is the part worth keeping. A grant's scope column is the thing the granter's ceiling reads. D-163, narrowed by D-188 to "reads a grant and nothing else", compares the code and the scope a grant names against what the granter holds. Let a bound operand sit at unit scope and that row would say the unit while meaning whatever item is being asked about — a grant whose reach is decided by a question its own row cannot answer, checked by a ceiling that reads the row. It is the same reason there is no permission cache (D-114): this design has already decided that a permission answer is not a fact about a person, it is a fact about a person, an item and a date, resolved when asked. The scope column is the part of a grant that does not depend on the question being asked, and a bound operand in it would make it depend on the question. Per-item rows are not a workaround for a missing feature; they are how a scope stays a scope.

    What was declined, and what it will cost somebody. The editor warning. Somebody will build an audience called organizers, be pleased with it, try to grant event.edit with it at the unit, and be refused at the save by a message about a bound operand — with the explanation two clicks away in domain/06a and nothing at the audience editor saying this row will only ever be usable on an item. That is a real, recurring, small cost, and the answer accepted it rather than adding a warning that would have to be right about an audience whose expression may name ORGANIZERS in one branch and MEMBERS in another, where the honest verdict is "depends on the grant". The refusal lands on the grant, which is faithful to the rule — the page's own sentence, now its verdict too.

    What this costs the corpus rather than a user. 07a §13's list keeps the bullet, with this answer written into it, because a bullet deleted from an open-issues list reads as "still open, we forgot". The twelve-rows-per-event consequence of the rule is unaffected by this entry either way, which is worth saying plainly, since the question looked like it might dissolve it.

    D-204 — custody_arrangement is deleted: a court order is stored here neither as prose nor as structure¶

    Status: decided (from your answer) — closes 04 §7, and it is the second half of a deletion the corpus started in D-191.

    The answer, as given. The question was whether to structure the free-text field, with the page recommending not — custody orders vary too much to model, and a wrong model is worse than honest prose. The answer was neither option: "Delete it."

    The rule. The column is gone. The arrangement a court ordered is not structured, is not prose, and is not stored anywhere: it is not a flag, not an enum, not a note, and not a second table. What the row keeps is the two people, the relationship, the right_level, lives_with, source, the dates, and the two contact flags (D-185 is the last ruling on its life).

    The row is not prose-free, and pretending otherwise would be a false claim about the table on the page beside this one. Three free-text columns survive on the same row, and the line between them and the deleted one is the whole of this entry: evidence_reference, ended_reason and ended_note say something the organization did or holds — where the proof is filed, why a party ended the row — while custody_arrangement said something about a child's life that no rule reads. A column recording an act is a record; a column describing a private arrangement is a copy of it, and copies are what D-083 and D-191 both refused. If that line ever blurs — if ended_note becomes the place a person types an order because the box is there — this entry is the sentence that was broken, and it is written here so the breaking is visible.

    Why deleting beats the recommendation the page made. The page's argument for prose was that a wrong model is worse than honest prose, and that is true — but the field was already not doing the job its name claims. It is read by nobody, gates nothing, and cannot be, because D-083 deleted the idea of a graded category of field, and authority here is an expression over named sets rather than a flag beside a value. A column that no rule reads and no screen computes from is D-191's argument for deleting person.notes in a smaller size, and it is worse than notes in one respect: a text field named custody_arrangement invites a person to type a court order into a database that cannot act on it, which is the worst place for that sentence — sensitive enough to be a problem if leaked, useless enough to be a problem when needed, and invisible enough that whoever typed it will assume somebody downstream read it.

    What this does to the case the corpus kept naming. Both 04 and domain/01 use the same worked example as the design's honest limit: a court order restricting one parent's access to the health facts about a child while their other access continues — "a realistic custody arrangement, and one the model must" refuse. The refusal stands, and this entry widens it: until now the arrangement could not be expressed but could at least be written down somewhere in the row. Now it can do neither. Where it lives: the organization's paper file, and the memory of the person it was told to. That is stated here rather than left to be discovered, because it is the sharpest cost in this round and it belongs next to the deletion that causes it.

    The audit trail does not soften this. An edit that deletes the column moves its values into payloads once, and payloads are emptied 24 months after they were written, so the trail is a two-year trace of a fact the design has decided to stop holding rather than a second copy of it. The two places the corpus names this column in past tense — the sensitive key-name list that 00-conventions §8 says it once carried, and the same list's history in the audit page — stay true and are not rewritten; the column joins the dead-name register so the checker treats every later appearance of the identifier as news.

    D-205 — There is no legal review, and assumption 49 is retired¶

    Status: decided (from your answer) — closes 06b §6's retention-as-law bullet, domain/03 §9's last bullet, 07-finance §5's legal-direction half, and open-questions "How to answer"'s second debt. It retires assumption 49 — the only numbered assumption in this corpus that named a person outside the design as the one who would settle it.

    The answer, as given. "Everything is our decision, there will be no review by the law so do not plan for one. We design the system, everything is my call."

    The rule. There is no legal review of this design, and there will not be one. No page may say one is required, pending, deferred, handed, or owed; no section may describe its own content as "the technical mechanism, not the compliance policy" on the understanding that somebody else supplies the other half; no policy may be filed as awaiting a basis. Every retention rule, every erasure rule, and every decision about what this system holds about a child is a decision of the person designing the system, taken on the day written beside it, with the risk named in the same paragraph instead of a lawful basis claimed.

    The five policies this now owns, and what "owns" means for each. They are the four 06b §6 was already counting, plus the one D-187 left with a lawyer and D-197 made unavoidable:

    The policy The decision, and the risk carried
    Named membership and fee_obligation rows are kept indefinitely, through and after an erasure request (D-088) Chosen because the alternative falsifies the organization's own history. The risk: it is the one class of row here that outlives every safeguard in §8, and nothing outside the design vouches for it.
    audit_log rows forever, payloads 24 months, 12 months the floor a window may be set to Chosen because a row without its payload answers who said this without saying what. The risk: two years of a person's record exist in a log that most readers never see.
    access_log rows deleted at 24 months (D-145) Chosen because a log of every check is the most sensitive table in the system and has no historical duty. The risk: a question asked in year three about a check in year one has no answer — the third hard delete, and the only one a clock runs.
    A child may hold an account with no guardian's answer recorded anywhere (D-166) Chosen because a consent checkbox nobody can verify records a decision that was never made. The risk: this is the answer a data subject asks about first, and the design answers it by holding nothing.
    What an invoice must contain, and how long it is kept Chosen by D-198: the row is the record that a document exists, the issuer's half of the document comes from deployment configuration, and the requirement to print more than this design holds is met outside it.

    What "the risk is named instead of a basis" means in practice, since it is the whole of the ruling. Where a page used to write this stands as an assumption pending review, it now writes this is what the design does, this is what could be asked about it, and the person designing it has decided. That is not a weakening — it is the removal of the only thing in this corpus that could have overruled a design choice from outside, and it should be read that way. The checks in 05d §11 and 06b §5 still verify the mechanisms against their own rules; what no longer exists is a rule those mechanisms could be measured against that anyone outside this document wrote. A design that has decided its own lawfulness is self-validating, and the sentence saying so belongs in the section it describes rather than here.

    What this changes, page by page — because this ruling is mostly a wording change, and a wording change that is left half-done is worse than the sentence it replaced. Nine live pages carried the review as a fact about the future of this project, and each is rewritten to carry the decision and the risk instead: 00-conventions §8 — item 3's "an assumption pending review" and the closing blockquote that begins "Legal review is required before implementing §8", which becomes a statement of who decided and when; 06b §6 — the "decided as policy, still open as law" bullet and its count of policies in front of a review; 06-audit-and-import where it says the claim "needs a legal basis and a decision log"; 07-finance §5 and its invoice rule; domain/01 §6 and domain/03 §9 on the same retention claim; domain/02 §2 on the child's account; 05c §9; glossary.md where it glosses the retention pair as having a legal basis; and the register's § I row 49 plus § I row 3, whose "falsified" note cites the review as the place the answer went. Past rounds are not rewritten — round 20 calling the review "the largest unowned risk in this corpus" and round 22 counting its four policies stay as they were written, because they are the record of a belief this round removes, and the register's § R row for assumption 49 is where the removal is stated. What no page may say afterwards: that anything in this design is awaiting verification, that a basis is outstanding, or that a mechanism is described "while the policy waits".
