    Decision Log — Refinement round 4 (D-047 … D-057)¶

    The organisational year, the gate and its waiver, roles as runtime data, and the removal of household.

    Refinement rounds 2 and 3 (D-031 … D-046) ← | All decisions | Refinement round 5 — the events module (D-058 … D-071) →

    D-047 — The fee expires on 1 January¶

    Status: decided (from your answer) Date: refinement round 4 Resolves: A-4 (the date half)

    Decision. The organizational membership year runs with the calendar: the fee for a year expires on 1 January. The yearly expiry sweep runs on that date, and the renewal campaign follows it.

    Why. Your answer, from the three options offered: "1 January". It aligns the year with the calendar, so "a member this year" means what a reader expects, and the sweep, the campaign and the reports all land on a boundary nobody has to look up.

    Consequence. membership_type.expiry_month / expiry_day carry it as data rather than as a constant, because a date written into code is a date the organization cannot move when it changes its mind. Every lapse date, the sweep, the renewal worklist ~~and the cache invalidation~~ derive from this one parameter **(cache invalidation left the list with the cache itself — see D-039's first cost bullet and D-114); changing it re-dates every future period but leaves historical ones alone, which is the reason it is a parameter and not an expression computed into stored rows.

    See: D-040, ../data-model/03-membership.md §1.

    D-048 — The renewal window is 60 days, and is a configuration parameter¶

    Status: decided (from your answer) Date: refinement round 4 Resolves: A-4 (the window half)

    Decision. The renewal window after the expiry date is 60 days. It is stored as a parameter (membership_type.renewal_grace_days), not compiled into the application.

    Why. Your answer: "Make it 60 days, but it should not be hard to modify (even if it would be in the code)." The 60 is a starting value; the requirement is that it can be changed without a code change.

    The design is already the parameter, deliberately. The window lives in the database, and in_renewal_window() reads it. So the second half of the answer is not a constraint to satisfy — it is a description of what is already built. The one thing that must not happen is a front-end or a scheduled job hardcoding 90 or 60; every reader goes through the column.

    Consequence. Seat the sweep and the renewal campaign to read the window from the row, and the value can be tuned between years as the organization learns what actually gets paid.

    See: D-040, ../data-model/03-membership.md §3.

    D-049 — The root may not read into its sub-units; scope_exposure is the only crossing¶

    Status: decided (from your answer) Date: refinement round 4

    Superseded by D-097. The crossing is gone because both of its sides are gone. The property this bought — that access to a body's members had to be granted by that body, by people who recruited the leader and can be asked — is recorded as lost in docs/README.md and in D-097's own costing, rather than being dropped quietly.

    Resolves: C-1

    Decision. There is no reporting channel outside the permission model and none is built. A unit's own material — its events, its rosters, its local leadership — is visible to the root only when that unit writes a scope_exposure naming the assignment it is exposed to. Every crossing is a written, attributed act by the unit that owns the data.

    Why. Your answer, from the three routes offered: "scope_exposure only". The no-downward-inheritance rule (D-031) is the organization's own constitutional principle — "the organization itself should not be able to itself decide permissions over the nested unit, it must come from the unit itself up." A reporting channel would be an exception to it that grows, because every subsequent question ("can the root see X?") has a plausible answer of "yes, for reporting". The rule is easier to keep when there is no side door to argue about.

    The cost, named. The root must ask, per unit, per period, and nobody can make a pack expose its scope. For the questions the root genuinely needs to answer, this is less painful than it sounds — see the consequence below, which is the part that makes this decision affordable.

    Consequence — most of C-1 dissolved when the root became the membership unit. The root owns the registry, the memberships and the fees (D-035), so a treasurer or registrar working at the root is not reaching into anyone else's data. These need no exposure at all:

    Question Answered from Exposure needed
    How many members were there in 2019? membership — the root's own table No
    Who is currently a member? membership No
    What fees are outstanding? fee_obligation No
    Was this person a member on this date? membership No
    Who was in the 4th Pack in 1998? unit_participation, scoped to that unit Yes
    Which events did a pack run? the pack's own material Yes
    Who leads a pack? position_assignment, scoped to that unit Yes

    The upper half is the organization's own records. The lower half is the unit's. This split is the whole of D-049: the root does not need to see into a unit to answer a question about membership, because membership is the root's.

    One thing that now looks like a bug and is not. The registrar role (appendix/permission-catalog.md §11) holds unit_participation.view at organization scope. Under exact-match scoping that does not let the registrar read any sub-unit's roster — the role's scope is the root, and a sub-unit roster is a different scope. It reads the root participation rows, which is what the registrar actually needs (D-041, 03-membership.md §5.1). This will be misread as a mistake by someone who assumes a parent can see its children. It is not a mistake, and the catalog says so in place.

    See: ../domain/06-authorization-model.md §2, ../data-model/05-authorization.md §5.

    D-050 — A renewal inside the window extends the existing root participation row¶

    Status: decided (from your answer) Date: refinement round 4

    Superseded by D-097 and D-098: there is no participation row, so a renewal extends nothing but the membership. The renewal-window semantics D-040 defined are untouched.

    Resolves: D-7

    Decision. When a membership lapses on 1 January and the person pays inside the window, the new membership period is backdated to the expiry date (D-040) and the root-unit participation row is extended to cover the same span. It is not closed and reopened.

    Mechanically, the sweep does not close the root row: it moves valid_to forward to 1 January, and the renewal clears it back to NULL (or sets the new period end), so the row tracks the membership across the boundary without ever being two rows.

    Why. Your answer: "Extend the existing row." The alternative — close at the expiry date, open a new backdated row — is more literal about what happened, but it makes the renewal path diverge from the initial-creation path, and it produces two rows per person per year for the one row the schema most wants to be simple.

    The honest cost, recorded rather than hidden. Extending erases the evidence that a lapse occurred: a person who lapsed and renewed looks, in unit_participation, exactly like a person who never lapsed. The gap exists only if you go and look at the membership rows. This is accepted deliberately, because:

    1. membership remains the authority on payment, and the lapse is fully visible there — the two rows disagree about nothing, they simply answer different questions.
    2. The participation table is a roster history ("who was in this unit when"), not a payment history. Erasing a gap in the roster is the correct thing to do: the child was in the pack across that period, and the question the table exists to answer has one answer, not two.

    Consequence for the participation overlap constraint. ex_unit_participation_no_overlap (03-membership.md §5) is what makes the close-and-reopen alternative awkward, and extending avoids the constraint entirely. The integrity check that verifies the root row mirrors the membership (03-membership.md §7, check 1b) remains valid and unchanged: after a renewal the two agree, and during a lapse-only period — before any payment — the membership has ended while the root row has not yet been swept, which is why the sweep must run and must run before anything reads the roster for authority.

    See: ../data-model/03-membership.md §5.1.

    D-051 — The membership gate is waived by a permission, not only by a role flag¶

    Status: decided (from your answer) Date: refinement round 4

    Reversed entirely by D-100. D-100 moves the gate into the audience expression, and this entry's system.gate_exempt is deleted together with the lockout hazard it documented. The diagnosis that produced it — a membership check performed inside can() is a lockout nobody can see — is still the reason the round kept every one of these tests out of the resolver.

    Resolves: D-6

    Decision. A new permission, system.gate_exempt, waives the membership gate (D-039) for anyone who holds it. It is delivered the way every other permission is: by being placed in a role, which is then assigned to a person at the root or at a unit.

    • It is a system-namespace permission, so it can only arrive through a role_assignment — never through an access_grant. An item-level grant must not be able to lift the gate.
    • It is exempt from the gate itself. Holding it does not depend on membership, or it could not do its job.
    • It is scope-independent: a person who holds it anywhere is waived everywhere. This is what makes it "system-wide" — the gate is a property of the person's membership, so the waiver has to be too.

    Why. Your answer: "Let's create a system-wide permission, that means that their permissions do not end if their membership lapses... The system.gate_exempt is one of the permissions that can be assigned to the permission groups."

    This replaces the earlier framing, in which role.requires_membership was the only switch and the question was therefore which roles to exempt. It is a better fit for the model as it actually works: every organizational role is a permission group, a person may hold several at once (a leader may also hold a role carrying system_admin; a counselor may hold a role carrying event creation and person.view), and a role may be assigned at the root or at a unit. A flag on the role answers "is this role membership-dependent?"; a permission answers "is this person exempt?", which is the question the exemption actually is.

    role.requires_membership stays. It is not superseded — the two compose, and they answer different questions:

    Question Granularity Effect
    role.requires_membership = false Is this role membership-dependent? the role the role grants regardless of membership
    system.gate_exempt Is this person exempt? the person every gated role they hold is honoured

    A role flag is the blunt form: it exempts everyone who holds that role, everywhere. The permission is the precise form: it exempts named people, and can be granted and revoked as an act of its own. The resolver honours a role if either applies.

    Who holds it. system_admin — without it, the 1 January sweep lapses every membership including the administrator's, and the person who would repair the system loses the ability to use it on the day it breaks. That is a lockout, not a policy. Beyond system_admin, which roles carry it is settled in the role review (F-1) — the mechanism is fixed here, the membership of the list is data.

    The evaluation must not go through can(). Reading the permission through the resolver would be circular: can() would have to evaluate the gate in order to learn whether the gate applies, and a lapsed holder of system.gate_exempt would be denied it by the very rule it exists to lift — an unbreakable lockout with no error message. The resolver therefore checks for the permission by direct lookup against live role assignments, outside its own gate condition. This is the one place the resolver reads the role tables without consulting itself, and it is deliberate.

    Consequence. Integrity reports gain a row: who currently holds an exemption, and under which assignment. It is a small list of people who keep authority without paying, and it should be as visible as the system.admin holders already are.

    See: ../data-model/05-authorization.md §3, ../data-model/05 §12, ../appendix/permission-catalog.md §11.

    D-052 — Roles are runtime data; the seed set is not a specification¶

    Status: decided (from your answer) Date: refinement round 4 Resolves: the remainder of D-6, and the framing of F-1

    Decision. Roles are created, edited, assigned and deleted by the organization at runtime — they are not a vocabulary the specification fixes. A person may hold several roles at once, at the root or at a unit, so a leader can hold a leadership role and a role carrying administrative permissions, and a counselor can hold a role carrying event creation and person.view.

    Why. Your answer: "The roles should be create-able in the system, and it should be also possible to assign people, delete the roles and so on. So you don't really need to know which roles carry system.gate_exempt."

    This is a correction to how the documentation was framing F-1. Three documents presented the role list as something awaiting approval, with a Waives gate column to be filled in. That was wrong: a role is a permission group, and which permissions an organization puts in a group is a configuration act, not a design decision. The seed set survives as a worked example of the shape; it is explicitly not a requirement list.

    What this does not delegate. The shape stays fixed, and two properties of it are load-bearing precisely because a configuration screen must not be able to weaken them:

    1. system.gate_exempt is system-namespace, so it can only arrive through a role and never through an access_grant. An item grant cannot lift the membership gate.
    2. A role that has ever been assigned cannot be deleted, so the assignment history survives. Deleting system_admin would remove the only route to system.admin permanently.

    Consequences, and what had to change in the schema.

    Gap Fix
    is_system claimed "cannot be deleted" with nothing enforcing it A BEFORE DELETE trigger: refuses deletion of any role with assignments, and of any system role
    No permissions existed for role lifecycle role.create and role.delete added to the catalog, both 🔒🔒
    Integrity check 16 Orphaned assignments

    This decision was amended by D-053, which removed the retirement mechanism D-052 originally introduced. D-052 as first written gave role a valid_from/valid_to, added a role.retire permission, made the resolver check the role's period, replaced UNIQUE (code) with a partial index so retired codes could be reused, and added check 17 reporting roles expiring while still held. All of that is gone: a role is valid always. The correction came from the user in the same round, and the superseded reasoning is kept here rather than edited away, because the reason it looked right is instructive — retirement appears to be the elegant way to abolish a role in one act, and it is only when you ask what "a role that is not in force" means for an assignment made in 2019 that the problem shows.

    The sharpest consequence, recorded because it is easy to miss. Editing a role's permissions takes effect immediately for every holder — no assignment touched, nobody notified. role.edit is a bulk privilege change wearing the clothes of a settings screen, and it is now marked as such. role.assign escalates one person; role.edit escalates everyone holding the role. Neither implies the other.

    See: ../data-model/05-authorization.md §3, ../appendix/permission-catalog.md §11.

    D-053 — A role is valid always; only its assignment is temporal¶

    Status: decided (from your answer) Date: refinement round 4 Amends: D-052

    Decision. role has no valid_from / valid_to. A role is valid all the time, for as long as the row exists. The only period in the picture is role_assignment.valid_from / valid_to — who held it, and from when to when.

    Why. Your answer: "The roles should be valid all the time, but their assignment to people shouldn't."

    D-052 had just given role a period, on the reasoning that retiring a role is a neat way to abolish one in a single act. That was wrong, and the reason is worth recording because it is not obvious until you push on it: a role is a definition, and a definition does not come into and out of force. "What did unit_leader grant?" has one answer, and it is the same answer for every date. What varies is who held it, which is what role_assignment records.

    The proposed retirement also creates a question with no good answer. If a role has a period and an assignment does not, then an assignment from 2019 rests on a role that is "not in force" — so was that person a leader in 2019? The honest answers are either "yes, and the role's period is irrelevant" (in which case the role's period never did anything) or "who knows" (in which case the design has damaged its own history). Making the role permanently valid avoids the question entirely.

    What this removes. The whole retirement mechanism D-052 introduced:

    Removed Replaced by
    role.valid_from, role.valid_to, ck_role_period, ix_role_current nothing — the columns are gone
    role.retire permission role.unassign, per assignment. A role nobody holds is unused, not retired
    The resolver's check on the role's period (channel 4) nothing. Channel 4 already checks the assignment's period, which was always the load-bearing one
    uq_role_code_current (partial unique, so retired codes could be reused) code TEXT NOT NULL UNIQUE — plain, because codes are never in competition
    Integrity check 17 (roles expiring while held) Reformulated as check 17 (unused roles) — a glance, not a warning

    What this keeps from D-052. Roles are still runtime data: created, edited, assigned and — if never assigned — deleted. is_system still guard-rails deletion. role and role_permission are still audited, because role.edit still changes effective permissions for every holder at once. The BEFORE DELETE trigger stays.

    The consequence worth carrying forward. Because a role never expires, the only thing that ends authority is the assignment ending. That puts the entire weight on role_assignment.valid_to, which is exactly where the design already wanted it — and it is why the open-ended assignment is called the classic access failure (data-model/05 §4). A role-based permission now has no expiry of its own; it is the assignment or nothing.

    See: ../data-model/05-authorization.md §3, ../appendix/permission-catalog.md §7.

    D-054 — The sub-unit vocabulary is not seeded; the organization defines its own types¶

    Status: decided (from your answer) Date: refinement round 4

    Superseded by D-097: there is no unit-type vocabulary left to seed or to withhold. 'The organization defines its own labels and the platform ships none' transfers intact to person_group and role, where it remains the rule.

    Amends: D-008 in spirit; the self-referencing table is untouched

    Decision. The unit-type seed data contains exactly one row: the root. No sub-unit types are seeded at all. org_unit_type is runtime data, like role (D-052): the organization creates the types it uses, renames them, and adds new ones as its structure changes.

    Why. Your answer: "Seed only the root."

    pack/group/team was a placeholder, and it was the last thing in the schema that was comfortably wrong — three invented words that would have read as a specification to anyone implementing the system. The deeper reason to remove rather than correct it: the organization's own vocabulary is not knowable at specification time, and guessing it badly is worse than not guessing. This is the same argument as D-052 applied to units. The retired national/region/district ladder (D-035) was a guess; so was its replacement.

    Consequence: nesting is entirely permissive. org_unit_type_rule exists and is seeded with nothing, so any type may sit under any other — including under the root — until the organization states a rule. This was already true with the placeholder (data-model/02 §10); it is now true with no ambiguity about which types the permissiveness applies to. A new type is usable the moment it is created.

    Consequence: the root type is special and must be seeded. The organization row stays, because exactly one unit must have parent_unit_id IS NULL (D-035) and that unit needs a type. It also carries the property that matters: allows_minors = false on the root type, since no minor belongs to "The Organization" as a unit — minors hold memberships and sub-unit participation.

    What this means for every example in these docs. The words pack, troop, section and group still appear throughout the documentation as illustration — "a pack has its own roster" is how the prose explains a self-governing sub-unit. They are not types the system ships with. Read them as "some sub-unit type the organization has defined", the same way unit_leader in §11 of the permission catalog is a role the organization may not have.

    What this removes. The three placeholder rows (pack, group, team) and the "PLACEHOLDER" note that graded them. Nothing else: no columns, no constraints, no checks. The change is confined to data-model/02 §10.

    See: ../data-model/02-organization.md §10, ../domain/04-organization-structure.md §1–2.

    D-055 — The permission catalog is deliberately comprehensive and granular¶

    Status: decided (from your answer) Date: refinement round 4 Amends: D-030

    Decision. The permission catalog is large and granular on purpose, and it stays that way. It is a menu from which administrators assign people only what they actually need — not a checklist every deployment is expected to use in full. Adding a permission remains one row plus its permission_level rows (D-007), and further permissions will arrive with the modules not yet specified (events, documents, communication, reporting).

    Why. Your answer, in full: "The permissions will be added by the additional modules I am yet to specify. It is good, that the list is large and granular so that the admins can assign people only the permissions they actually need."

    This settles F-1, and it settles it in the opposite direction from the roles question. The two are not symmetrical and it is worth saying why, because the asymmetry looks like an inconsistency:

    Roles Permissions
    What it is A grouping an organization invents A capability the code implements
    Who defines it The organization, at runtime The system, at build time
    Why Nobody can guess your vocabulary You cannot assign a power that is not expressible

    A permission is not a preference. membership.terminate exists because the code has a terminate operation; an organization that never expels anyone simply never grants it. A role is the opposite: unit_leader is only meaningful because someone in this organization chose that word. So a comprehensive permission catalog costs nothing but rows, while a comprehensive role list would be a specification pretending to be data.

    The sharp edge. Granularity is what makes least-privilege possible, so it is load-bearing rather than decorative — but .admin subsumption means a new permission is retroactively held by everyone with the namespace's .admin. Split a broad permission in two and an existing holder of the .admin gains both halves without an assignment being written. That is the intended behaviour (D-007), and it is also why role.edit is marked 🔒🔒: the catalog's granularity only helps if the grants are actually narrow.

    What this does not settle. The catalog's contents remain open to revision as the unbuilt modules land — this decision fixes the disposition (comprehensive, granular, menu-like), not the exact 90 rows. permission.edit exists for later pruning, and appendix/permission-catalog.md §12 tracks what is still being decided.

    See: ../appendix/permission-catalog.md, ../open-questions.md F-1.

    D-056 — The legacy import expects a large corpus with legacy member numbers¶

    Status: decided (from your answer) Date: refinement round 4

    Decision. The import is designed for a substantial historical corpus — thousands of records, not hundreds — and the old records carry a legacy member number. That number is imported into person_identifier with scheme = 'legacy' (data-model/01 §7).

    Why. Your answer: "Large, with legacy member numbers."

    This is the favourable case for D-004, and it materially changes what the import has to be:

    If there were no numbers With legacy numbers
    Matching Name + birth date + address, probabilistic, review-heavy Exact key when the number is present; heuristics only for backfilling it
    Review burden Every match needs a human (D-004 assumed this) Only the unmatched remainder does
    Confidence "Probably the same person" "Same number, same person"

    The consequence for the review design. D-004's "no automatic merging, ever" was costed on the assumption that review was the bottleneck and the corpus was small enough to review by hand. With thousands of rows,"review everything" is no longer feasible, and the honest position is that the number-bearing rows are the trustworthy set and the rest is a work queue. The number does the work the human would have done; the human handles the rows the number cannot resolve. This is a strengthening of D-004, not a weakening — the rule is unchanged, but the input is better, so the rule is cheaper to obey.

    What must hold for this to work. The legacy number must be imported verbatim and never reissued. person_identifier is already the right home: it records a scheme and a value with a period, so a second numbering era can coexist with the first. The check that matters is that no legacy number is ever assigned to two people — worth an integrity check on the (scheme, value) uniqueness, which the table's index already provides.

    The part that gets harder, not easier. Volume and numbers make identity resolution reliable, but they do nothing for the membership-vs-participation question raised by D-041. At this scale, a 1998 roster almost certainly records which unit someone attended and not whether their fee was paid, and the import must decide per row which of the two it becomes. The safe default — participation, unless there is payment evidence — matters more at thousands of rows than at hundreds, because the failure mode compounds: an inferred open-ended membership would make every 2003 roster member a current member today through the gate (D-039).

    Not settled here: the exact volume, the format, and the year range — those are operational inputs the import batch will carry. B-1 stays open for the sample, but the shape of the answer is now known.

    See: ../data-model/06-audit-and-import.md, ../domain/03-identity-and-legacy.md, ../open-questions.md B-1.

    D-057 — household is removed¶

    Status: decided (from your answer) Date: refinement round 4 Supersedes: D-019 — the table it described is gone, but the principle it stated is not, and that is deliberate. Amends: D-037

    Decision. The household and household_member tables are removed, along with fee_obligation.household_id, its CHECK arm, and ix_obligation_household. A person's address is a person_contact row of type postal — data-model/01 §6 — which already exists, already keeps history, and already carries a purpose ('billing') that the permission model can distinguish. Same-address matching for the legacy import becomes a query over person_contact, not a maintained table.

    Why. Your answer: "Drop household entirely."

    D-037 had already removed the load-bearing half: the fee is flat and per person, so there is no per-family price for a household to hold. What remained was two conveniences — "one invoice per household" and "which children lived at this address in 2003?" — and both are served by tables that must exist anyway:

    • The invoice. person_contact with purpose = 'billing' is where a billing address belongs. A household table was duplicating it under a different key, and the duplication had already gone stale: household.postal_address was documented as "denormalised from the billing contact's primary postal contact", which is a copy with no trigger keeping it true.
    • The 2003 address question. person_contact is temporal (valid_from / valid_to, "Addresses change; history is required"). So "who lived at this address in 2003?" is answered from the contacts table directly — and more honestly, because it asks about the address a person had, rather than requiring that someone first grouped those people into a household row that has itself since been renamed or split.

    The one thing genuinely lost. A household is a group with a name and its own lifetime, and person_contact records addresses per person. Two people at one address are related by a query, not by a row — so there is nowhere to hang "the Novák family" as a first-class thing, and no valid_from for the grouping. Accepted, because no feature was using it: nothing set fee_obligation.household_id, and the display-name use (household.name) has no consumer in the design.

    What this does not change. The safeguarding property D-019 was written to protect survives intact, and this is the point worth checking before deleting anything: being at the same address still grants no access. It never came from the household table in the first place — all access comes from guardianship + guardianship_permission (D-020). Removing a table that granted nothing cannot grant anything. The old D-019 entry stays in the log, marked superseded, because "why does living together not imply access?" is a question that will be asked again.
