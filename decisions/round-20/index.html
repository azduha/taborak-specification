    Decision Log — Refinement round 20 (D-137 … D-149)¶

    Thirteen open questions answered in one sitting: the attribute vocabulary loses its proposal stage and its queue, access_log rows are deleted at 24 months while the audit trail keeps its shape, a delegation is validated once and stands, a display name's drift gets the check it already had, and four words — WHITELISTED, a translated label, an expired registration, a notification nobody receives — are refused in writing. Four questions survive the round untouched and a fifth arrives with it; not one of the five is 🟡.

    Refinement round 19 (D-133 … D-136) ← | All decisions

    What was asked. Put the open questions, in batches, and write down what came back. Seventeen were on the page; thirteen were put and answered, and four were left where they are (A-4's remainder, E-3, F-9, F-11) because answering them needs a document or a lawyer rather than a ruling. Every 🟡 on that page was answered this round — all six of them — which is why the page now reads as a list of things that can be deferred, and why § J emptied itself and was deleted the way sections B, C and D were.

    What the round found while answering, which is again the part worth reading. Four of the thirteen questions arrived with a premise the corpus had already invalidated, and in one case the premise and the answer were on opposite sides of a contradiction the corpus was living inside.

    • F-32 was not a choice between two policies; it was a contradiction with one side already implemented. The question asked whether a stale delegation should be reported or left invisible, presuming the design re-checks it. domain/06 §9 does promise that — "validate at grant time and re-check at use time" — and 05 §6 says flatly the opposite: "nothing about the person who lent it is re-validated when a check runs". removed-concepts records why: the column that made a use-time re-check possible was deleted, "and with it the second half of the guard — there is no source row left to re-validate". So two pages promised a thing two other pages say cannot happen, and the invariant register said the rule was enforced twice, once of them silently. The ruling picked the side the data model was already on, which makes this entry a repair with a reason attached rather than a change of behaviour.
    • F-28's register row claimed a rule had no report beneath it. The report was already written. 01b §10 counts persons whose given_name/family_name differ from the open person_name row — the exact check the open question offered as "cheap to write" — and closes by saying the check exists and that what is undecided is whether a report is the right thing or the copy is the wrong one. Round 18 minted that row beside the inventory, and its **none** was wrong in the understating direction: the table said nothing watches a rule that something watches. Round 19 found a register row claiming more than its page said; this round found the mirror image, and D-129 catches both — a register row is a claim about a page, and none is as much a claim as a citation.
    • AT-2's proposal stage was a third status no decision had granted. D-084 settled the vocabulary in one sentence — "a status of live or retired" — and the pages and the inventory carried proposed anyway, complete with a proposed_by, a proposed_at, an import fallback that writes it, and an integrity check whose whole job is to age it out. A mechanism was built for a status that arrived by transcription.
    • E-6's answer had no addressee. The question's own recommendation asked for a "safeguarding contact" to be notified, and no such office exists: safeguarding_officer is listed among the worked examples the catalog marks as not shipping, and D-132 is about to delete the seed that would have offered one. A question that asks "who should be told" cannot be answered by naming a person the design does not have, so the answer is the one that needs nobody: the trail is the control.
    • One deletion moved ten citations, all of them ordinals. Deleting 09 §8's first check renumbers the six that follow it, and ten sentences cite those checks by number: six inside the page that owns them, two in the invariant register, one in 01b and one in 06b §5. Two more citations died with the thing they pointed at — both were the curation queue reaching for the age-out check that is now gone. All ten were rewritten, and all ten were found by grepping the number rather than by remembering to look: a checker aimed at the target of §8 check 4 keeps passing after the list above it shrinks, because the target still exists and still says check 4. An ordinal is a pointer that moves when the list above it changes, which is the same failure this corpus keeps hitting from the other end: a checker verifies a target and never a claim, and nothing at all verifies a number beside a sentence. This round counted this list wrong while writing it — it said seven sentences and then named eight — which is why the count here is stated as grepped, not as remembered.

    D-137 — A guardian may appoint a guardian, and nobody at the organization is told¶

    Status: applied.

    Question. E-6 asked what happens when a guardian adds a second guardian over a child. Guardianship is a channel with a right_level (D-113), so the act is inside the model already: a person with guardianship.assign writes a row. What was undecided was whether the organization should hear about it — the page offered a notification to a safeguarding contact, a review queue, or silence, and it recommended the notification.

    Answer. Attribution is enough. No notification, no queue, no addressee. The act is a write, the write is audited with the actor who made it and the person it was about, and that trail is the whole control. There is no office to notify and nothing in this design that would receive a notification at all.

    Why the recommended option was the wrong one, not merely the expensive one. The recommendation named a "safeguarding contact" as if the design had one. It does not, and it cannot offer one without a grant aimed at a person or an office somebody writes — which is exactly the machinery D-132 is going to delete from the seed. A notification whose recipient is a row somebody must remember to create is a control that is off by default and silent about being off, which is worse than the trail, because the trail is on whether or not anyone configured it. This is the same reasoning that refused a report for F-29: a guard nobody reads is not a control, and a guard nobody configures is not one either.

    What this costs, said plainly. Nothing will ever tell a parent, a second guardian, or an officer that a third adult was given authority over a child. The answer to "who added this person, and when" is excellent and the answer to "did anyone think about it" is the person who did it, alone, at the moment they did it. The mitigation that does exist is that guardianship.assign is one of the codes this corpus marks ⚠⚠ — the grant is the rare thing, not the act.

    Changes no table and no column. The open question's recommendation is deleted with the question; the guardianship.assign row in catalog-a §5 is unchanged.

    See: domain/06 §1, 04 §Guardianship, and E-6 in § R.

    D-138 — One permission guards the whole audit trail, and the widening is on the record¶

    Status: applied.

    Question. AT-4 asked whether audit.view is enough to guard a trail that holds person data. The trail's payloads are values a person wrote — an old address, an allergy, a diagnosis — emptied 24 months later (D-088) but readable in full until then, and they are readable whether or not the reader may read the person they describe. 06 §2.3 records that a per-entry "could this reader see this field" check was considered and left unimplemented; D-133 declined to stretch the trail for a report and explicitly did not answer this.

    Answer. audit.view is the whole gate. One permission, one check, no per-entry filter, now decided rather than merely unbuilt. The history view is a person-data surface and is treated as one: the code is granted rarely, it is marked, and what it opens is the entire trail.

    What was refused, and what the refusal is really buying. The alternative was a check inside the history view comparing each entry's subject against the reader's own reach. It would have been correct and it would have been slow in the way D-114 says this system accepts — a check that resolves rather than caches — but it would also have made the trail's readability depend on the same audience expressions it exists to explain, so that a rule changed last week would silently rewrite what an officer can read about 2019. The trail's value is that it is legible without re-resolving history, and a per-entry check spends exactly that. The widening is therefore accepted as a property, stated in the pages that grant the code instead of being left to be discovered by whoever first opens the screen.

    What this does not do. It does not widen anything. No behaviour changes: nothing in the corpus has ever implemented the per-entry check, so this decision turns an omission into a position and pays for it in a sentence. It is also not the same question as F-17 — whether "who could read this in 2023" is reconstructable at all — which stays open.

    Changes no table and no column.

    See: 06 §2.3, 05c §9, D-114, D-133, and AT-4 in § R.

    D-139 — An event question may bind to a stored value: it prefills as a suggestion and writes back as an answer¶

    Status: applied.

    Question. AT-1 asked whether an event_question may be tied to an attribute_definition code, so that a camp form asking "do you take any medication" and the person's stored attribute are the same fact twice, or one fact asked twice. Two reasons had kept it open: the form's answer and the record's value would disagree with no rule saying which wins, and 00 §4's erasure exception had a condition on its face: "This exception is safe only while nothing references an attribute row, and nothing does today", naming this very binding as the thing that would break it.

    Answer. The binding is allowed, in one direction, with both ends named. A question may hold a bound_attribute_code — nullable, pointing at the definition, never at a value row. The form prefills from the person's current value as a suggestion, which the answerer sees and may change; nothing is hidden from them and nothing is silently copied. On submit, the answer writes back to person_attribute as a new value, dated today, recorded as written by the person who submitted the form. The answer stays a row of the event either way; the write-back is a second act, not the same row twice.

    Why the binding does not detonate the rule it was fenced off by. The condition in §4 protects erasure's one hard delete: redaction deletes person_attribute rows outright, and that delete is invisible only for as long as nothing points at those rows. A bound question points at the vocabulary, not at a value — at attribute_definition.code, which is never deleted, is retired instead, and survives the person entirely. So the reference edge §9 would have to walk does not exist: the question names a code, the value rows under it come and go with the person, and erasure's delete stays invisible. This is recorded here rather than left as a lucky consequence, because the next person to design a binding will reach for a value's key; a reference from an event row to a person_attribute row id remains exactly as forbidden as §4 says it is.

    Which one wins, and when neither does. The person record wins as of today, because the write-back is dated today and the attribute's history is the record of what was believed when. An answer is not corrected when the record later changes, and the record is not corrected when an old answer said otherwise — they are two dated statements of the same fact at two moments, which is the shape this corpus already uses for a name. Where the answer contradicts the stored value, the difference is visible on the form (the prefill shows the stored value) and that is the intended repair route: a person who edits the prefill is editing the record.

    What this costs. A form can now write to the person record, which is a new path into person_attribute and the first place outside the person's own editing where an attribute value arrives without a staff member in the middle of it. The write goes through the same person.edit permission the answering channel already uses, is audited with the answerer as its actor, and is refused — with the form still saving its answer — when that write is not permitted, so a form never half-writes a person. And a bound question now cannot be re-pointed at a different code without noticing that its past answers belong to the old one; they stay what they were.

    Adds: one nullable column, event_question.bound_attribute_code, holding an attribute_definition.code by value and not a row id — which is what keeps 00 §4's ban on row references intact, and what makes this decision answer a question shape 4 had never been asked. Because a code is now referenced, D-131's test reaches a vocabulary code for the first time: attribute_definition.code joins shape 4. A definition may still be retired, and may still be merged — the merge of two codes is exactly the deliberate re-point this entry already demands, and it carries the bound questions with it rather than orphaning them — but a code with a question bound to it may no longer be renamed, because a rename would silently re-answer every form that was asked under the old name. The register carries the row (00a §4), and the register grew from six members to seven the day a form was allowed to point at a word. Retires: assumption 58's half that a form answer never reaches the person record.

    See: 09 §3, 09 §4, 08 §7, 00 §4, domain/07a §12, and AT-1 in § R.

    D-140 — Nothing about an event is deleted on a schedule, and erasure empties an answer without deleting it¶

    Status: applied.

    Question. EV-2 asked how long an event_answer survives its event. Three pages said answers "end with the event" and none said when, what happens to them, or who does it; the same question noted that the identical fact recorded as a person_attribute is not deleted on a schedule at all, so the freshest copy of a health fact was the weakest-protected one.

    Answer. There is no expiry for registrations or answers. Neither ends with its event, and neither ends on a clock. They follow the rule the accounting skeleton already follows: the row is a fact about a dated occasion, and it is kept. Erasure does reach them, and it empties them: a redacted person's event_answer.value is set to empty and the row survives, because "they answered that question" and "this is what they said" are two different facts and only the second one is theirs to withdraw. The rows of event_registration are kept as they are today; a cancelled event already keeps every registration (08a §8, guard 2), and a cancelled event with no registrations would be an event that demonstrably never happened.

    Why this direction and not a schedule. The question's own sharpest point was the asymmetry: the same sentence of personal data, kept forever in one table and quietly discarded in another, with the discarded one being the copy a camp leader actually reads. A schedule would have had to be justified to the person it deletes from, and "we forget it after N years because that number is defensible" is a weaker answer than "we keep what happened and we erase what you ask us to". It also would have been the third retention clock in a system that has deliberately managed with two (D-088).

    What "empty the value, keep the row" costs. An emptied answer still says something — that a question was asked and answered, by whom, on what date — and that residue is deliberate, the same residue D-088 leaves in an audit payload. It is also a rule that store has to implement rather than one the schema enforces: 00 §8 now lists event_answer beside person_attribute among the places erasure has to reach, and 06b §5's erasure check counts emptied-value answers the way it counts surviving names, so an erasure that forgot the event tables stops passing a check.

    Deletes: the phrase "answers end with the event" wherever it stands as a policy, and event_answer's open retention item from two open-issue lists. Changes no table and no column.

    See: 08 §7, 08a §11, 07a §12, 00 §8, D-083, and EV-2 in § R.

    D-141 — A definition is live at the save; the proposal stage, its queue and its check are deleted¶

    Status: applied.

    Question. AT-2 asked who curates the attribute vocabulary and how long a proposed definition may sit before somebody rules on it. The pages described a two-stage life: an attribute_definition could be proposed, carrying proposed_by and proposed_at, waiting in a curation queue for a curator to make it live — and an import column nobody had mapped arrived there by itself, so the corpus could acquire vocabulary through nobody deciding anything.

    Answer. There is no proposal, and every field must be defined beforehand. attribute.define is the curating permission and a definition is live at the save: the same person's write that names a code publishes it. status keeps the two values D-084 granted it — live and retired — and nothing else. proposed_by and proposed_at are deleted, there is no review list, no waiting, no age-out, and no curator role: the permission is the curator.

    Refused: a value inventing a code. A person recording a value under person.edit cannot create vocabulary by typing something adjacent to it, and the mechanism that made that possible — writing the unknown as a proposed definition and letting it wait — is gone. An import column that maps to no defined attribute is an error at apply time, not a proposal. A row that would need a new code either has one already written by someone holding attribute.define, or it does not import and the batch says so. That is the strictest of the options on the question's page and it was chosen for the reason the answer gave: a vocabulary is the one registry where an unreviewed addition is permanent in effect even if it is deletable in principle, because every value recorded against a bad code inherits it.

    What this exposes, stated rather than smoothed over. attribute.define is held by nothing at day one — the seed that would have held it is deleted by D-132, and the vocabulary therefore starts empty and stays empty until a person with the code writes it. Day one has exactly one account, holding system.admin, so day one's vocabulary is written by that account; after that, whoever the organization aims the code at. Two pages had been asserting a "named curator" as a role that reads what it is given; both are corrected, because a role is an office that confers nothing (D-100) and the only thing here that curates is a permission.

    What a bad code costs now. It is live the moment it is saved, with no gate in front of it, and the values recorded against it are real values on real people from that moment. The defence is three things and no more: saving a definition needs a permission, the trail records every save, and a code can be retired — which keeps its values and stops new ones (D-109's rule for anything a rule names).

    Deletes: the proposed status, proposed_by, proposed_at, the curation queue, the "Proposed code" glossary entry, the import's unmapped-column fallback, and 09 §8 check 1 — which ages out a status that no longer exists. Renumbering §8's remaining six checks moves six ordinal citations in other pages and four more in the same page; all ten are recomputed, not remembered. Changes one table (attribute_definition: one status value and two columns gone; event_question gains D-139's column in the same round, so the vocabulary and its first consumer move together).

    See: 09 §2, 09 §7, 06a, 08a §11, catalog-b §12, D-084, and AT-2 in § R.

    D-142 — The displayed name stays a copy, and the check that counts its drift is named at last¶

    Status: applied.

    Question. F-28 asked whether the denormalised given_name/family_name on person is a copy to keep in step with person_name, or a field to delete so that the displayed name is always read from the history. The register carried the rule "the denormalised name agrees with the name history" with no report beneath it at all.

    Answer. The copy stays, and the drift is reported. person.given_name/family_name remain, written in the same transaction that writes the name history, and the check that counts the persons where they have driven apart is named as the rule's backstop: 01b §10 counts persons whose two columns differ from the person_name row with no valid_to. That check already existed — see the lede — so what this decision changes is which page admits it.

    Why keep a denormalised copy at all, in a corpus that has deleted several. Because the name a reader must be able to print for a person who has never been opened as a record — a roster printed at a door, a label, a search hit — should not depend on walking a history table to find the open row. The alternative was a view, and a view is what this design already has: the copy is the materialised half of it, and the drift check is the price of materialising anything, paid in the open. The display rule is unchanged — preferred_name ?? given_name, from 01 §2.

    What it costs: a name can be wrong in one place while right in another, and only a report will notice. That is what a denormalised column is; the design declines to be surprised by it.

    Deletes: nothing but a sentence in 01b §10 that was still asking the question the check beside it had answered, and one **none** in 00a — the second of the seven to go, and the first that was wrong because it understated what the design already does.

    Changes no table and no column.

    See: 01 §2, 01b §10, 00a, D-129, and F-28 in § R.

    D-143 — A delegation is validated once, when it is written, and stands to its own end date¶

    Status: applied.

    Question. F-32 asked whether a delegation whose delegator has lost the code should be reported or left invisible, on the understanding that the design re-checks the delegator at use time, so that a loan stops working the instant it goes stale and the delegatee gets an ordinary denial that explains nothing.

    Answer. The delegator is checked once, at grant time, and never again. No report is written, nothing cascades, and the delegation stands until its own valid_to or a revocation. "You may not lend more than you hold" is a set-membership test on the person writing the grant (domain/06 §9); it is true of the moment it is checked and it is not a promise about the term. The check was simplified as well as scoped: the register's layer column no longer claims the rule is enforced twice a day, once of them silently.

    Why this is a repair and not a policy choice. Because the design was already this way and one page had not noticed — see the lede. 05 §6 states that nothing about the lender is re-validated when a check runs and draws the consequence itself: that is why a delegation must expire. removed-concepts records that the column which made the re-check possible is gone with the table it named. Keeping domain/06's promise would have been a design change; deleting it is the cheapest correct answer available, and the user's ruling said to simplify the check rather than to build it.

    The two bounds that survive, which are what makes this tolerable rather than merely cheap. A delegation must expire — valid_to is required on a delegated grant and integrity check 11 reports one that does not — and system.admin may not be delegated at all. So the failure this ruling permits has a shape: a person keeps an authority their lender no longer holds, for a term somebody fixed in advance and cannot exceed, and the trail says who lent it to whom. What it does not permit is a permanent loan of the only code that can restructure the system.

    What it costs. A delegate may be doing something they should no longer be able to do, and nothing will tell anybody until the loan's own date arrives or someone notices and revokes it. The corpus previously promised the opposite and promised it silently — a stale loan would stop with an ordinary denial, indistinguishable from never having had it, which is the kind of guarantee that reads as safety and is actually a person wondering why their tool broke. A bound that is visible and ends is a better object than an invisible one that might never end.

    Deletes: the use-time half of the delegation rule in domain/06 §9, the register's second enforcement point for that row, and F-32's report option. Changes no table and no column — and no code, since the resolver never had the second check to remove.

    See: domain/06 §9, 05 §6, 05d §11 check 11, removed-concepts, and F-32 in § R.

    D-144 — right_kind is a note: required by the schema, checked against nothing, and no longer claiming to be checked¶

    Status: applied.

    Question. F-33 asked whether a permission's right_kind has to agree with what the code says — event.answer.view being view — or whether it is a description that the row keeps for its reader. The register carried "a code's right_kind matches what the code says" against the save path, with no report beneath it.

    Answer. It stays required and it agrees with nothing. No constraint, no check, no report. The column is required because a row in permission is read by a person deciding what to grant, and a code whose declared kind reads oddly is worth noticing while reading; not because anything will ever tell anybody. It is admitted as a note, which is what catalog-a §8b has been saying all along with its sentence that the marking changes no answer and is filled in because the column is required.

    Why keep a required column that nothing checks, when deletion was on the table. Deleting it was the tidy answer and it was refused, for a reason worth keeping: the code's naming grammar (D-104) is enforced by a hand-run diff, and a second, independently written word for the same fact is where that kind of drift surfaces in a human reading rather than in a script. The column's whole job is to be legible. The cost is symmetrical and is now stated in the page rather than hidden in a column comment: a right_kind can contradict its code forever, and only a reader will notice.

    What this corrected as it went. The register row is withdrawn, not repaired — D-129 again, the third row this register has had to lose. No page enforces the match; no page claims to; the row was a claim about a save path that has no such rule. This is the third of the register's eleven none rows to be resolved, and the only one resolved by deletion.

    Deletes: one row of 00a and one shape-3 rule from its count. Changes no table and no column — permission.right_kind remains required exactly as it was.

    See: 05 §2, catalog-a §8b, 00a, D-129, and F-33 in § R.

    D-145 — access_log rows are deleted at 24 months, and the job that does it has a name¶

    Status: applied.

    Question. F-8 asked whether the access trail is bounded at all. The page that owns the table had already reversed its own original "never deletion" and put forward the same 24 months audit_log's payloads get — as a proposal awaiting a person, saying so in as many words, with rows kept and their state columns emptied. Assumption 50 registered the legal premise under it.

    Answer. Confirmed, with the rows going rather than the contents: an access_log row older than 24 months is deleted. audit_log is untouched and keeps D-088's opposite shape — rows for the life of the system, payloads emptied at 24 months. The two clocks now differ in kind as well as in length: one trail shrinks and stays, the other disappears. Monthly partitioning survives as the mechanism, because dropping a partition is how a row deletion of this size is done without locking the table.

    The corpus-wide consequence, which is the reason this entry is long. 00 §4 says "Nothing is hard-deleted. There is no deleted_at and no delete for business data," and then names two exceptions — a staging truncate and the delete erasure has to make. This is the third, and it is named in §4 rather than excepted in a corner, because a page that quietly deletes rows while the conventions page promises none is the kind of page that gets "fixed" back. The exception is narrow in exactly the way §4 wants its exceptions narrow: it reaches no business row, it deletes only access_log, only by age, only forward from the write, and it is the third writer the append-only guard must let through beside erasure and payload reduction. "Append-only" continues to mean no application writes or removes these rows, which is what it always protected.

    What dies with a row. Everything the row could answer after its 24th month: that a person opened a record, that a screen was reached, which channel and which grant answered it. audit_log answers what was changed, and answers it forever; nothing answers what was read beyond two years. The register and the inventory say so plainly instead of letting the reader infer permanence from the word "trail", and 05d §11 check 23 — which walks this table's three keyless columns, with the legal-absence rule beside them — keeps working unchanged, because a deleted row cannot be an orphan. It also gains nothing: the age-out gets its own check, the sibling of the one that counts audit rows past their window still holding a payload, because D-133's reasoning cuts both ways — a reduction nobody verifies is a reduction that quietly stops happening, and here the failure direction is data kept too long, which is the failure the window exists to prevent.

    Adds: a sixth actor label, system:log-reduction, to the fixed vocabulary in 00 §7a. A job that deletes rows must be nameable, and an empty actor column that could mean the renewal sweep, the bank pull, the payload reduction or a migration is not an answer to "who deleted my access history". Every sentence in the corpus stating that the list has five labels is corrected to six — five pages, one of them the register row for F-25, which is the live record of that decision and is amended rather than left to be contradicted.

    Refused: emptying the rows instead, which the proposal on the page preferred. It is the weaker rule with the better story — it keeps "the table never loses a row" — and it was declined because a row that says this record was read on this date by this person is itself the personal data the window was asked about, not a skeleton worth keeping.

    Changes no table and no column. Deletes: the "rows are never deleted" position in three pages, the proposal framing in two, and assumption 50, which is now a decision.

    See: 05c §9, 00 §4, 00 §7a, 06b §5, 05d §11, D-088, D-125, D-134, and F-8 in § R.

    D-146 — An outbound call is a person's act or a job, and nothing about it is designed yet¶

    Status: applied.

    Question. F-27 asked what happens when this system calls out — everything designed so far is a person calling in, or one of two inbound jobs pulling data — so who is the actor of an outbound write, and against what is it authorized?

    Answer. No outbound integration is designed, and no third kind of actor will be invented when one is. An outbound call is either a person acting — the actor column holds them, their permission is checked, and the trail shows them the way every other act shows them — or this system acting on its own, which is the empty actor column and a label from the fixed vocabulary (D-125). There is no service identity, no key acting as a person, no "the integration did it". If a future integration needs a caller that is not a person and is not this system, that is D-132's successor conversation, not a column added in passing.

    What was refused with it. An api_key acting as its own actor for outbound calls. Keys were given a careful, narrow shape last round (D-116): a key is a caller into this system, is never inside an expression, and is refused five codes by equality. Making the same object mean "the thing we call out to" as well would put the accountability of an outbound write on a secret instead of on a person or a job, and a secret is exactly the thing that leaks.

    What this costs. A real integration, when it comes, will have to make one of the two choices explicitly and will probably find the choice uncomfortable: a person acting means their permission gates a third party's system, and a job means an empty actor column on a write the organization cannot pin on anybody. Both are answerable; neither is answered here, and the point of deciding now is that the answer must be one of the two, so the vocabulary of five labels becoming six (D-145) is the only growth this axis gets by accident.

    Changes no table, no column, no code.

    See: 00 §7a, 05e §A key, D-125, D-116, and F-27 in § R.

    D-147 — Presence stays on the registration: no clock of its own, no permission of its own¶

    Status: applied.

    Question. EV-4 asked whether was_present — the three-valued mark of who actually turned up (D-067) — needs its own retention rule or its own permission, because "who was in our care on that day" is the sentence a safeguarding question would be asked with.

    Answer. Neither. Presence is a column of a registration and inherits everything a registration is: the same retention D-140 just settled for registrations, and the same two codes that already reach the roster and the mark. It is not a record class and this system does not keep a safeguarding register. There are no background checks here (D-043) and no medical record (D-083); a column with a special clock and a special permission would be the first wall of an archive this design has refused four times.

    What stands in its place, since the need is real. The question "who was there on 14 March" is answered by a report over registrations — which is why the index behind it exists and why it serves a report and no selector — and the answer is legible for as long as the registrations are, which is now indefinitely rather than until somebody's retention number. The protection this column has is that it is boring: readable only with the codes that reach the event, erased by nothing except the person's own erasure, which reaches a registration the way it reaches an answer.

    Refused both halves — a presence.retention shorter than the event's, and a presence.view narrower than the roster's. The second would have been a permission whose whole purpose is to hide a fact from the people who are asking because they were there.

    Changes no table and no column.

    See: 07a §9, 08a §11, D-067, D-140, and EV-4 in § R.

    D-148 — There is no WHITELISTED, and the refusal is written where the tiers are¶

    Status: applied.

    Question. EV-6 named the word people reach for: an audience for "the ones we invited". The language has four attendance tiers and ORGANIZERS, and refuses PRESENT(<event>) outright because was_present is NULL until somebody takes a register — a volunteer's forgotten tick would revoke a child's own photos. WHITELISTED was never seeded, was described as having no stored state to evaluate, and was asked about in the open questions because the word kept coming up in the corpus as a refusal without a home: the question's own instruction was that "no WHITELISTED" is a sentence that has to exist somewhere, in the same paragraph that refuses PRESENT.

    Answer. Refused, in writing, where the tiers are listed. No is_whitelisted column, no fifth tier, no operand, and no reserved name. Anyone adding is_whitelisted is reimplementing grants badly, and the sentence now appears beside the presence refusal in the one place that named a PRESENT refusal without it (domain/07a §9).

    Why the answer is free here, which is worth noticing. The refusal already existed four times over — 08a §8, domain/07, domain/06a §Event sets, 05a and the catalog all state it, and three of those passages already pair it with the presence refusal. The count of refused operands does not move: WHITELISTED is already one of "three operands not in this table" and of the two event sets refused in domain/06a. What was missing was a sentence with the word in it in the presence section, and that is a paragraph, not a design.

    The need underneath, which is not denied. An invited person who has not registered is, at capacity, ON_WAITLIST if they register onto a full event and described by the absence of a row if they never register (D-059) — and they are invited by a grant aimed at them, which is a row that exists and says who. A tier is a state of a registration; an invitation is not about a registration at all. That is the argument the refusal rests on, and it is why the fifth tier would have cost the meaning of the four that exist: PLACED is the capacity set, so an invited person who registers onto a full event is already misdescribed the moment a whitelist set is added beside it.

    What this costs: the same thing every prose refusal costs — nothing in the database refuses a future WHITELISTED row in selector_type, so an operand with no state behind it is a deployment bug rather than a type error, exactly as an off-list actor label is. That is D-125's cost again, on the vocabulary side.

    Changes no table and no column.

    See: domain/06a §Event sets, 05a, 08a §8, 07a §9, D-102, F-20, and EV-6 in § R.

    D-149 — Words are stored in one language, and a label is not translated¶

    Status: applied.

    Question. AT-3 asked whether attribute_definition and attribute_option labels need a second language, because a form that asks a question in a language the family reads is the whole point of a form.

    Answer. One language: the one the organization writes in. No label_cs/label_en pair, no translation table, no per-language row, no locale column anywhere in the attribute vocabulary. The vocabulary is the organization's own words, written at runtime by whoever it hands the curating permission to (D-084), and the page that owns the table describes a label in four words: what a human sees (09 §2). A label is not a rendering of something more authoritative underneath it — and a second language would need one, because a row holding label_cs and label_en has to be asked which of the two the record is, and whichever answer is chosen demotes the other to a translation of it.

    What happens to a translated form, since the need is real. The people who need another language are served by the person reading the question aloud or writing it down, which is how a Scout organisation has always done it, and by an event form's own question text — a question's text is free text somebody types in whichever language the event is run in, and it is not a vocabulary item. The vocabulary is for the record; the form is for the conversation, and only one of the two has to be stable enough to query.

    What this costs: an imported label arrives in whatever language the paper form was in and stays there, which makes correcting it a curation act under attribute.define — live at the save, per D-141 — and means an organisation that genuinely runs in two languages will have two codes for one fact, which is a misery the design does not prevent and does not pretend to.

    Changes no table and no column.

    See: 09 §2, 09 §5, D-084, and AT-3 in § R.
