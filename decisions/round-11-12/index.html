    Decision Log — Refinement rounds 11 and 12 (D-105 … D-108)¶

    No LIST, no depth bound, and an operand that may name a stored fact — age among them.

    Refinement round 10 — the deletions, part 2 (D-101 … D-104) ← | All decisions | Refinement round 13 (D-109 … D-113) →

    D-105 — There are no LISTs: a hand-written set is an expression like any other¶

    Status: decided — from your request, with both open points ruled on the same day. Date: refinement round 11

    Ask: "Let's completely remove an option to specify people in an audience in a table (as a LIST) and instead list them all in a rule (eg. PERSON(a) + PERSON(b)). Will it cause any issues? It feels like a great simplification." Ruled on: dated names → no; the depth bound → removed, and the tree stored exactly as the writer wrote it.

    Follows: D-011, D-048, D-088, D-091, D-098, D-099, D-101, D-103, D-104.

    1. What is deleted, and the embarrassment that makes it easy¶

    audience_member is deleted, and with it the LIST operand and its seed row. The selector registry is twelve rows. A hand-written set of people is now written where every other set is written — in the expression, as PERSON(a) + PERSON(b) — and it parses today, which is the whole reason this is a deletion rather than a redesign.

    The embarrassing part, recorded because it is the same shape as D-104's finding: LIST was kept by D-101 on the stated grounds that "a hand-written set of three people is not derivable from anything", and D-101 was amended, late in the same round, to add PERSON(x) — the operand that names exactly one human being. That amendment removed the table's reason and the round did not notice, so the last round ended with two ways to write the same set of names and a page of prose (data-model/05 §8, What LIST is, said plainly — withdrawn by D-105) arguing that they were two operands and not one wearing two names. They were one. The argument was correct about the semantics and wrong about the conclusion, and the difference between those two is a table nobody needs.

    What survives untouched, because the hazard it guarded is real and unchanged: naming three people inside a grant must not mean making them members of a person_group. A group's members are true when no grant points at them (D-098); editing one to move one grant's reach edits a roster other grants read. The old expression (GROUP(kitchen) + LIST) - PERSON(a) - PERSON(b) becomes (GROUP(kitchen) + PERSON(honza)) - PERSON(a) - PERSON(b): same meaning, one fewer kind of thing, and the reason not to use a group is unchanged.

    2. What is genuinely given up, written as lost rather than as managed¶

    Four things go, and the first three are not recovered by anything in this decision.

    Per-person dates. audience_member carried valid_from / valid_to with the half-open no-overlap rule; audience_operand carries no period and gains none. You ruled no: a person is in the audience or not, and a June-to-August exception is its own grant with its own period, which is what D-099's shape is for. The cost is exact: where there was one audience and one grant there are now two grants, and a reviewer asking "what does Honza have?" must find both. A grant aimed at one person is no longer a curiosity, it is the ordinary expression of a temporary exception, which is worth saying because for ten rounds the model's answer to that was a row in a side table.

    One precondition on that equivalence, because it is not free in every shape. A grant carries a scope and a permission as well as a period, so the second grant restates the dated name only where the first grant's scope and permission are the ones the exception needs. "These three may see the answers until August" beside an organization-wide grant restates cleanly; the same sentence meant for one camp does not, because the second grant must be aimed at that camp, and is then a different reach in two dimensions at once rather than a dated copy of the first. That is not an argument for keeping the dates — the row that carried them could not express the scope split either, it only hid it inside a list — but "the language can still state the same facts" is true per scope and permission, and printing it unqualified would be exactly the kind of sentence this log has spent two rounds catching itself write.

    Per-name provenance. added_by named who put one person on one list. What remains is created_by on a node, which — because saving an expression rewrites its rows — answers "who last saved this tree" and not "who added Honza". The act is in audit_log with its actor taken from the request context, as it is for every other write; that is a real record and a weaker one, and the difference shows up only when two people have edited the same audience.

    The list's past. With the table and its dates gone, "who was on this audience in 2026" is a recomputation over audit payloads, and those payloads expire after 24 months (D-088). So the question stops being answerable in 2028. This is not new exposure of a kind the model had avoided: D-091 already turned "who could read this in 2023" into a recomputation, and F-17 already states it for the nested kind of audience. It is the same hole, one table wider, and it is accepted rather than papered over.

    Two writers at once. Adding a name was an insert; two clerks adding two people to one audience could not collide. Adding a name is now an edit of the audience's expression, so they can: whoever implements the editor owes this an optimistic-concurrency check or a queue, and a silent lost update here quietly removes somebody's access. Nothing in the schema will notice. It is stated here rather than in the rules because the table whose absence caused it no longer exists to carry a note.

    One thing is lost that does not cost anything today, and is listed so it is not re-derived as a bug in two years: there was a moment when the list could have had its own permission — may tick names on this audience without may rewrite the rule. It never got one. Permission-catalog §5a wrote audience.manage as one code covering "change its expression, or the LIST a LIST leaf resolves to", so the split option existed in the data model and not in the authorization model, and this decision closes the option in the data model too. If a real need for a names-only write ever appears, it is a new question about the editor, not an argument that this decision was wrong.

    3. The depth bound is removed, and the sentence it was holding up¶

    selector_depth_limit is deleted, along with the stored-column argument that justified its shape (D-048 is the precedent this design cites for it — a bound somebody may want to change belongs in a column, because a job containing the literal 3 has turned a parameter back into a constant). The precedent outlives the column: there is no parameter left to place. The tree is stored exactly as the writer wrote it: one row of two operands and one operator per node, left-to-right, no rebalancing — which matters because rebalancing was the tempting engineering answer here, and a rebalanced tree is a different expression than the one a person wrote, which is the reason the storage exists at all.

    One phrase above needs separating from a neighbouring ruling, because the two read as one sentence and are not. "Stored as the writer wrote it" is a claim about the tree's shape: no rebalancing, no reassociation, no reordering — the structure is the structure the writer made. It is not a claim that the stored string is what was typed: D-101 rules that the canonical rendering brackets every bracket and that the editor rewrites typed input into that form before saving, so what is stored is a canonical rendering of the writer's tree, and its shape is still the writer's. The distinction has one customer, and it is the one that matters here: adding brackets changes no expression, while reassociating one — or sorting children into a canonical order to compare two audiences for equality, which F-14 floats — changes exactly the thing this paragraph refuses to change. This entry therefore does not settle F-14; it draws the line F-14 has to be decided on one side of, and says so rather than letting "as written" be read as an answer to a question it was not asked.

    What has to be said about the consequence, because it is the real cost of this ruling and not a footnote: for the whole life of this design, one property of authorization checks was that they cost a bounded amount. That property came from here. With no bound on height and no bound ever asserted on breadth, the size of an audience's expansion is now bounded only by acyclicity, and:

    • The save-time walks change role. The cycle walk — inside the tree, and across audiences through every AUDIENCE(x) leaf, which is the one D-103 made necessary — survives and becomes the only structural guard there is. It must not be relaxed alongside the bound; an expansion that never terminates is the failure the removed bound was never protecting against anyway.
    • The read path still gets no depth cap and no cycle check. That refusal was argued as "the cap becomes the thing that decides who a grant reaches", and it stands — but it used to be a back-up for a guard that existed at save time. Now it is the whole design, so the sentence "a check costs a bounded amount" is deleted from the properties of this system, and what replaces it is the standing report of the widest and deepest audiences and their per-check cost, ~~plus the operand cache~~. One of those two legs was removed the round after this one: D-114 deleted the cached answers and the operand cache was keyed per person — (person, selector_kind, selector_arg_id[, item]) — so it went with them, and what is left on the read path is a compilation cache, which removes parsing and name resolution from a check but not the reads, and a memo that dies with the request. So this bullet's arithmetic got worse by one control while its prose kept listing two, and the correction is recorded here rather than left for a reader to notice: the report is now the only thing between an unbounded expression and a slow screen. A report, with no CI behind it — a convention, not a guard, in D-081's words, and it is the third control of this shape the authorization module now depends on. Round 14 is the round that made it carry more.
    • The invariant "depth must be refused at save, over the expanded tree" retires as an invariant and survives as the report above. Retiring it is D-081's case exactly: the rule's name outlives the mechanism, and the text must say which of the two happened.
    • The existing sentence that breaks is the one claiming the number of reads per check is "bounded by rule (the same selector_depth_limit) and known at save time". It becomes: one indexed read per audience in the closure, closure size discovered by walking it at save, not bounded by a number.
    • Canonical form still prints every bracket — that ruling stands, and its cost is now visible: a twenty-name hand-typed sum prints as nineteen nested pairs of brackets. Storage and display both say what was written, which is what was asked for. If that turns out unreadable, the answer is that a set of people big enough to be unreadable is a person_group, and F-5's neighbourhood is where that judgement gets registered — not a flattening rule invented in advance.

    4. The thing this change makes load-bearing, which no page required before¶

    PERSON(x) leaves used to be a curiosity: the hand-typed people lived in audience_member, whose person_id is an ordinary foreign key. Now every hand-named person in the system is a selector_arg_id, and that column deliberately has no foreign key (D-011's accepted trade-off, backstopped by an orphan scan rather than by the database).

    So a person who is merged away leaves leaves. A leaf that resolves to nobody is the empty set, which is quiet on the allow side and loud on the deny side, and the docs' own worked example is a subtraction: EVERYONE - PERSON(x). Un-merged, x stops subtracting anybody and the grant denies everybody — the failure this module has been pointing at for two rounds, now reachable by an ordinary merge.

    Requirement, and it is new: a person merge and a redaction must repoint every audience_operand leaf whose selector_arg_id names the absorbed or erased person, in all five argument kinds (person, role, person_group, event, audience) — the merge reassignment table gains the row, the erasure path gains the scan, and the nightly orphan check becomes the backstop rather than the only mechanism. It belongs to F-10's register for the reason that register exists: the ORM cannot express it, because the column has no key to follow. Nothing in the corpus required this before, and it was defensible before only because the volume of hand-named people was in a table that had a key.

    And the same failure is reachable by typing, which is the ordinary path; the merge is the rare one. Before this round, naming a person by hand was an insert into a keyed table, and an insert could not name nobody — the database refused it. Now a PERSON(x) leaf is a value in a keyless column, so a mistyped, stale or half-deleted argument resolves to nobody on the day it is saved, and on the - side of an expression that means the grant is wider than its author wrote, silently. The orphan scan does not catch this one, because the person row it would look for exists; the id is simply not the one anybody meant. What closes it is the preview D-091 already requires and nothing else: the editor shows the resolved people before the save, and a subtraction that subtracts nobody is visible on that screen as a subtraction that subtracts nobody. No new screen and no new check are added here — the point is that the preview, bought as a control over reach, is now also the only control over whether a hand-typed name resolves, and whoever builds the editor should know that a preview they may be tempted to make lazy, cached or skippable is carrying a second duty it was never argued for. F-15 is where that tension is registered, and this entry names it rather than resolving it.

    5. What got simpler, counted¶

    • Twelve registry rows, and every one of them is a rule about people or takes an argument — the operand with neither is gone, so the registry has no special case left to explain.
    • The four-way comparison GROUP(x) / AUDIENCE(x) / LIST / PERSON(x) is a three-way one, and the third and fourth are now "an office's holders, a group's roster, an audience's expression" with no fourth shape.
    • audience_member's rules go with it: the no-overlap rule, the refusal to add a person to an is_system audience, the editor affordance that rendered LIST (this audience), and one entry in the cache-invalidation trigger list.
    • §13's open question about renaming LIST to ADDED is moot, not answered, and is deleted rather than closed.
    • D-091's list arm — the last of its three arms still standing — is closed outright.
    • One of the two dated-member tables the design apologises for is gone, so the kept-duplication argument D-080 would raise has one fewer instance to explain.

    Migration, and where it bites: no seeded audience and no import used LIST, so for a fresh database this is a table drop and one fewer seed row. Any existing audience whose expression names LIST has to be rewritten by hand, and rewriting an expression changes who a grant reaches — so the migration prints the resolved set before and after for every audience it touches, which is the same report D-104 adopted as its remedy, run once, on the rows this migration puts at risk.

    D-106 — A failed operand stays the empty set; the missing half was a report, not a refusal¶

    Status: decided (from your answer) Date: refinement round 12 Amends: the failure semantics described in D-101 and written out in data-model/05 §8

    Decision. Nothing about resolution changes. An operand whose argument names nothing — a deleted person, a renamed role, a merged-away id — is the empty set, and EVERYONE - PERSON(x) with a dead x therefore still denies everybody. What is added is not a rule but a page: an audience with a leaf that does not resolve is listed, for the audience's owner, on the same report F-21 already asked for, in a second column. No save is refused because a leaf is broken. No expression is pinned. The failure stays fail-closed and stops being silent.

    Why. Your answer, in full: "It still feels unnecessary, it is enough to notify, do not deny (it might cause very advanced problems more, than it gains to be loud)."

    Two fixes were on the table and both are declined here, so that neither is proposed again.

    1. "Treat an unresolvable id as the named one" — resolve PERSON(x) to the person by name when the id is gone. Declined: it invents a person the model does not have, and it invents them on the widening side, which is the one place a wrong answer grants instead of withholds.
    2. "Never wider than saved" — at save time, record the resolved set and refuse any later resolution that reaches more people than the saved one. Declined, and it is worth seeing why: it makes the stored expression a ceiling on truth, so a person who leaves a unit cannot be removed from what a grant means without a write, and a grant silently freezes at the shape it had on the day it was saved. It buys a guarantee nobody asked for by breaking the one property an expression has over a snapshot — that it means today what it says today.

    The vocabulary is retired, and this is a D-081 case. The corpus has been saying "the - side is loud", which names a volume where there is only a direction. What is true: a failed operand on the + or * side narrows the set, and a failed operand on the - side widens it. - is not noisy; it is the only operator whose failed operand can grant. Everywhere the corpus says "loud" it should say that, and everywhere it says "fail closed" it should be clear that closed is a direction, not a safe state — EVERYONE - PERSON(x) failing is fail-closed and lockout-everybody, in one step.

    The line this entry draws, which is narrower than it looks. D-106 is about an operand that cannot be resolved at all, because its argument names nothing. It is not about a person who lacks a value. A person with no birth date is not a failed OLDER(18) — the operand resolves perfectly well, to the set of people who are 18 or older on the date being checked, and that person is simply not in it, exactly as a person with no membership row is not in MEMBERS. A missing attribute on the person being asked about is not a resolution failure and never reaches this entry, which is the distinction the report has to preserve or it will page about three thousand people with incomplete imports.

    What this costs, said plainly. A broken leaf on a - side denies people with no signal beyond a page nobody is required to read, and the report is a query with an owner, not a guard — in D-081's phrase, a convention, not a guard. It was offered as one and it is written as one. The reason to accept that is in D-107, which records the premise that makes a notification enough: the people writing these expressions are the people who know what they say.

    See: domain/06 §6.4 for the rules that make the tree safe, data-model/05 §8 for where the empty-set rule is stated, and F-15, which this does not answer — a report is not a preview and neither is free.

    D-107 — The grammar opens: an attribute may be an operand, and the vocabulary stays seeded¶

    Status: decided (from your answer) Date: refinement round 12 Amends: D-060, whose stated test for what an operand may be is deleted here; D-101's grammar; D-105's count

    Decision, in five clauses.

    1. The tree grows attribute leaves. Two seeded operands arrive — OLDER(n) and YOUNGER(n) — read against the person's birth date as of the date the check is made. The registry has fourteen rows — thirteen as this entry is being re-read, GUARDIANS having left in D-113; the number stays printed here because growing the table by two is the whole content of this clause, and it is the only measurement in it.
    2. The operators do not change at all. + - * are the only operators this language has, and OLDER/YOUNGER are just two more operands composed by them — MEMBERS * OLDER(13) * YOUNGER(19), a band, is the same shape as MEMBERS * GROUP(cubs) * PERSON(a). There is no comparison operator: no >=, no <, no bare AGE. The boolean spellings &, | and ! were put to this language and declined; §Why the operators stayed three is the record of that, since this entry was first written granting them.
    3. D-060 survives; its sentence does not. See below — this is the load-bearing clause.
    4. A selector's membership may change with no write, by ruling. Recorded in §Time below.
    5. The premise is written down. Recorded in §Authors below, with the two things it does not buy.

    Why the gate opened, in the words that opened it. Asked whether the boolean grammar could be given attribute operands, the answer came back as a question first: "Can the grammar still work with the sets? OLDER(18) is still a set of people." It is, and that question is the whole decision, because the corpus's argument against admitting age was never about age:

    "an operand must be a named, materializable set of people. An attribute is not a set of people. Guardianship is (GUARDIANS(x) is a set), age is not, and the line falls exactly where D-060 already drew it." — domain/06 §6, until this round

    That sentence is wrong and it is deleted. OLDER(18) denotes the set of people who are eighteen or older on the date being checked. MEMBERS denotes the set of people who pass is_current_member. GUARDIANS(x) denotes the set of people who hold a row. Every operand in the registry is a test with a set on the other side of it; a birth-date comparison is not a different kind of thing, it is a test that reads one column instead of joining one table. The materializability test was therefore never what kept rule engines out — MEMBERS would have failed it too, since nothing stores the member list.

    What was doing the work, and it is kept. An operand must be named. Not "must be a set" — must be a row. OLDER(18) is in the registry; AGE >= 18 is not, and cannot be, because the language has no place to put >=. The door D-060 was guarding is now shut by the clause that actually fits it: a writer may compose seeded operands and nothing else. D-060's decision — no eligibility rule table, no prerequisite system, no rule evaluated outside can() — is untouched, and its **Decision.** paragraph is amended rather than replaced, because "no age gate" was its plainest consequence and is now false in the plain sense and true in the real one: there is still no gate the system applies on its own; there is a vocabulary a person may use to write one.

    The precedent that made this cheap is already in the corpus, and it is the reason no new storage appeared. data-model/01-identity.md opens by refusing to store age at all: "There is no is_member, member_type, is_child, is_adult…" — because a stored age flag is a fact that goes false at midnight, and this model's rule about facts that go false is to keep the input (the birth date) and compute the rest. Round 12 did not change that rule; it used it. OLDER(n) is where a computed fact is allowed to live in this design — in the resolver, evaluated on the checked date, never in a column — which is also why adding it cost two seed rows and no table, and why the four-decade-old instinct to add an is_adult column remains refused.

    Why the operators stayed three. The boolean spellings were put to this language — &, |, ! beside + - * — and the answer came back first as the observation that settled the whole round: "OLDER(18) is still a set of people." It is, and once that is seen, boolean notation has no work left to do. p ∈ (A ∩ B) ∪ C is by definition (p ∈ A ∧ p ∈ B) ∨ p ∈ C, and with EVERYONE in the language !X is EVERYONE - X. So & | ! would be a second spelling of three operators the language already has, and a second spelling is not free even when it is pure sugar: the canonical rendering has to pick one, the editor has to accept both, every write-path check has to reason over both, and two people's identical rules stop being textually comparable — which is the property the canonical rendering exists to buy, and the reason F-22 exists at all. The case for them was genuine: a person writing an eligibility rule is thinking in conditions, not in algebra. But that is an editor's case, and it is answered in the editor — what a writer types is a rendering, what is stored is a set expression, and if the editor ever wants to accept ! it can expand it into EVERYONE - on the way in and never store that anything happened. Declined, then, and recorded as declined rather than left absent, because this entry granted them twenty minutes earlier and an entry that reverses itself inside its own round should show that it did.

    One argument this round made and dropped, and why it is on the record anyway. The case for a closed vocabulary was made in governance terms: volunteers will write FEE_PAID into an audience and nobody will notice until it is wrong. That argument is not answered here, it is overruled — see §Authors — and it is left in this entry because it was aimed at the wrong half of the design. A row per operand, shipped with its evaluator arm so that a code with no arm behind it is a deployment bug rather than a held feature (D-104), costs a trusted author nothing; what would have cost them something is a language that refuses to grow, and this one does not. The shape that survived both rulings is worth naming, because the two rulings pull in opposite directions: the vocabulary is open, the grammar is closed. New tests arrive as named rows a person may compose with the three operators they already know; the language never grows a place to put a comparison. That is the whole difference between a vocabulary that grows and a parser that accepts anything, and it is a difference in where the decision is made, not in how much freedom anybody has.

    Time, and what accepting it costs. Your ruling, in full: "I know, that the time will change, how the rules resolve and IT IS FINE." Recorded as accepted rather than as an open risk, with its mechanics stated so that nobody rediscovers them as a bug:

    • A derived set can gain or lose a member with no write at all, at midnight, one person at a time. This is the first exception to F-6's model in which invalidation is caused by a write; the answer is not a new invalidation path but the observation that a cached decision is already keyed to a date and already expires.
    • Nothing is stored, so there is nothing to invalidate. No table changes when a person turns eighteen; there is no row to expire and no cache of membership to keep. This is the property that age bands would not have had, which is why they are not in this design.
    • What it buys is history. can(p, view, doc, on 2024-05-01, about q) now has a right answer for an age-bearing rule, computed from a birth date and a date, which is a question no snapshot design could answer and which the audit trail will ask.

    The honest residue is not performance, it is the record. A check for yesterday answers for yesterday, but the decision taken yesterday — "this grant reaches these people", printed by the preview and written into audit at the moment of the write (D-104) — was a snapshot of a set that has since moved. That is not a cache problem and it is not raised again as one; it is carried as F-23.

    Authors: the premise, and the two things it does not buy. Your standing answer, quoted because several entries in this round argue against something it already settled: "The people writing the rules WILL know, what they are doing. I know, that the time will change, how the rules resolve and IT IS FINE." Until now this was implied by D-091's preview — the entry that requires it — and by the absence of anything like a lint; it is now a premise, and it has three consequences and two limits.

    • The preview is a workbench, not a guard. It stays required — but the corpus's justification for it ("the writer may not know whom they are reaching") is replaced with "the writer can ask, and the answer is printed and logged". Same requirement, different reason, and the difference matters to whoever is tempted to make it a confirmation dialog.
    • No new save-time refusal anywhere in this round, and none is implied by it. The only refusals left in the write path are structural (a cycle, a dangling parent_id, an argument of the wrong kind).
    • A report is offered rather than a block, which is exactly the shape D-106 chose.
    • It does not buy a different audience. Competence in the author says nothing about the people the rule reaches. The fail-closed order, guardianship's allows-only ceiling, and the unreachable dependent.* namespace are untouched by this premise, because those protect people who did not write the rule. This is the boundary of the premise and it is drawn here rather than discovered later.
    • It does not buy an unlogged write. Every refusal this round declined was a refusal to stop a save; none of them was a refusal to record one. The audit trail is where the premise stops being a courtesy — see D-088 for what it keeps and for how long it keeps it.

    The boundary of each operand, fixed here because nothing else fixed it. OLDER(n) is n years or older on the checked date; YOUNGER(n) is strictly under n. The pair therefore partitions everyone who has a birth date, with no overlap and no gap, and YOUNGER(15) means what "under 15" means in ordinary speech — a fifteen-year-old is outside it, which is load-bearing for EV-1's ceiling example MEMBERS * YOUNGER(15) and for every camp form that will ever print this as an age limit. Both readings were already implied in domain/06 §6.1 and in the registry rows; they are written here because an inclusive-or-exclusive off-by-one in a ceiling is a safeguarding bug with a plausible implementation behind it, and because a corpus that leaves a boundary to inference will implement it twice.

    A worked example this entry shipped was vacuous, and it is worth keeping as an example of its own. The first draft illustrated the two operands with MEMBERS * OLDER(18) - YOUNGER(6). Nobody is both eighteen and under six, so the subtraction excluded nobody and the expression meant MEMBERS * OLDER(18); a subagent reading the registry rows noticed it while doing unrelated propagation, which is the only reason it was caught. It matters beyond tidiness because the example was being cited as the reason both operands exist — and an example that needs neither operand argues for neither. The band that genuinely requires both is MEMBERS * OLDER(13) * YOUNGER(19), which is thirteen-to-eighteen and which excludes people at both ends. The lesson is the corpus's own, turned on me: an example is an assertion, and it gets checked against the registry like anything else (D-104); the person writing the prose is the worst place to check it, including when that person is the one who asked for the feature.

    Refusals become decisions, and both of them keep their answers. PRESENT(event) and WHITELISTED were refused as kind — "not materializable", "no stored state" — and with attribute operands admitted, that class of argument is gone: a predicate over a column is now an ordinary operand, so nothing can be excluded by what it is. Both stay out, on their own merits, which were always the real reason:

    • PRESENT(event) — still not seeded. A tick box on a check-in screen would become the thing that revokes a leader's access to their own camp when somebody forgets to tick it. That is an argument about consequences and it survives intact; what is withdrawn is the claim that the language could not express it.
    • WHITELISTED — still not seeded, because there is no state to evaluate (F-20), and an operand that reads nothing is a word with nothing behind it — a row whose evaluator would answer the same for every person on every date, which is not what a registry row is for. F-20 stays open as a question about whether the feature is wanted, and is no longer a question about whether the type system forbids it.

    Storage, and the one invariant this could have broken. OLDER's argument is an integer, which makes it the first operand argument in the registry that is not an entity reference. The registry still names exactly two reference-less polymorphic keys — access_grant.scope_id and audience_operand.selector_arg_id — because a scalar stored in selector_arg_id is not a key pointing at anything: nothing to repoint on a merge, nothing for a cascade to find, nothing for F-10's orphan check to walk. §11's check that the count is two stays true, and stays true for the right reason rather than by luck: the check is about references, and 18 is not one. argument_type therefore gains a scalar domain, and the pages that enumerated the argument kinds as though the list were complete now say what it is: six argument_type values, five of which are tables. The merge and orphan scans still walk exactly the five that name something, and that is the sentence those pages should always have carried — the count this corpus keeps getting wrong is the count of things to scan, not the count of values a column may hold.

    See: domain/06 §6 and §6.1 for the language and the corrected argument, data-model/05 §8 for the fourteen-row registry, permission-catalog §1 for the leaf list, F-5, which closes on its age half, and EV-1, which stays closed for a different reason than it was.

    D-108 — There is no SCHOOL operand: a selector may only name a fact the model stores¶

    Status: decided (from your answer) Date: refinement round 12

    Decision. No SCHOOL(x) operand, no school column on person, no school-shaped selector of any kind. Asked whether school should enter the selector language, the answer was that it is not an important selector — at least not for now — and this entry records that as a refusal with a reason, rather than as an oversight.

    The rule it instantiates, which is the useful part. A selector names a set of people the model can compute from data it holds. OLDER(n) passes that test because a birth date is stored; MEMBERS because a membership period is stored; GROUP(x) because the roster is stored. A school affiliation is not stored anywhere in this system, and D-092 put it out of scope on purpose: the corpus is a people export and an event export, and it carries no school. So the refusal is not about schools — it is that an operand over an absent column would be a selector whose answer is always the empty set, which is the one failure mode this module has decided, repeatedly, to make impossible rather than handle gracefully.

    What to use instead, which works today. A person_group per school, filled by whoever knows — by hand, or by the import if it ever carries the fact. GROUP(zszos_2) is a selector now, it takes an argument, and MEMBERS * GROUP(zszos_2) is the school-bounded audience. The difference is not a limitation: a group is a set somebody is responsible for, and a school cohort in a Scout organization is exactly that kind of thing — known by a leader, not derivable from an attribute. A derived operand would have quietly asserted that school membership is a fact the system owns.

    What would reopen it, and this is the "for now" written as a trigger rather than a date. Either of these:

    • the import starts carrying a school column for a meaningful part of the people — at which point school is stored, SCHOOL(x) becomes computable, and the operand is one decision entry and one seed row (F-5's cost model for a new operand, which has now been paid once and came to two rows and a reserved word);
    • a fee, a subsidy or an eligibility rule turns out to depend on school — at which point the question is no longer about a selector at all, because the dependency is a fact with consequences outside authorization, and it belongs in the person model first.

    Worth saying about how this entry came to exist. It was prompted by a finding this round filed against another page and then retracted: the "all_in_school audience the prose names but the registry cannot express" was never in the corpus (F-5 carries that retraction, with the grep that kills it). The invented defect asked one true question, and this entry is the answer to it, which is the best outcome available from a fabricated citation and the reason the retraction is kept in print rather than edited out.

    See: D-107 for what an operand is allowed to be, D-092 for what the corpus actually contains, and data-model/02 §3 for person_group as a set somebody is answerable for.
