    D-109 — Nothing a rule names is deleted: deprecated, hidden, disabled — and a tab instead of a report¶

    Status: decided (from your answer) Date: refinement round 13 Supersedes: the report D-106 added, the merge-repoint duty D-105 recorded, and part of F-21

    Decision. Anything a stored expression can name — a selector_type row, a role, a person_group, an audience — is never deleted while a rule may name it. It is given a state instead. Three states, and they must stay three different things or one of them will silently become the other:

    State Does it still resolve? Is it offered when writing a rule? Is it flagged?
    deprecated yes, unchanged no — it cannot be added to a new rule, and editing a rule cannot introduce it yes, highlighted in the editor wherever it appears
    hidden yes, unchanged no no — it is not obsolete, it is not everybody's business
    disabled no — the empty set no yes, and this is the only state that changes an answer

    No standing report. There is a tab on the permissions page listing every live rule whose expression names a deprecated, hidden or disabled thing, or fails to resolve at all.

    Why. Your answer, in full: "There should be no deleted selectors, only deprecated/hidden/disabled. In that case, the original resource is still referenced and applied, BUT it cannot be added to new or edited rules AND it is highlighted in the rule editor. No real report is needed, there can be a tab on the permissions page listing all the 'broken' permissions."

    This deletes the reason the report existed, which is the elegant part of it. D-106 added a report to catch an unresolvable operand; F-21 asked who would read the report of the widest expansions and could not name anybody. Both are answers about a failure whose cause is now removed: a leaf broke because the thing it named was deleted, and here nothing is deleted. F-21's reader question resolves to nobody, because there is nothing to read — and what replaces it is better than a report for the ordinary reason that a report has to be remembered and a tab is where the person already is. The tab is not a control either, in D-081's sense; it is a control that has a decent chance of being seen, which is all the difference between a convention and a decoration.

    Two duties this withdraws, and one it leaves standing. Round 11 recorded that a person merge or a redaction must repoint every audience_operand leaf, because a leaf naming nobody is the empty set and on a - side that widens. For the resources in the table above that duty is gone — retiring a group is now one write to the group, not N writes to N expressions, and the expressions keep meaning what they meant until somebody edits them. It survives for exactly one argument kind, and that is the collision this entry has to name: PERSON(x) names a person, and D-088 lets a redaction require the row to actually go. Erasure wins. A person is not a selector, and no convenience owed to an expression author survives a former member's right to be deleted. So the corpus now has exactly one remaining way to produce a broken leaf — a redacted person — and D-106's semantics cover it unchanged: the empty set, narrowing an allow and widening a deny, now visible in the tab rather than in a report nobody opens. That is a much smaller domain than D-106 assumed, and stating it that way is the point.

    What disabled costs, said plainly, because it is the loudest switch in this system. Disabling a group, an office or an audience stops every rule that names it, in every scope, at once, and re-enabling it restores all of them — a widening in which nobody edited a grant, so the audit trail would show one flag write and a hundred permissions appear if it were logged as anything less. Therefore: disabling and re-enabling are audited writes that print the rules they touch, resolved before and after, which is the same report D-104 and D-105 adopted for their own migrations; and the tab lists the rules currently dead for this reason, so the state is reversible knowingly. disabled is also the answer to a question this corpus kept meeting — "how do I turn off that whole naming without editing forty grants?" — which until now had no answer except a deny per grant.

    The invariant that keeps deprecated from drifting into disabled: deprecating or hiding what an expression names must not change the set the expression resolves to. That is check-worthy, it is cheap, and it is the precise failure this whole design is meant to make impossible — a rule that quietly stops granting because somebody tidied a list. The editor's reserved-name rule (D-101) keeps a deprecated or disabled name reserved, so nothing new can quietly claim the word and split its meaning in two.

    See: D-106 (whose semantics now apply to redacted people only), D-105 (the repoint duty, halved), D-088 (why erasure wins), and F-21, closed as "no report; a tab; nothing bounds a check, deliberately".

    D-110 — The expression is the record of whom a grant reached¶

    Status: decided (from your answer) Date: refinement round 13 Closes: F-23, and the reconstructable-past half of F-17

    Decision. The audit record of a grant's reach is the expression. The preview still prints a resolved set before a save (D-091) and that printing is logged as a rendering on a date, not as a statement of who was in it. "Whom did this grant reach in May?" is answered, if at all, by evaluating the words against the data of May — and these documents do not promise the answer.

    Why. Your answer, four words: "Words only — the expression is the record." The alternative was to keep the preview's list beside the audit row, and that list is the roster table this corpus has deleted twice (D-105, and assumption 58's history of it). It would also store, indefinitely and against D-088's window, a per-person record of people who were never granted anything — a list of everyone a rule happened to cover, kept after the rule was withdrawn. Storing that to make a historical question comfortable is the trade this corpus has refused every time it has been offered.

    Why words-only is cheap now rather than brave. It was nearly untenable an hour ago and it is sound now, because of D-109: if nothing a rule names is deleted, the words stay evaluable long after their reason is gone. A role that was retired still resolves; a group that was deprecated still resolves; a name stays reserved. The two rulings are one design — refuse to delete the nouns, and then keeping only the words is not an amputation. Without D-109, this answer would mean "the past is usually unanswerable"; with it, the past is usually computable, and what is left is honestly bounded.

    What is conceded, named so nobody finds it later. Editing a shared audience changes what every grant that names it has meant all along, because those grants hold a word, not a copy — and a re-evaluation for a past date uses today's inner expression. An edit to a shared audience is therefore a retrospective edit to every rule that names it. That is not new (D-103 accepted it as the price of reuse) but until now it was only implied; here it is the reason a past-date answer can be wrong rather than missing, and the control is D-103's existing one: the editor prints the resolved set before an edit to a shared expression saves, so the person editing sees, at least once, the thing they are changing backwards in time. What is not kept is a history of inner expressions; the audit trail records the edit, not the meaning that existed before it.

    A consequence worth holding onto: the audit trail gets smaller and more honest. A row says this person wrote these words on this date about this item, which is a fact that cannot go stale, rather than this grant reached these 214 people, which starts decaying the night somebody's birthday falls.

    See: D-107 for why a set now moves on its own, D-109 for why the words keep resolving, and F-17, whose premise about materializing this removes.

    D-111 — The permission cache is rebuilt nightly, on any ruleset change, and on request¶

    Status: decided (from your answer) Date: refinement round 13 Closes: F-6, and F-7 by absorption Superseded in part by D-114: the cache is deleted, so there is nothing to rebuild. What survives of this entry is the integer — ruleset_version, now keying a compiled expression instead of a cached answer, with its bump set cut from the eight kinds of write listed below — six tables, this entry's own D-109 flag and a merge — to audience_operand and selector_type. What went is the object, the schedule and both invalidation lists. Reading this entry as wrong would be the mistake it does not deserve: it removed a TTL question by removing the need for a TTL, which was the right move against the problem it could see, and what D-114 found is that the rebuild had quietly inherited the staleness window the TTL was for — the answer moved on a schedule instead of on a clock, and a schedule is a harder place to notice that than a column. Its "the safeguarding case is immediate" argument is true now by construction rather than by trigger, which is the best outcome a superseded ruling can have.

    Decision. There is one cache policy and three triggers. Every cached authorization answer is rebuilt (a) nightly, in full; (b) immediately on any write to the ruleset; (c) on explicit request — an administrative action, which belongs on the same permissions page as the tab. No TTL per entry, no asymmetric TTL, no partial invalidation list to get right.

    Why. Your answer, verbatim: "All cache is represhed at night every day OR on manual request and ruleset change." F-6 had spent four rounds on which write invalidates how wide a fan-out, and on an asymmetric TTL that would make a deny visible sooner than an allow. This replaces the whole invalidation table with a rebuild and a rule about what counts as a ruleset change, which is a smaller thing to be right about — and the fan-out question was only hard because per-entry invalidation was assumed.

    What counts as the ruleset, because the boundary is now the whole design: a write to access_grant, audience_operand, guardianship_permission, or permission; to role_holder and person_group_member; to the deprecated | hidden | disabled flag of anything in D-109's table (that flag must be a ruleset write — if it is not, "turn off this naming" waits until midnight, which is exactly the failure the flag exists to prevent); and a person merge or unmerge. An answer that moves without one of these is allowed to be up to one night stale: a birthday crossing OLDER(n)/YOUNGER(n), the 1 January membership sweep, a role or group period that ends by its own date, attendance writes behind the event tiers.

    That residual window is the ruling, and it is the same ruling as D-107's. A withdrawn permission keeps working until the nightly rebuild only if the withdrawal was not a write — and every withdrawal a person performs is a write: revoke, add a deny, close a grant's period, end a guardianship, end a role assignment. What waits for the night is what nobody does: an expiry arriving on its own, an age arriving on its own. The safeguarding case is immediate; the calendar case waits at most one refresh, and the refresh is what fixes it. This is D-107's "I know, that the time will change, how the rules resolve and IT IS FINE" with the mechanism attached — until now the sentence described a behaviour, and now it describes a policy.

    What "immediately" means when you cannot enumerate the affected people — the mechanism, added the same round because the ruling was incomplete without it. Found while propagating this entry: the cache key is (person_id, permission_code, scope_type, scope_id, at_bucket), and an audience appears in it nowhere. So the writes that need global invalidation are exactly the ones that name nobody. Ending one role_holder row names its person; ending one person_group_member row names its person; an edit to a shared audience_operand tree names nobody at all — the people it stops reaching are whatever the expression happens to resolve to, and finding them means evaluating it for everyone, which is the job, not a way around it. Per-entry invalidation had no answer to that, which is why F-6's table spent four rounds pretending the fan-out was tractable. The answer is a ruleset version: one integer, bumped by any write in the ruleset set above, carried in every cache entry, and an entry is valid only while its version matches. "Rebuild immediately on a ruleset change" then means bump the version, which is one write to one row and never needs to know whom a rule reaches; the nightly rebuild stays as the correctness backstop for everything the version does not cover (the calendar cases); and the manual request becomes "bump it and warm it". This is also what makes the deprecated | hidden | disabled flag safe in D-109's sense: flipping disabled on a group is a ruleset write, so it bumps the version, so no cached true survives it — and if the flag ever stops bumping, D-109's "deprecating or hiding must not change the answer" invariant still holds while "disabling takes effect now" silently does not, which is a failure worth having a check for. What the version does not buy: it is not a per-audience version and not a version per grant. A system large enough to want that is the system D-112 says this one is not, and the trigger there is what reopens this.

    What F-7 was, and why it is answered rather than decided. F-7 asked whether to keep a cache or materialize an effective_permission table. A nightly full rebuild of cached answers and a nightly materialized effective_permission table are the same job with two names. There is now one job; the question was not resolved between two designs, it was dissolved by the design being chosen for a different reason — which is worth recording as such, because a dissolved question is the kind that comes back if nobody writes down that it dissolved.

    The constraint this does not lift. The rebuild may evaluate expressions per person — that is what a cache warm is, and at the size in D-112 it is cheap. What stays forbidden is the failure domain/06 names as the one way to get this wrong: inside a check, an expression is compiled to a predicate on the person being asked, never materialized as a set — otherwise the nightly job is correct and every request still materializes the whole set instead of testing the one person in front of it. That clause used to end "…and every request still costs three thousand rows," and the number is gone on purpose: it was an estimate of the whole grant table, written three rounds before D-112 stated a size and said plainly that nothing in these documents had measured anything. It is in the same order of magnitude as that size, so nothing computed here changes — but a load-bearing number in a decision entry has to be one somebody counted, and this one was not.

    See: F-6 and its invalidation table, which this replaces, and data-model/05 §10's cache section.

    D-112 — The system's scale is stated: hundreds of people, tens of registrants per event¶

    Status: decided (from your answer) Date: refinement round 13 Closes: F-15, F-16, and the cost half of F-21

    Decision. The system is specified against a stated size, and the size is now written down instead of being assumed differently by every page that reasons about cost: a few hundred people in the organization, a few dozen registrants on a typical event, a big camp on the order of two hundred. Every "is this cheap enough?" question this project has carried — the per-item preview, listing the grants that name an audience, an unbounded audience expansion, a nightly full rebuild — resolves at that size, and resolves only at that size.

    Why. The pair chosen was "Hundreds of people, tens per event" — asked because two questions had stayed open for three rounds for the reason that nobody had stated a number, and a cost argument without a number is not an argument, it is a mood. Nothing in these documents measured this. No page carried a headcount before this entry, including the import pages that describe the legacy export row by row — so the number is stated, not observed, and it is the answer to a question rather than a finding. That is the whole reason the trigger below exists: a stated size is a load-bearing assumption with no evidence under it, which is exactly the shape this corpus has repeatedly had to come back and pin down.

    What it closes. F-15: a preview that resolves an audience for one item is, at this size, evaluating a couple of hundred predicates — nobody will feel it, so the preview stays per-item and lazy (assumption 60) with no cap and no background job. F-16: listing the grants that name an audience is a scan over a small table with a handful of referencing rows each; pagination is a UI nicety, not a requirement, and D-103's assumption that listing is cheap is true, now on stated grounds. F-21's cost half: nothing bounds what an expression can cost, and at this size that is accepted rather than merely unaddressed. ~~D-111's nightly rebuild: people × 66 permission codes × a small number of items is a bounded, short job — "rebuild everything nightly" is affordable here specifically, and would be a different decision at ten thousand people, which is the honest reason it was chosen.~~ This half of the entry's affordability arithmetic was overtaken by D-114, which deleted the job whose cost was being estimated — so the answer to "is the rebuild affordable at this size?" is that there is no rebuild, and this entry's other four answers (preview, listing, expression cost, F-16) are the parts that stand. The method here is what matters and it is unchanged: a stated size is what makes a cost claim checkable, and a claim that stops applying says so.

    The trigger, since a stated assumption without a trigger is just a guess with more confidence. If the population reaches a few thousand people, or a thousand registrants on one event, reopen F-15, F-16, F-7 and F-21 — in that order, because the preview is the first thing a user will feel and ~~the nightly rebuild the last~~ the nightly rebuild was last in that list and is now deleted; what sits in its place is nothing, so the order below it is unchanged and one shorter. Nothing in the design depends on the number, on purpose: the two rules that must survive any growth are the ones already written — a check compiles its expression to a predicate and never materializes the set, and a listing is paginated — so the system gets slow before it gets wrong, which is the only graceful failure this module can promise.

    See: F-15 and F-16, both closed against this entry, and D-111.

    D-113 — The guardianship channel has no expression: GUARDIANS is not a selector¶

    Status: decided (found while closing F-5) Date: refinement round 13 Closes: F-5's last live bullet, and with it the contradiction F-6 recorded about where a bound GUARDIANS could be written.

    How it was found, since both the method and the mistake in it are useful. F-5 had one live bullet left — does GUARDIANS bind to the item? — and answering it needed every claim the corpus makes about that operand, so a read-only sweep went out to collect them. What came back was a confident report that the registry has no GUARDIANS row at all, fourteen rows and none of them guardianship. That was false: the row is the sixth in data-model/05 §8's table, takes_argument optional, argument_type person, and round 12's count of fourteen was a correct count of a table that includes it. The report also named a file that does not exist and a membership kind that does not, so its claims became leads and nothing more. What survived checking was better than what was claimed.

    The real defect, which no count could have shown. The row's third column reads binds_to_item = "yes, person" — the one row in a table where that column means the item this grant sits on using it to mean a person, and its gloss promises a bound form: "Bound: the guardians of the person this item is about." That form cannot be written. F-6 found it independently while being closed: the save path refuses any bound operand on a unit-scope grant, and the only item scope that could host one is an event, which is the and the registry's own argument_type entry says the deciding sentence in passing — "person never was" a grant_scope_type — so a bound operand whose argument is a person can never satisfy the type match, at any scope, ever. The bound half of the row was illegal by the registry's own fields, while §12 was saying it may be "bound as GUARDIANS on a grant about that person". So the fourteenth row was a row whose advertised half no grant could hold, and the count agreed with itself the whole time.

    Which is the opposite lesson to the one this entry was going to record. It was drafted as "a count is a good alarm" — an uncounted name pointing at a defect. Here the count was not the alarm, it was the camouflage: fourteen was arithmetically true of a table containing a row that could not be used as described, and every page that repeated "fourteen" was reinforcing a fiction with arithmetic. A registry checked against itself is not a registry checked against the rules that write it; that is D-104's finding again, one layer up, and it is why the checks that matter here are on the save path rather than on the row count.

    Two things about this round's own conduct belong in the same section, because the entry is about method. The unsavability of the bound form was found twice, independently — once while closing F-6, once by an agent sweeping for this operand — which is the only reason I trusted it after this round's reports had already been wrong four times over. And this round wrote two false claims of its own while deleting the fiction: a quotation attributed to a page that does not contain it, and an invented role_holder "kind" whose enum I wrote out of nothing in a page I was editing to be tidy about D-100. The second was repaired by the agent that found it, in domain/04, and the correction says so on the page. The pattern across all six cases is one line: a confident claim with a line number is the most dangerous object in this repository, and the check is two seconds of grep. Which is worth more here than in most projects, because in a repo that is only documents, the grep is the test suite.

    Decision.

    1. The registry drops to thirteen: the GUARDIANS row is deleted, and nothing replaces it. Neither the bound form it advertised nor the GUARDIANS(p) form it actually supported is a selector. Fourteen was a true count of a table with a row that should not have been in it — clause 4 is the argument for deleting the usable half along with the unusable one, and it is also the part of this entry that changed after the entry was written.
    2. The guardianship channel contains no expression, and never did since D-074. A check asks whether a live guardianship row links the person asking to the person the action is about, and reads right_level ∈ none | view | write off it — a lookup over a relationship, not a set that something resolves. guardianship_permission is that value's history: guardianship_id, right_level, valid_from, valid_to, changed_reason, changed_by. It has no grantee, no permission code, no audience and no expression column, so there was never anywhere in it for a selector to be seeded.
    3. What the corpus did was describe a channel with a selector's syntax. The word GUARDIANS appeared in tables and prose across domain/06, glossary, data-model/05, data-model/08-events, domain/07-events, appendix/erd, appendix/permission-catalog and open-questions — 31 occurrences outside this log, counted with grep rather than remembered, and deliberately not all of them live claims: several are struck quotations of the round that introduced the word, which stay struck and stay wrong in place because that is what keeping history means. The live ones all state a set where the model has a relationship. In a table listing seeded values the word meant "the guardians of this ward, via the guardianship channel", which is a true and useful thing to mean and the wrong notation to write, because D-107's test for an operand is a named registry row and no such row exists. Worse than a typo: a notation that parses reads as a decision someone made. The fix at each site is to name the channel and the value — a guardianship.right_level of view or write — which the same tables already do in their prose one line away, and which several of them do in the very next sentence.
    4. GUARDIANS(p) goes too, and this clause is the one that changed after the entry was first written. My first version removed the empty form and kept the parenthesised one as a narrowing operand — "the set of guardians of the named child", useful, I argued, for granting a ward's guardians something the three right_levels do not contain. That was wrong, and the reason is a fact about the channel I had described twice without drawing its consequence:

    "True, GUARDIANS should not be selectable in the audience, guardians can perform ANY action (their read/write perms grant) on behalf of the child."

    A guardian's authority is not a list of codes; it is a level over a child. write does not mean these six permissions — it means any action about this child. So an operand aiming a grant at a child's guardians offers, one code at a time, a subset of what those people already hold, and it can only ever be used about an item the channel would have reached anyway. It is not redundant by accident; it is the long way round to something the model grants outright. The reason it took a round to see is that GUARDIANS(p) looks like a perfectly ordinary narrowing operand — the ROLE(x) of family relationships — and the reason it is not one is that what it narrows is not a permission set but a relationship that carries its own authority, which nothing else in the registry is. That is the whole content of "a channel is not a selector"; clause 3 was only the half I had got.

    What a real need looks like instead. "Send the parents of these six children the newsletter" is not a permission question — it is a set of people with a lifetime, which is what person_group is for (D-098): name the group, put the six guardians in it, aim the grant at GROUP(x). If the list must be derived rather than kept, the honest sentence is that this system stores a guardianship row and no query over it as an audience. A derived guardian list is a feature this system does not have — checked, not assumed: nothing anywhere in the model derives a set of people from the guardianship rows except the channel itself, not as a group's membership kind, not as an office's holder, in no table — and a missing feature is a question or a decision entry, not an operand nobody checked for. 5. binds_to_item keeps its meaning and loses its exhibit. D-102 introduced the column to settle a question about guardianship — it "records whose guardianship is being asked about, which is the only thing GUARDIANS ever needed and could not say" — and the thing it was introduced to classify turns out not to exist. The column is undamaged: ATTENDED, PLACED, CONFIRMED, ON_WAITLIST and ORGANIZERS are true because they cannot be evaluated without the event the grant sits on, and everything else is false because it can — five true rows, and this clause first printed four, having written the four attendance tiers and forgotten the roster, which is the same slip in the same entry that exists to record slips of exactly this kind: a count written from the shape of a sentence instead of from the table. ORGANIZERS is true on the registry's own row (yes, event) and is named in the same save-path rule in data-model/08-events.md. What changes is that the column is now justified by the event operands, which is a large and ordinary reason, instead of by one strange case that was never real. Where the confusion actually lived, because the two sentences are the evidence: data-model/05's registry row read Binds to item = "yes, person" — the one row in a table whose third column means the item this grant sits on, using the column to say something the column cannot say, and adding "Bound: the guardians of the person this item is about" — asserting a bound form the registry could not produce. data-model/08-events.md then counted it as the sixth attendance tier: "The sixth is GUARDIANS, which binds to the person an item is about rather than to an event." An operand with no seed row, listed among five that have rows, declared bound by a page while its own row used a column whose definition excludes it. Corrected at both sites: nothing guardian-shaped is bound to anything, because nothing guardian-shaped is an operand. What is left is four attendance tiers plus the organizer roster — five operands that bind, and no sixth, which is the naming data-model/08-events.md uses and the one this entry should have used the first time: "five tiers" and "five binding operands" are different claims wearing the same number, and only one of them is about the tiers.

    The lesson is not about guardianship, it is about registries that are copied. A row can be wrong in a way no check catches, because the check compares the row's fields to each other and a field used to mean two things agrees with itself. binds_to_item needed a third value, or the sentence needed a different column, and neither happened for eleven rounds because the sentence was doing a good job in prose.

    Why this is not merely a naming cleanup, and what class of error it is. SCHOOL was refused (D-108) for naming a fact no table stores. This is the same refusal one level up: a selector naming a mechanism the model implements somewhere else entirely. The grammar is closed and its vocabulary is checkable, so a word that parses without a row behind it is the only way to say something false in it — and this corpus has now been caught doing exactly that with a word nobody hand-typed, which means the vocabulary's open half was not the only place the check was needed. The general rule, for F-10's list: every selector name that appears in a document must be a selector_type row, and a check should be able to say so. That is a lint over documentation against a seed list, which is a strange thing to want until the fourteenth selector turns out to be a prose idiom.

    What this costs, and why the answer is "less than it looked like from the clause above". Deleting the operand removes no capability, which is worth being precise about because the first draft of this entry thought otherwise and minted an open question for the loss. The worry was: nothing can now denote "whatever guardians the person this item is about has", so a guardian could never be given a permission wider than view/write over their ward. That worry presumes view/write is a ceiling on codes, and it is not — it is a ceiling on acts about that child. A guardian at write may already perform any action whose subject is their ward, which is a larger grant than any audience operand could have assembled from single codes, and smaller in exactly the direction that matters: it reaches no item that is not about the child. The channel is both wider and narrower than the operand it replaces, which is what makes it a channel. The residual case — guardians of this child should get at something that is not about the child — is not a permissions question and never was: it is a list of people, and lists are groups. So there is no F-24 and there is no new row — this sentence printed "the fifteenth row" while GUARDIANS still held slot fourteen, and at thirteen the next operand would be the fourteenth, so the number has been replaced by the words rather than corrected: an ordinal in this entry turned out to be a count in disguise, which is the collision this entry exists to stop — and no third binding kind is needed: binds_to_item keeps its two operands' worth of justification on the event side and never has to mean the subject.

    Recorded because the pattern is the point: the open question I minted for this entry's supposed loss was emptied by the same ruling that caused it, ten minutes later, and an entry that had shipped in between would have put a real-sounding question in front of somebody who could not answer it. A question written against a capability should be checked against the channel that grants it before it is written down.

    The seam this entry leaves open, and whose it is. D-109 says a selector that a rule may name is never deleted — it is deprecated, hidden, or disabled — and this entry deletes a row. The two are reconciled by a fact about dates, not by an exception: the row is unseeded in the same round it is criticized, in a spec that has shipped no release, so the set of stored expressions naming it is empty by construction. It would not have stayed empty. The argument-less form was unsavable on every scope (person never was a grant_scope_type), but the explicit GUARDIANS(p) form — legal arity, an argument type that column can hold, and no item-type check because an explicit argument binds to nothing — was savable, and one writer finding it in a dropdown would have made this deletion illegal. That is the whole of the reconciliation, so it is also a standing instruction: a deployment that does hold such a leaf must hide the row, rewrite the leaves, and only then unseed it, because deleting a row under a live leaf produces precisely the failing leaf D-109 retired deletion to prevent, and D-109 said the only remaining way to make one was a redacted person. If this entry is ever re-read as licence to delete a seeded operand, the reading is wrong: what licenses the deletion here is the emptiness of the set of grants that could name it, and that set has to be checked, not assumed.

It is named here in prose and linked from nowhere, which is the smallest honest repair: deleting the words would hide the invention, and leaving the link would keep it.
