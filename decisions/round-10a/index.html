    Decision Log — Refinement round 10 — the deletions, part 1 (D-097 … D-100)¶

    The unit becomes a scope rather than a row, and a grant becomes one permission that either allows or denies.

    D-097 — There are no sub-units. The unit is a scope, not a row¶

    Status: decided (from your answer) Date: refinement round 10 Reverses: D-008, D-035, D-036, D-041, D-049, D-050, D-054, D-078, D-096. Amends D-031 and D-095, whose subject was always the nested unit.

    Your words. "I want to remove the sub-organizations completely. They are more of a 'group of people'. They are useful in permission groups, however they should not hide any information or have any special features. I have over-specified the system and this complicates things." Asked whether anything below the organization should still scope authority, the answer was no — and asked whether the root unit is still a table, the answer was "no table — the unit is a scope", with the vocabulary settled in the same breath: unit means the root, the organization itself, of which there is one; group means a set of people, which is where D-098 goes.

    Decision. The organization has no internal structure in this system. There is one unit, it is the organization, and it is not a row: it is the non-item scope that grant_scope_type already carries (is_item_scoped = false, a grant at it has no scope_id). Its code is unit. Everything that existed to describe the inside of an organization is deleted:

    Deleted With it goes
    org_unit the root row, the one-root partial unique index, path, depth, status, code, data_confidence on units
    org_unit_type, org_unit_type_rule the runtime unit vocabulary, is_leaf, max_depth, the seven-step nesting check, and the whole of D-054's argument about not guessing words
    org_unit_name rename and renumber history, change_reason, and the denormalised org_unit.name it fed
    org_unit_relationship split_from, merged_into, successor_of, renamed_to
    unit_participation sub-unit rosters, the root participation row every membership owed, and the transfers between units
    scope_exposure, scope.expose the only downward crossing, the direction-of-authority check, the scope.exposure.review code, and the nightly alert that was its sole detector
    unit_ancestors(), unit_descendants() the recursive walk, the path-consistency check, and the CI assertion that the resolver never calls it
    role_assignment.scope_type / scope_id a role is always held at the unit; there is nothing else to hold it at
    the resolver's unit tier the ladder of D-032 drops from four tiers to the ones D-099 states

    Why this is affordable now, when it was not affordable in round 1. Because of D-092, which your own answer supplied: the legacy corpus names no units. Two exports — people with their membership period at the root, and events with attendance — and no unit column anywhere. So the hierarchy has no recorded history to lose; "who was in pack X in 2011" already had no answer at go-live, and was already written down as a consequence rather than a bug. A structure that describes nothing imported and (per D-095) restricts nothing is a structure that carries no information, which is the definition of the over-specification you were pointing at.

    The rule that dies hardest is the founding one. D-031 —"authority over a unit originates at that unit and cannot be claimed from outside it"— existed to stop the center deciding permissions inside a body it does not run. With one unit there is no "outside it" to be stopped from. The rule is not weakened by this decision, it is deprived of its subject: every permission is held at the unit or on an item, and the unit's people are the only people there are. What D-031's reasoning still supports is carried forward by D-099 (deny is absolute) and by the ownership and guardianship channels, neither of which was ever about units.

    What is genuinely lost, said once and not argued away.

    1. The unit is no longer a locus of accountability for a child's records. D-031's safeguarding property was that access to a pack's children had to be granted by that pack, by people who recruited the leader and can be asked. That property is gone, and nothing in the remaining model reproduces it: a permission held at the unit reaches every member, and person.view was already the widest read permission in the system (D-083, D-086). What is left of the safeguarding story is the same three things D-095 was left with — a permission held by a named adult, an audit trail whose payloads expire after two years (D-088), and a report of who holds the write — minus the part where the named adult was somebody the unit chose.
    2. "Who was in the Thursday group in 2011?" stays answerable, because D-098 dates its member rows. What is lost is the unit framing: there is no "and everything nested under it" left to ask about, so the subtree question that justified the materialized path has no subject.
    3. Restructuring stops being a recorded event. Splits, mergers and successions were the organization module's whole history problem (domain/04 §3) and they are simply gone: a group that splits is an old group that ended and two that began, with no edge between them unless somebody writes one.
    4. Unit-scoped reports — a pack's roster, a pack's events, "which units exist" — have no subject to report on. The organizational-year reports that mattered are membership and fee reports, which live at the unit and are unaffected.

    One thing gets easier that nobody counted. F-10 shape 4 — a column that may not change once set, the only shape with no report at all because a violation leaves every row individually valid — had exactly one instance in the whole design, org_unit.parent_unit_id. It has none now. The single-root conditional uniqueness (shape 2) goes too, along with integrity checks 1–10 of data-model/02-organization.md §9 and check 10's peculiar job of asserting that a migration had emitted a constraint.

    Import, restated. The one-off import owes person, membership (D-093) and — since D-102 — events and registrations. It owes no participation rows to anything, which retires D-093's knock-on about the root participation row and 03-membership.md §5.1's write obligation with it. Group membership is never inferred from anything: the corpus does not name groups either, so every person_group_member row is typed by a person, which is also the only way it can be true.

    Two-way door? No. This is a deletion of a dimension, not a flag. The route back is a decision with a reason attached — most plausibly "the organization has grown past one center's visibility" — and that decision would have to re-argue D-031 from its own premises, because the mechanism that made it work (scope_exposure, written by the covered unit) no longer exists.

    See: domain/04-organization-structure.md (rewritten as the groups module), data-model/02-organization.md, data-model/05-authorization.md §6, data-model/03-membership.md §5.

    D-098 — A group is a person_group: explicit members, a lifetime, and no authority¶

    Status: decided (from your answer) Date: refinement round 10 Follows: D-097 Supersedes the subject of: D-041

    Closes: —

    Your words. "A group defined by display name and an audience… A group is just an audience, but it is visible to the users of the system (if the permissions say so)." Then, when the choice was put back as group-is-an-audience or audience-is-a-selector: "person_group, but let's make it upside down — a group will have explicitly stated members and a person_group can be used as an audience selector", and "a group will have explicitly stated members (all members live, while the group lives). The group can have a lifetime (start and end date)."

    Decision. person_group — a name, a description, its own valid_from/valid_to, the standard audit columns. person_group_member — (person_group_id, person_id, valid_from, valid_to, who wrote it, when). The member row is dated: assignment, removal and re-assignment are all periods on the same pair, so the roster has a history of its own rather than borrowing the audit trail's. person_group is registered in grant_scope_type, so a group is an item and a grant can be written on one — which is how "these two people may work this group's roster" is said without inventing a scope (D-099).

    What a group is not, in one list, because this is the part the previous design got wrong. It has no parent and no children, no type and no vocabulary of types, no code, no path, no nesting rules, no rename history, no split or merger edge, no leadership of its own, no roles (your words: "screw them, the groups don't have any roles, only the root unit"), and no fee. It holds no permissions over its members by being a group: a member of a group is not able to do anything, which is D-005 and D-019 restated one level down — participation is not authority, and the fact that the participation is now stored in a tidier table does not quietly promote it.

    Why the word "audience" survives at all, and where the line is. You named the inversion, and it is the load-bearing part of this entry: a group states its members; a selector picks them. person_group is the domain fact — a thing a user sees, names, opens, and ends. audience is the grantee a grant names, and one of its operands may be a person_group (D-101). Two tables answer "who is in it", which is precisely the shape D-080 teaches to be suspicious of, so the difference is stated rather than implied: a group's members are a list of people that is true whether or not any permission points at it; audience_member rows are an addition to a rule, meaningful only inside the selector they complete. A group exists to be talked about; a selector exists to be aimed at something.

    Membership of the unit is unchanged and is not a group. One membership per person, at the unit, paid for yearly (D-037, D-040, D-047). unit_participation is deleted with the units (D-097), including the root row every membership used to be issued with, so "everyone in the organization" is now one query against membership and "everyone in the Thursday group" is one query against person_group_member — two shapes, and no longer one shape with a depth in it. D-050's renewal mechanism narrows to exactly what it always claimed to be about: the membership row, per D-016.

    Joining a group is still somebody else's act, and D-095's reasoning transfers intact. The gate is person_group.member.manage, held at the unit (which reaches every group, per D-099) or granted on one group as an item. There is no self-scoped variant and none will be added: nobody writes themselves onto a roster, which was the whole of D-095 and is unchanged by moving the roster. membership_request loses requested_unit_id — a request is for membership of the unit, and which group a new member lands in is a write on the group, not a preference on the request.

    So the history question has an answer, and it is the same answer every dated row in this system gives. "Who was in schuzka2026-1 on 14 March 2026?" is a point-in-time read over person_group_member, exactly as domain/05's query 3 was for a unit, and it survives the audit trail's 24-month payload expiry (D-088) because it does not depend on the trail. The group's own valid_from/valid_to and the member rows' periods are two different facts and both are kept: the first says the group existed over that span, the second says who was in it. An open group with dated members answers the historical question just fine, which is the whole reason the rows are dated — the naming in your own example (schuzka2026-1) becomes a habit the organization may or may not keep, rather than the mechanism history depends on.

    Dating the rows imports the invariant set that dating always imports, and naming them here is the work: where both ends are set, valid_to is later than valid_from; no two rows for the same person and group may overlap, counted over all rows and not only the open ones, half-open so a row ending on a day and one beginning that same day do not collide (the D-002 shape, and F-10 shape 1, so a hand-written migration emits it and the ORM will not); a person who left in 2020 and came back in 2024 is two rows, never one row edited in place, because the edit destroys the answer to "was she in the group in 2019?"; and moving from one group to another closes one row and opens another — 03-membership.md §5.2's transfer discipline, retargeted at groups and otherwise unchanged.

    The duplication is now real, which is why the two tables are said apart by their rules and not only by their names. person_group_member and audience_member are both dated sets of people, which is precisely the pair D-080 teaches a reader to distrust — two statements that can drift, one of which will be the one the screens read. They stay apart because the maintenance differs, and the dated shape makes the difference sharper rather than softer: a group's members are the organization's record of who attends, kept by the people who run the group, and it is true when no grant exists; audience_member rows are a selector's additions, kept by whoever composes permissions, and are meaningless outside the selector they complete. Merging them would make the permission table the system's roster, which is the same mistake D-062 refused when it declined to answer "who is running this camp?" by reading the grant table.

    Amended by D-105, which deleted one half of the pair. There is no audience_member any more, so the distrust this paragraph arranges is aimed at a table that no longer exists — and the duplication it feared is gone rather than reconciled. What survives is the distinction, now carried by a leaf instead of a table: a group's dated roster is the organization's record of who attends and is true when no grant exists, while PERSON(x) inside an expression is an addition made by whoever composes permissions and means nothing outside it. The closing refusal survives, and is cheaper to honour than it was. The temptation to answer "who is running this camp?" from the grant table was always resisted by saying use the roster instead; when the roster was the only dated set of people, that was a real constraint on the composer. Now there is a second way to name the same people — a run of PERSON(...) leaves written beside the grant — so the hazard this entry named has moved from "two tables drift" to "the composer chose the wrong one", which is what data-model/02-organization.md now says out loud, and what a MEMBERS * ROLE(...) expression exists to make the obvious answer.

    What is actually given up against unit_participation is small and worth listing, because "no special features" was the instruction. No join_reason_code / leave_reason_code (and so no transfer, promotion, reorganisation, unit_closed vocabulary to maintain or to get wrong), no is_primary saying which of a person's groups to show first, no role_in_unit display label, no data_confidence and nothing to import into: the corpus names no groups, so every member row is written by a person (D-092, D-097).

    Two-way door? The periods are the door and they are open. What to refuse later is the vocabulary that used to hang off them — a controlled leave_reason_code list is exactly the eighteen-ticks-without-a-receipt shape D-095 removed, and a role_in_unit column is the thing that always turns into an authorization check the moment somebody is asked "can she edit the roster?"

    See: domain/04-organization-structure.md, data-model/02-organization.md §1–§2, data-model/03-membership.md §5.

    D-099 — A grant allows or denies one permission. There is no level, and no locality¶

    Status: decided (from your answer) Date: refinement round 10 Reverses: D-012 (including the reason it was written), D-013 as a level, D-032's second half, D-074's level form, D-010. Amends D-055, D-068 and D-079.

    Your words. "This is more complicated. I said that there are read, write, none permissions. This is not correct. What would make the most sense is if items would inherit all unit-scope permissions and would have their own item-scope permissions in the same format (e.g. event.edit). Both of those permissions are essentially the same, however in the item-scope some of them don't make sense (e.g. on an event, membership.manage, or event.create, make no sense). On the item-scope there should also be a deny-permission, not just allow-permission." And on whether anything still needs a ladder: "only allow/deny on the same permissions as on the root unit."

    Decision. One grant is: a grantee, a scope, one permission code, an effect, and a period. The grantee is a person or an audience (D-042, unchanged). The scope is the unit or exactly one item (D-097). The effect is allow or deny. The answer is:

    allowed when some live allow applies and no live deny applies.

    That is the whole of the precedence rule. What it replaces, item by item:

    Deleted Was Why it goes
    access_level revoked 0, view 10, contribute 20, manage 30, admin 40 A level is a bundle of permissions identified by one word. D-068 already refused that idea once, when it deleted <resource>.admin and said roles are the bundle. This finishes the same thought: roles and audiences are the bundle, and nothing else is
    permission_level which level satisfies which permission at which scope type Its two jobs move to two columns on permission itself (§below)
    The rank integer D-012 There is no ordering left to compare. The famous bug it guarded — 'admin' < 'contribute' — cannot occur in a system with no levels, which is a better fix than the guard
    Locality D-032: a nearer allow beats a further one Locality only ever ranked allows against each other. With a boolean answer, two allows that both apply produce one answer; there is nothing to order. D-032's first half — deny is absolute — survives intact and is now its entire content
    revoked as a level resolver channel 0 Deny survives as an effect on a grant row. D-013's requirement — a court-ordered restriction must not be beatable by a broader grant — is unchanged and is still checked first

    permission gains two columns, and only one of them is comfortable.

    • applies_to ∈ unit, unit_and_item — where a code may be granted at all. This is your observation that event.create and membership.manage are nonsense on an event, written as data instead of as prose. It is checked when the grant is saved, not at check time: a grant of event.create written on one event is refused with the reason in the error, because an event cannot be created twice, and a creation permission attached to a thing that already exists answers no question.
    • right_kind ∈ read, write — and it exists for exactly one customer. D-074 fixes a guardian's rights at three values, and with the ladder gone the guardianship channel has nothing left to compare a permission against. This column is that thing: none covers nothing, view covers read codes, write covers both. It is a column whose only job is one other mechanism, and saying so is cheaper than discovering it later. The alternative was to store permission codes on the guardianship row, which D-074 refused on the grounds that a volunteer cannot maintain a 74-code checklist per child.

    Inheritance, stated once. A grant at the unit reaches every item of every type, because after D-097 there is no intermediate scope left between the unit and an item, and no ownership relation to test. A grant on one item reaches that item and nothing else. This is D-006 — per-item permissions are capabilities on the item — with the unit tier left standing, and it deletes can_event() outright (D-102).

    - in a selector is not a deny, and the two are not interchangeable. An audience expression may subtract a set (MEMBERS - GROUP(x)), which narrows one grant's reach. A deny defeats every grant that applies to that person, including ones written elsewhere, later, or by somebody else. The first cannot express "she must not have this, whatever else she is given", which is D-013's whole reason for existing, so denies stay — and they are legal at the unit as well as on an item, since "exclude her from what the unit grants everyone" is exactly the case the selector cannot serve.

    The round silently upgraded deny, and the upgrade needs naming because it changes an operator's job. D-013 did not say a deny beats everything; it said revoked beats deny, which left an escape hatch — a stale denial could be outranked by a newer act, and revoking it was a decision somebody could take late and still take. Deleting the ranking deleted the hatch. A live deny now beats every live allow with no tie-break of any kind, and the only thing that removes one is a person ending its period. That is why the integrity check below is a requirement rather than a nicety, and why a deny is the row on which access_grant.valid_to earns its keep: everywhere else an open-ended grant that outlived its reason costs a tidy database, and here it costs a child who cannot see their own camp photos. A date is the only remedy that works without somebody remembering to act.

    What it costs, in rows. A role that used to be one grant at manage is now one row per code. The catalog holds 66 codes; a broad office is a couple of dozen rows. That is the price of the question you are now able to ask — "what exactly does this person have?" answered by reading rows — and the price of the one you can no longer ask, which is "what does manage give me on this event?": there is no such word, so there is no such lookup. Integrity check to add: a live grant whose effect is deny and whose grantee, scope and code match no live allow is dead weight and should be reported, because a deny that denies nothing is how a stale restriction survives its reason.

    Two-way door? Cheap in one direction, expensive in the other. Levels can come back as names for sets of codes with no comparison defined — which is what a role already is, so no new mechanism is needed to approximate one. A real ordering coming back means re-importing permission_level, the rank rule, and locality's tie-break, all of which were deleted with their only consumers.

    See: data-model/05-authorization.md §2, §6–§7, domain/06-authorization-model.md, appendix/permission-catalog.md.

    D-100 — A role is an office and a group is company: two tables of the same shape¶

    Status: decided (from your answer) Date: refinement round 10 Follows: D-097, D-098, D-099

    Reverses: D-039's resolver clause, D-051 entirely, D-033 and the position module, D-009's remaining subject.

    Closes: F-12.

    Your words. "A role_assignment is no longer necessary, as it can be supplemented by a ROLE selector in the audience string." Then, when asked whether roles go too: *"Role and group are technically the same, but not semantically. A role was meant to be for a single person / a small couple of individuals that are visible as the leaders / financial / IT managers of the organization, while the group is a temporary set of members that do e.g. events together." And on the gate: "This should be sortable with the audiences, right? Some permission (e.g. event.create) can be assigned to MEMBERS * COUNSELORS."

    Decision. Three things, of which the first is the one that looks like nothing.

    1. role_assignment becomes role_holder. (role_id, person_id, valid_from, valid_to, granted_by, note) — no scope columns, because D-097 left one scope. role keeps code, name, description, is_system, sort_order. role_permission is deleted: a role confers nothing by itself, and the codes an office carries are the live grants whose audience names ROLE(x). What that buys is the question the old design could not answer cleanly — "what may a treasurer do?" — which is now a query over visible grant rows rather than a walk through a bundle table nobody reads. This is where D-068 was already going; it just went the rest of the way. role.create, role.edit, role.delete, role.view, role.assign, role.unassign survive, retargeted: the last two write role_holder. role.scope_required and role.requires_membership are deleted with their mechanisms. role joins person_group in grant_scope_type, so "these two may manage the counselors roster" is a grant on one role, which is D-098's move applied symmetrically.

    2. Positions are deleted. position, position_assignment, is_leadership, max_holders and the appointment workflows go: with no units there is no body to be a leader of, which is the only thing a position recorded and a role does not (D-033's own reasoning for why leadership is a position-assignment fact and not a role fact). An elected leader is now a holder of a role, and the audience rule that used to read unit_leaders — which round 6 had to correct because it was silently matching nobody — is ROLE(leaders) and means what its name says. What is genuinely lost is the elected-versus-appointed distinction as stored data: it survives only as a role the organization invents (elected_council next to appointed_it) or not at all. F-12 is closed and its answer is no: role_holder names one person. The reason the register refused it — a derived holder list cannot answer "who was treasurer in 2023", and an office record that cannot answer that is not an office record — is untouched, and the grantee pair that F-12 contemplated is no longer even tempting, because grants now aim at roles instead of roles being filled by grants.

    3. The gate moves from the resolver into the selector, and this is the round's largest deletion. D-039 made the resolver ask, for every grant, whether the holder currently holds a membership; D-051 then needed system.gate_exempt and person_holds_gate_exempt to let appointed people through anyway, with the documented hazard that a permission check performed through can() there means a lockout with no error message. Now MEMBERS is a selector operand evaluated live on the checked date, and:

    Old mechanism New expression
    The gate applied to a role with requires_membership = true MEMBERS * ROLE(x)
    The gate waived for a whole role ROLE(x) — write it without MEMBERS
    The gate waived for one named person (system.gate_exempt) MEMBERS * ROLE(x) + PERSON(y)

    role.requires_membership, system.gate_exempt and person_holds_gate_exempt are all deleted, the resolver's membership clause is deleted, and the special-case path that had to be carefully kept out of can() goes with it. Ownership and guardianship stay channels and are unaffected — they were never gated. D-071's posture transfers to role_holder: nothing forces a holder row to end, and the control is the report.

    The posture reverses, and the new failure mode is named rather than buried. The gate used to be default-on, so that forgetting it was impossible. It is now opt-in per grant: a grant written as ROLE(TREASURERS) with no MEMBERS authorizes a lapsed member, silently, forever. Three controls, none of which is the resolver:

    • A standing report of live grants naming a ROLE(...) operand whose expression does not contain MEMBERS. An office held by a non-member is the case worth a human eye, and this report is narrow enough to actually be read.
    • Seeded grants and any grant template carry MEMBERS, so the path of least repetition is the safe path.
    • Import completeness. MEMBERS * COUNSELORS is only as correct as the counselor roster: the gate used to fall out of membership alone, so an import that misses 300 counselors silently un-gates them. It fails closed — nothing breaks, which is exactly why nobody notices. The import reconciliation report of D-090 must count people marked as counselors in the corpus against role_holder rows written.

    PERSON(y) is a selector, and this entry is the reason it had to become one. The grammar D-101 first shipped did not include it — LIST and GROUP(x) cover the enumeration cases, and a language with no attribute operands has no obvious place for one person — which left the third row of the table above unparseable in the very entry that wrote it down. It is seeded in selector_type like every other operand, it takes a person, and it passes the materializability test for the plain reason that a set of one is a set. The workaround the membership module reached for before this was repaired — write a second grant whose grantee is that person — stays legal and is arguably clearer for a permanent exception; what was not acceptable is a control that does not parse.

    A consequence of moving the gate that no entry in this round had named: the expiry sweep can lock the organization out of its own system. D-040 lapses memberships on a date, to everyone at once, and whoever runs that sweep is a member too. Under D-039 the collision existed but system.gate_exempt was the purpose-built hole in it, and that mechanism is deleted above. So an administrative office whose grants read MEMBERS * ROLE(...) stops working at the instant the sweep lapses its holder — including the office that would have been asked to fix it. What carries the case now is the second control: the seeded grants for offices that must survive a lapse are written ROLE(...) with no MEMBERS, which turns a flag nobody read into a written expression with an outage attached to getting it wrong. Assumption: the seeded is_system role set contains at least one such office; nothing in the catalog requires it to, and if it does not, 1 January is a support call.

    Why two tables, when you said they are technically the same. Because the difference decides who may edit the membership, and one table would merge those two permissions into one. role.* is the most sensitive code family in the catalog (🔒🔒); writing a group's roster is person_group.member.manage (⚠). One person_group table with a kind column and one membership table gives a single code that both adds a child to the Thursday group and puts the holder into SYSTEM_ADMINS. That is the merge to refuse, and it is the answer to D-080's lesson about two same-shaped tables: these are not two statements of one rule, they are two different grants of power. Same columns, different codes, and ROLE(x) / GROUP(x) distinct in the grant text so the distinction is visible to whoever reads the grant — which is where you asked for it.

    COUNSELORS is seeded, as a role. A permanent, organization-wide population is the opposite of your own definition of a group ("a temporary set of members that do events together"), and the table decides the sensitivity that protects the set every safeguarding convention hangs from. It ships is_system so an editor refuses to delete it. Consistent with D-005: this is a set, and being in it confers nothing until a grant names it — audience membership has never been authority (D-042, restated in D-091). Bare COUNSELORS is legal in an expression, which makes the name namespace load-bearing (D-101).

    Two-way door? The gate is the expensive half. Restoring a default-on membership test means re-adding a resolver clause and re-deciding which permissions it applies to, because a system where every expression is explicit has no record of which grants were meant to be gated. The role/group split is a cheap door in both directions: the tables have the same shape, so merging them is a migration, and the only thing to re-argue is the permission separation this entry records.
