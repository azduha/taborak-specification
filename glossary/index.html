    Glossary — Ubiquitous Language¶

    Terms used consistently across every document in this folder. Where everyday language is ambiguous, the definition here wins and the schema follows it.

    The three-way split that everything else depends on¶

    These three words are frequently conflated in real organizations. This design keeps them strictly apart, because conflating them is the single most common source of unfixable data models in this domain.

    Term Definition Table
    Person A human being the organization knows about, now or ever. Exists independent of whether they have ever logged in, paid, or applied. person
    Account A set of login credentials attached to a person. Optional — a person imported from a 1994 paper roster has no account. account
    Member Not a noun in this system. A person is "a member" at a moment in time if they hold a qualifying membership. It is always a question with a date attached. membership

    A fourth, related distinction:

    Term Definition Table
    User Avoid entirely. When you mean the account, say account; when you mean the human, say person. Most systems use "user" for the wrong one and it never gets fixed. —

    Identity¶

    Person — A human. Stores given name, family name, birth date, and gender. Nothing else. In particular: no member status, no child/adult flag, no troop, no role, and no deceased flag — the organization does not record death. What the organization records about the human is not on this table at all: it is descriptive data, reached by the same permission as the rest of the person record, because none of it is a different kind of object.

    Account — Credentials and login state for a person. A person may have zero or more accounts over their lifetime (e.g. a childhood account abandoned in 2010, a new one created in 2024), but at most one live one — closed rows aside. Accounts are never shared between people, not even between a parent and child.

    Account activation gate — The rule that an account may not reach active while an unresolved identity hypothesis names its person. This is what a colliding sign-up runs into: a sign-up that strong-matches an existing person — exact legacy member number, or exact birth date together with a normalized name, never the fuzzy band — raises a hypothesis. Its own rows are created and stay pending_verification: nothing is merged, nothing is linked, nothing is activated. The contact point the person typed survives as the credential it was offered as, and identity_reviewer decides it with person.merge. The holding message confirms nothing, since a sign-up form that can answer "yes, that person exists" is an existence oracle over a roster of children; and a hypothesis that is blocking an account gets an age check that alerts, because a stalled queue is no longer a data-quality problem but a specific person who cannot log in (D-089). "I have a new e-mail address" is not this case and is not routed through it: account_credential holds many credentials for one account.

    Contact point — A single email address, phone number, or postal address belonging to a person. Stored as many rows per person, not as columns, so that a person can have five email addresses, or a new kind invented later, without a migration. Each has a type, a purpose, and a verification timestamp.

    Identity hypothesis — A proposed but unconfirmed link between two person rows that may actually be the same human. Created during legacy import, by the nightly matcher, when someone registers claiming a historical identity, or by a sign-up that strong-matches a person who already exists. Confirming one triggers a merge; while one is unresolved it also holds the account of any person it names out of active — see account activation gate.

    Merge — The act of declaring two person rows to be one human, and collapsing them. Never a delete: the absorbed row survives as a tombstone pointing at the survivor, so that historical foreign keys keep resolving. Always a human's decision (person.merge), including at the highest score — and refused outright when the chosen survivor turns out, on resolution, to be the person being absorbed.

    Merge chain — What forms when a survivor is itself later absorbed. The pointer is not rewritten forward: A→B→C stays a chain and resolve_person() walks it to the live row. This is decided, not provisional — chains persist because reversing a merge would otherwise be a reconstruction of facts nobody recorded, and flattening is closed off rather than merely faster (D-087). Two rules come with them, neither on the read: a cycle is impossible because the merge is refused, never because a hop limit gave up; and a chain longer than three hops is reported, which is a statement about the matcher's queue, not about the row.

    Unmerge — Reversing a merge a human approved. The tombstone comes off, rows created before the merge go back to the revived person, and rows created inside the window are flagged for a human rather than split by a rule — every access_grant from that window flagged, not guessed. The merge is reversible; the merged window is not: what two people did while they were one row stays one row's history, because the record cannot separate who did it (D-087). No permission of its own — undoing an identity correction is the same act as making one, so it is person.merge.

    Membership export — One of the two files the legacy corpus actually is, and neither of them is a roster. A row says: a person, a birth date, and the period they were a member of the unit (D-092). A people batch turns it into person rows and bounded membership rows — both ends dated, data_confidence = 'low', batch id set — and no fee obligation of any kind (D-093). ~~Each bringing its root-unit participation row with it~~ — withdrawn by D-097, which deleted the table those rows lived in; the import owes participation rows to nothing. It names no group either, so it answers neither "who was in the Thursday group in 2011" nor "were they a child or a rover", and nothing is created in person_group from it: a corpus that names no groups means every roster row is typed by a person, which is also the only way one can be true (D-098).

    Attendance export — The other file: events, and which people came. It becomes event rows with no owner — ~~owned by the root unit~~, withdrawn by D-102, which deleted the owner column outright and D-094's rule with it: there is no column left for an imported event to have — with one event_registration per attendee at attending and was_present = true, and no not_attending rows, because an absence row would say the system asked someone who was never asked. Attendance never becomes a membership, and it never becomes a group roster either: attendance is read through the ATTENDED and PLACED selectors, and no rows are written into person_group_member from it.

    Personal data¶

    Everything the system knows about a person once you subtract what they are called and counted as. The module is domain/08-person-data.md, the tables are data-model/09-attributes.md, and the reasoning is D-083, D-084 and D-085.

    Descriptive data — Everything the system holds about a person other than their name, their gender and their age: one class of data, with no privileged corner inside it. Allergies, medication, insurance numbers, languages, measurements, clothing sizes, dietary habits, a grandparent's phone manner — the same kind of fact, held in fields rather than files, none of it a different kind of object from any other, and all of it reached by the permission that already reads the person. The term exists to rule a category out: there is no sensitive sub-record with its own codes, which is what D-083 declined on the merits rather than failed to build. Its cost is stated there and in domain/08 §4, and it is the reason person.view is graded in the catalog's top tier.

    Attribute definition — One thing the organization has decided to collect: a code, a label, a description of what belongs in the field, a value type, and whether several values may be current at once. The type draws the form and raises the warning; it never refuses a save a human insists on — 2019 recorded as the year of a checkup is a truer record than an empty field behind a validation error. Creating, renaming, merging or retiring a definition is attribute.define; recording a value against one needs nothing beyond person.edit.

    Person attribute — The value one person currently has for one code. Overwritten in place: the row is the organization's present belief, and the sequence of earlier beliefs lives in the audit trail, values included, for 24 months — logged on the same terms as a contact point or a note, because a list of person fields whose values must not be logged would be exactly the privileged corner D-083 deleted, and would leave nothing behind for an overwritten value. What guards the trail is who may read it, not what it omits — and it now omits, on a clock: those logged values are emptied 24 months after they were written (see payload reduction), so the trail is the record of what changed, who did it and when for the life of the system and the record of the values for two years. The honest name for the whole stack is therefore no long history, not merely no temporal columns: the value lives in one overwritten row, the copy of it depends on a write path remembering to log, and the copy ages out. If a case ever needs the long version, the fix is temporal columns on the value — D-085 reversed — and not a longer trail. No valid_from / valid_to, deliberately (data-model/09 §3).

    Proposed code — A definition that came into existence because somebody recorded a value against a code that did not exist. It waits in the curation queue: kept out of data entry forms and reports, yet readable by anyone already entitled to read the person, because the organization's data quality problem is not the family's confidentiality problem. It is where an improvised need at 23:00 goes instead of into a notes box that nobody will read again — improvised by a person at a desk, which is the whole meaning. It is emphatically not "whatever the legacy import did not understand": the codes a batch's column→code map names arrive live, because a curator read that map as one object before the batch applied, and several hundred proposals landing on day one would make this queue's age signal meaningless before it had ever meant anything (D-090).

    Retired code — A definition taken out of data entry and left alone everywhere else: readable, reportable, revivable, its values untouched. Deleting a label must never delete the facts filed under it, or the badly-named label is never retired and the vocabulary accretes instead of being curated. Redaction is the opposite operation: retiring keeps the values, erasing a person deletes them, immediately and whatever their age. It used to be called the only eraser; there is now a second one — payload reduction, below — and the distinction is that redaction answers to a person and is absolute, while reduction answers to a calendar and is bounded.

    Payload reduction — Emptying the old_values / new_values out of an audit row 24 months after it was written, leaving everything else standing: actor, on-behalf-of, operation, table, record id, changed fields, transaction, session, channel. The trail goes on answering who changed this row, when, and were they allowed to for the life of the system, and stops answering and to what after two years; twelve months is the floor the window may be set to. Not redaction, which is a named person's erasure, immediate and absolute, and reaches payloads of any age; not an import revert, which deletes the rows a batch created. Reduction is the one case in this design that deliberately unwrites later than the write, so its failure mode is not running at all — hence a scheduled job with a check that alerts on any row past its window still carrying a payload (D-088).

    The two clocks — The accounting skeleton (membership, fee_obligation, payment, and the anonymised person row an erasure leaves behind) has no clock at all, now justified as the organization's own history rather than merely unchallenged. Assumption: that this indefinite retention of rows about a named person has a legal basis — organizational history plus accounting obligations, against a redacted person row that keeps the skeleton non-identifying — is unverified, and a decision log is not the document that grants it. The audit trail runs the opposite direction: rows forever, payloads for 24 months. Erasure sits outside both, immediate and absolute. The same window is proposed for access_log and is not yet confirmed (D-088).

    Curation — Keeping the vocabulary meaning one thing: merging near-duplicates, promoting or discarding proposals, retiring codes nobody uses, and writing the description that stops one code meaning four things to four people. It is attribute.define plus somebody who reads what they are given, and the documents treat it as a job rather than an outcome — an open vocabulary left unwatched decays into allergy, Allergy, alergie and allergies (2), after which the layer still stores everything and answers nothing.

    Not to be confused with identity, which stays structured in its own tables: a fact keeps a table of its own when there is a rule about it that an open layer physically cannot hold — one primary postal contact, one current name, a birth number that may not repeat. The boundary is that test, not sensitivity (domain/08 §1).

    Organization¶

    The unit — There is exactly one, and it is the organization. It is a scope, not a row: no table, no id, no parent, no type, no name history, no path (D-097). In grant_scope_type its code is unit — the one row there with is_item_scoped = false — and a grant written at the unit carries no scope_id. Say the unit: not "the root unit", not "the organization unit", not "org". There is no second unit for the word root to distinguish this one from, and a name that implies a second one is how the hierarchy creeps back into a sentence. Membership, a role, and a grant at non-item scope are all held at the unit, and a grant at it reaches every item of every type (D-099).

    ~~Org unit — Any node in the organization's structural hierarchy, of two kinds, and the difference was load-bearing: the single root unit, where people were members, and sub-units beneath it — weekly meeting groups, packs, sections, teams — created by the organization or by other sub-units, nested to arbitrary depth, each running its own events, its own local leadership and its own roster.~~ Deleted by D-097, which also deleted org_unit_type, org_unit_type_rule, org_unit_name, org_unit_relationship, unit_participation, scope_exposure, unit_ancestors() and unit_descendants(). Why it existed: a hierarchy is how a system says which body a piece of authority belongs to, and the type vocabulary existed because that vocabulary changes over decades while the data must not (D-054). Why the reason stopped applying: D-092 established that the legacy corpus names no units, and D-095 that a unit restricted nothing — a structure that describes nothing imported and restricts nothing carries no information. What is genuinely lost, said once and not argued away: the unit as a locus of accountability for a child's records (access to a body's children used to have to be granted by that body, which is D-031's property and nothing in the remaining model reproduces it); restructuring as a recorded event — splits, mergers and successions are gone; and every per-unit report, because there is one unit to report on. Two-way door: no. This is a deleted dimension, not a flag.

    Unit type — ~~A category of org unit (organization, pack, group, section, …), defining whether units of that type may nest and inside which others, and kept as runtime rows rather than an enum because the words change while the data must not.~~ Deleted by D-097, with the seven-step nesting check, is_leaf, max_depth, and D-054's whole argument about not guessing words. The words pack, troop and section are now illustration of nothing in particular — where these documents need a set of people they say group.

    Group — A person_group: a named set of people with explicitly stated, dated members and a lifetime of its own. A name, a description, valid_from / valid_to and the audit columns; its roster is person_group_member(person, valid_from, valid_to, who wrote it, note), dated rows that may not overlap counted over all rows, half-open (D-098). Company, not a body: the Thursday group, the kitchen crew, this year's camp staff — in the organization's own words "a temporary set of members that do e.g. events together". What a group is not, in one list, because this is the part the previous design got wrong: no parent and no children, no type and no vocabulary of types, no code, no path, no nesting rules, no rename history, no split or merger edge, no leadership of its own, no roles, and no fee. Being in a group authorizes nothing — person_group.member.manage writes a roster and nothing else, which is D-005 and D-019 restated one level down: participation is not authority, and tidier storage does not promote it. A group is an item (registered in grant_scope_type), so "these two may work this group's roster" is a grant on one group. Its two dated facts are different facts: the group's own period says it existed, the member rows say who was in it, and "who was in schuzka2026-1 on 14 March 2026" is a point-in-time read over the roster — which is why the rows are dated, and why that answer survives the audit trail's payload expiry (D-088). A group is not an audience: a group states its members and an audience picks them (Audience). Nobody writes themselves onto a roster — person_group.member.manage has no self-scoped variant and none will be added, which is D-095's rule moved intact, and no group is seeded.

    Office (a role) and role_holder — An office is a small, visible set of holders — the leaders, the finance people, IT — where a group is company. "Role and group are technically the same, but not semantically" is the sentence that kept them apart, and the difference decides who may edit the membership: role.assign / role.unassign are 🔒🔒, the most sensitive family in the catalog, while writing a group's roster is person_group.member.manage at ⚠ (D-100). role keeps code, name, description, is_system, sort_order; role_holder(role, person, valid_from, valid_to, granted_by, note) is the holder row and names exactly one person — one grantee per row, always, because F-12 is closed and the answer is no. A role confers nothing: role_permission is deleted, and the codes an office carries are the live grants whose expression names ROLE(x), which is what makes "what may a treasurer do?" a query over rows a person wrote. An office is held at the unit, always (D-097 left one scope), a role is an item so a grant can be written on one, and nothing forces a holder row to end — the report is the control (D-071, whose posture transferred from assignments unchanged).

    ~~Position — A defined role within a unit that people could occupy: scoutmaster, assistant_leader, unit_treasurer, quartermaster. A position was a slot: it existed whether or not anyone filled it, several people could occupy the same one at the same time, and max_holders was a per-position limit rather than a structural rule. A position assignment was the period during which a specific person occupied a specific position in a specific unit, with a start, an optional end and a primary flag.~~ Deleted by D-100, with position, position_assignment, is_leadership, max_holders, every position.* code, the appointment workflows, and D-033's occupancy rules. The reason is structural: with no units there is no body to be a leader of, which was the only fact a position recorded and a role does not. An elected leader is now a holder of a role, and the rule that used to read unit_leaders — which round 6 had to correct because it silently matched nobody — is ROLE(leaders), and means what its name says. What is lost: the elected-versus-appointed distinction as stored data, which survives only as roles the organization invents (elected_council beside appointed_it), and a vacancy as a reportable state — an empty office is the absence of rows, and absence has no start date (F-18).

    Membership¶

    Membership — The period during which a person is a member of the unit — the one unit, the organization, which is a scope and not a row. Holds valid_from, valid_to (null = open/current), and a status in an approval workflow. A person accumulates multiple memberships over time; they never overwrite one another. "Everyone in the organization" is now one query against membership, with no participation row beside it (D-097).

    There is exactly one membership, and it is ageless. Membership does not distinguish children from adults, has no age ceiling, and does not end because a person got older. Being a counselor or a leader is an office held through role_holder, never a membership type (D-100).

    Membership type — A table kept for the future, currently holding one row: member. It survives as a table rather than collapsing into a constant so that a second kind of membership (a supporting or honorary one) would be a data change rather than a migration. The fee is flat and per person, so the type does not affect pricing either.

    ~~Participation — The period during which a person takes part in one sub-unit: what a pack's roster was. Distinct from membership in every way — a person held exactly one membership and any number of participations, participation was free, and it ended when the person stopped attending rather than when a fee was unpaid. A participation row for the root unit was created alongside each membership, so "everyone in unit X" was one uniform query. Nobody wrote themselves into a sub-unit: a counselor did it, under unit_participation.manage at that unit, which had no self-scoped variant and none would be added (D-095).~~ Deleted by D-097 — the table, its codes (unit_participation.view, unit_participation.manage), the root row every membership owed, and the transfers between units. What replaced it is a group, and two of its rules survive the move verbatim: joining one is still somebody else's act under person_group.member.manage with no self-scoped variant, and moving between them still closes one dated row and opens another (D-098). What the legacy corpus wrote here changed in round 9 and then stopped being a question. This entry used to say an imported historical row is always a participation and never a membership, because the corpus carries no payment evidence (D-090) — then the people export turned out to state membership periods directly (D-092, D-093) — and now there is no table to write at all. What still never happens: a membership inferred from attendance, and an imported membership left open-ended. The surviving rule is one sentence: an imported membership is bounded at both ends, always.

    Membership request — A person's application to become a member of the unit. Separate from the membership itself, because a request has its own lifecycle (submitted, under review, rejected, withdrawn) and can be rejected without ever producing a membership. Nothing gates a request except identity and payment — there is no checklist to satisfy. ~~It could also name the unit the applicant wanted to join (requested_unit_id)~~ — deleted with the hierarchy by D-097, and D-098 left it deleted rather than retargeting it at a group: which group a new member lands in is a write on the group, never a preference on the request.

    Lapse — A membership ending because the yearly fee was not paid or the person withdrew. Distinct from termination for cause, which requires a reason code. A lapsed person is not a member and holds no member permissions, though a renewal window lets them rejoin without re-applying.

    Renewal window — The fixed period after a membership expires during which payment restores it without re-applying, and — importantly — opens the new period at the expiry date, so the record shows continuous coverage and the person is not misclassified as a returning member. Tracked as dates, not a status: during the window the person is simply expired.

    Returning member — A person whose new membership follows a gap. The system detects this rather than treating them as new. This is the case you described: 1990–2010, gap, 2024–present.

    Relationships¶

    Guardianship — A period during which one person is responsible for another. Directional: guardian → dependent. Carries relationship kind (parent, legal_guardian, foster_parent, step_parent, grandparent, sibling, other) and an end date that a person sets. Not a birthday: the system takes no position on majority, and no job ends one (D-069).

    Party to a guardianship — The guardian and the subject, both. They, and not staff, run the arrangement: either may end it, a guardian may add another guardian, and no request has to go through the organization (D-073). Staff hold the same powers and are a route, never the route. ~~This is scope authority applied to a household: authority over a nested thing originates at that thing.~~ — withdrawn by D-097, which deprived D-031 of the nesting that rule was about. The arrangement needs no borrowed principle: a guardianship is a relationship between two people who are both principals on the row, and that is why they run it.

    Guardian's right — One of exactly three values on the guardianship row: none, view, or write. Nothing more granular exists (D-074). ~~They rank onto the same ladder as every other grant — 0, 10, 20 — so a guardian is not a second authorization model.~~ The ladder is deleted by D-099, and with it the sentence that made this channel the same model: a guardian is now a channel of its own, and the three values are compared against a column rather than against a rank. permission.right_kind (read | write) is what a right is measured against — none covers nothing, view covers read codes, write covers both — and D-099 says out loud that this column exists for exactly this one customer, the guardianship channel being the only mechanism left that grades rather than flips. What is unchanged is the point the ladder was making: a guardian's act is checked under the ordinary code for the thing being touched, so this is one vocabulary, not a second authorization model.

    Being a guardian still grants nothing by itself; none is a real value and the common starting point for anything the guardian did not assert themselves. What D-074 gives up in exchange for the short vocabulary is recorded in the catalog: it is not possible to say "read everything except the medical record."

    That sentence is not waiting on a mechanism, and the right level has not become softer for it. D-083 was put the case the sentence was invented for — a parent keeping access to everything about their child except the medical file, by court order — and declined it on the merits: there is no medical record inside the person record to be excepted from, because everything other than name, gender and age is descriptive data of one class. So the right level is what it always was, none, view or write, applied to the record whole — and the thing that was given up is not a half-built feature but an arrangement the organization decided not to want.

    Not to be confused with guardianship permission, which was the deleted eighteen-code list (dependent.profile.view, dependent.medical.admin, …). Those codes are gone; do not write them into a role, a query, or a screen.

    Household — No longer a thing in this system (D-057). A person's address is a person_contact row of type postal, which is temporal like any other contact. Two people at one address are related by a query, not by a table. The word is kept here only to answer "where did the household go?": shared address never granted access, and still does not.

    Acting on behalf — Performing an operation as another person, under that guardianship row's right level (view to read it, write to change it). Every such operation records both the acting person and the subject person.

    Authorization¶

    Permission — The atomic unit of authority: a coded capability such as membership.request.approve or person.view. Permissions are the only things the authorization layer actually checks. Each row carries applies_to (unit, or unit_and_item — where the code may be granted at all, checked when a grant is saved, so a grant of event.create written on an event is refused rather than evaluated) and right_kind (read | write, which exists for exactly one customer: the guardianship channel, which has had nothing else to compare a permission against since the ladder went) (D-099). Codes are stable identifiers, never display strings, and there is no <resource>.admin wildcard and no prefix match (D-068).

    Role — An office, not a bundle: see Organization. What survives here is the one sentence people get wrong — a role confers nothing, and the codes it carries are the live grants naming ROLE(x) (D-100). Roles are runtime data: the organization invents them, composes them out of grants and deletes the unused ones (D-052).

    ~~Role assignment — The period during which a person holds a role, carrying a scope: organization-wide, or limited to a specific org unit — the same mechanism for both, because "treasurer of Pack 12" and "treasurer of the organization" differed only in scope.~~ Renamed role_holder and stripped of its scope columns by D-100, because D-097 left exactly one scope and there is nowhere else to hold an office; role_permission is deleted in the same entry, which is the half that mattered: an assignment used to be the far end of a permission bundle, and now it is a row in a set that grants point at.

    Scope — What a grant applies to: the unit, or exactly one item (scope_type + scope_id), never a nested body and never a choice between several items (D-097). Items are registered in grant_scope_type — event, person_group, role, and document deferred — and unit is the one row that is not item-scoped. Inheritance, stated once: a grant at the unit reaches every item of every type, because nothing is left between them and there is no owner to test; a grant on one item reaches that item and nothing else (D-099, D-102).

    Grant — The whole of the grants channel, and its shape is the whole definition: grantee + scope + one permission_id + effect + period. The grantee is a person or an audience, never both and never neither (D-042); the scope is the unit or one item; the effect is allow or deny; the period is valid_from / valid_to, live on the checked date. Plus provenance — granted_by, granted_at, grant_reason, and for lent authority is_delegated with delegated_from. One code per grant, not a set and not a level, which is what makes "what exactly does this person have?" a list of rows and "who else can do this?" the same list read the other way (D-099). See domain/06 §5.

    ~~Access grant (the levelled layer) — A period during which a person may act on one specific record with a level: revoked < view < contribute < manage < admin.~~ Withdrawn by D-099, which kept the row and deleted the ladder out of it. Nothing inherits a "level" now; a grant says yes-or-no about one code.

    Allow / deny, and the decision rule — Allowed when some live allow applies and no live deny applies. That is the whole of precedence, and everything else in the model is elaboration (domain/06 §7). A deny is absolute: it is checked before anything else, at any scope, so no item is local enough to override a suspension (D-013, whose surviving content is now exactly this — D-032's second half went with locality). A deny is legal at the unit as well as on an item, and is never conditioned on membership in either direction. - inside an expression is not a deny and the two are not interchangeable: subtracting a set narrows one grant's reach, while a deny defeats every grant that applies to the person, written anywhere by anyone — "she must not have this, whatever else she is given" is only ever a deny. The hygiene rule that comes with it is a report, not a constraint: a live deny whose grantee, scope and code match no live allow is dead weight, and a deny that denies nothing is how a stale restriction survives its reason.

    Channel — There are three, and one absolute deny in front of them: grants, guardianship, and resource ownership. ~~The fourth was role assignment,~~ and ~~a fifth, the membership gate, used to be described as "a condition on channels 1 and 3"~~ — both withdrawn by D-100: a role is now a set that grants point at rather than a source of authority, and the membership test is a term inside an expression rather than a clause in the resolver. How a check reaches a channel changed in round 6 and it is easy to state backwards. It used to be the permission's namespace — a code beginning dependent. went to guardianship. That silently skipped every guardian act whose code was an ordinary one, like a parent editing their child's address under person.contact.edit. Now guardianship is reached when the person the act is about is not the person performing it, which nobody has to remember, because the shape of the call decides (D-079). Corollary, and the part people get wrong: the guardianship channel can only say yes. When there is no guardianship, or the right is too low, it says nothing and the person's grants are still tried — otherwise a parent who is also the event registrar would be blocked from the roster by their own view over their child. Prohibitions are not made here; they are explicit denies.

    Audience — The organization's word is permission group; the table is audience; both mean one thing. An audience is an expression over named sets of people, stored as a tree of binary rows and aimed at by a grant (D-101). Author in sets, evaluate per person: a check asks about one person, so the resolver compiles the expression down to a predicate and never materializes a set. The named sets are the whole vocabulary — GROUP(x) (a dated roster), ROLE(x) (an office), AUDIENCE(code) (another saved expression), PERSON(x) (one person, and since D-105 the only way to write a set somebody typed by hand: PERSON(a) + PERSON(b)) — plus EVERYONE, MEMBERS, the two age operands OLDER(n) and YOUNGER(n), and the five event operands. GUARDIANS is not in that list and no longer is, in either spelling — it is not a selector, because guardianship is a channel and not a set; see Guardians are not a selector below, and D-113. The operators are + - * and round 12 added none of its own: the age operands are ordinary named operands composed by those three, so MEMBERS * OLDER(13) * YOUNGER(19) — thirteen to eighteen, a band that needs both — is an ordinary term and the language's vocabulary grew by decision while its grammar did not grow at all. The count is left unsaid here on purpose: the registry grew a row while round 10 was closing, when PERSON turned out to have been written by two decisions and seeded by neither (D-104), lost one while round 11 was closing (D-105), and grew two while round 12 was closing, when OLDER and YOUNGER were seeded (D-107) — which is why this page counts nothing: the number has moved three times in three rounds, and the one place a count belongs is the registry table itself (data-model/05 §8). ~~A group was filled by any combination of three optional arms: a filling rule, a hand-kept list, and the groups it included, unioned on the checked date~~ (D-091) — superseded by D-101, which turned the rule arm into the selectors and the manual arm into the LIST leaf, and deleted the inclusion arm; amended by D-103, which gave a rule a name to point at by letting an operand name another audience; and amended again by D-105, which deleted the LIST leaf and the table behind it with it, so the manual arm's last surviving spelling is a sum of PERSON(x) leaves inside the expression. What survived untouched is the cost D-091 named: a grant's reach is not visible in the row that grants it, so whoever edits a grant or an audience is shown the expanded set of people — for a specific item, since round 10 — rather than a name (domain/06 §6.2). Fail closed in every direction: an operand that does not resolve is the empty set, and an expression that cannot be evaluated reaches nobody. The direction is the half people get backwards, and the corpus's old word for it is retired: it used to say the - side was "loud", which names a volume where there is only a direction. What is true is that a failed operand on the + or * side narrows the set and a failed operand on the - side widens it, so EVERYONE - PERSON(x) with a dead x is fail-closed and locks out everybody, in one step, which is not a safe state (D-106). What round 12 added to this rule is a page, not a refusal: an audience carrying a leaf that does not resolve is listed for its owner, and no save is refused because a leaf is broken. And a missing value on the person being asked about is not a failure at all — a person with no birth date is simply not in OLDER(18), exactly as a person with no membership row is not in MEMBERS; the operand resolved perfectly well, and a report that conflated the two would page about every incomplete import in the country.

    Operand, operator, and the canonical form — An expression is built from + union, - difference, * intersection, with brackets. * and - bind tighter than +; * and - bind equally, left to right; brackets override — arithmetic-shaped on purpose, with one deliberate departure: - does not sit beneath *, because the first expression anybody writes in this language, MEMBERS - GROUP(schuzka2026-1) * COUNSELORS, is meant the left-to-right way (D-101). The canonical stored rendering prints every bracket, always — (MEMBERS - GROUP(schuzka2026-1)) * COUNSELORS — the editor rewriting what is typed into that form before it saves. Not for legibility: so that a grant read two years from now by someone who never learned the table above means what its author meant, and so that a later change of precedence changes nothing already stored. Storage is a binary tree, one row per node (operator + left_id + right_id) or leaf (selector_kind + selector_arg_id), with audience_id denormalized onto every row; one parent per node, never a DAG, because a shared node would let one edit silently widen every grant using it, and the tree is stored exactly as its author wrote it — one row per node, left to right, no rebalancing, because a rebalanced tree is a different expression than the one a person wrote and storing what was written is the reason the tree exists. Cycles are refused at the save — and since D-103 the guard is a reachability walk over the reference closure, not just the cheap parent-chain one, because a forest of trees pointing at each other does cycle. That walk is now the only structural guard there is: selector_depth_limit and the save-time depth bound it enforced are deleted, and an expansion is bounded by acyclicity alone — no bound on height, no bound ever asserted on breadth, and deliberately no cap on the read path, because a cap there would become the thing that decides who a grant reaches (D-105). "A check costs a bounded amount" is therefore no longer a property of this system, and what replaced it has changed twice since this page first wrote it down. ~~A standing report of the widest and deepest audiences~~ was the answer, and there is no such report now: D-109 retired the standing report this page had been promising, in favour of one tab on the permissions page, whose first section lists rules that are broken and whose second lists rules that merely look odd — neither section lists rules that are wide, because nothing produces that list any more, and the cost is carried by the stated size instead. It is a convention and not a guard either way (D-081). What an unbounded check costs is now accepted at a stated size rather than watched on a page: a few hundred people, a few dozen registrants on a typical event, a big camp on the order of two hundred (D-112), with the two rules that must survive any growth being a check compiles its expression to a predicate and never materializes the set and a listing is paginated. Round 13 generalised the idea and moved it one column over: is_system stops a screen from destroying a shipped row, and D-109 made the protection hold for organization-named things too — a role, a group, an audience nobody shipped — by ruling that nothing a rule may name is deleted at all, only retired as deprecated, hidden or disabled. It is not a sensitivity mark and not a category: applies_to and right_kind grade what a code does, and the catalog's 🔒 marks grade what it exposes (D-086).

    What is seeded — three answers, three different ones — Membership ships one row (member in membership_type, kept as a table so a second kind is a data change rather than a migration); no group ships at all, because a seeded group would be an invented one and the corpus names none, which is the same argument D-054 made about the unit vocabulary and which D-097 retired along with the vocabulary; and one role ships, counselors, marked is_system, because a permanent organization-wide population is the opposite of D-098's definition of a group. Plus the platform's own vocabulary: the permission codes, the grant_scope_type rows, the thirteen selector_type rows (PERSON was the last to arrive for two rounds — two decision entries had been writing it while the registry, copied from the evaluator's arm list, lacked it — and LIST the last to leave, deleted with its table and its seed row one round later by D-105, which left twelve for exactly one round; OLDER and YOUNGER arrived together after that, so the count went from twelve straight to fourteen, which was for exactly one round the only round this seed has had in which nothing was deleted (D-107), and their argument is an integer, the first seeded operand argument that is not a row in some table. The fourteen then fell to thirteen: GUARDIANS was never a selector and D-113 took its row out — see Guardians are not a selector below. Read the number as a history, not a total: twelve → fourteen → thirteen, three values in three rounds, and the registry has now moved four times across this corpus. That is precisely why no document may restate a count and expect it to hold, and why D-113's own rule was minted instead: every selector name that appears in a document must be a selector_type row — a count is a good alarm and a bad oracle), the two audiences everyone and all_members, and a grant template rather than a row for "every event's answers go to its organizers" — a bound operand is only legal on an item-scope grant, so the event-creation path instantiates that grant beside each event (D-102). Roles, groups and their rosters are runtime data in every other case (D-052).

    Effective permission — The union of everything a person may do right now, computed from live grants whose expressions contain them on the checked date, plus ownership and guardianship. Never a table, always derived, so it cannot drift out of sync with the grants that produce it. Two rounds narrowed that sentence, and round 14 has undone the first narrowing and left the second standing: ~~answers are now cached — see The nightly rebuild below — and the cache is a rebuild product rather than a store that can go stale on its own~~ — no answer is stored at all now (D-114), so derived means exactly what it meant before the cache existed: a check resolves against live rows every time, and the three things that may be kept are Compiled expression, The request memo and The batch reads below. The historical question still points at no table, because D-110 ruled that the record of whom a grant reached is the expression, not a snapshot of people. ~~which is why the historical questions point at F-7 instead of at a table~~ — F-7 is dissolved, not answered: ~~a nightly rebuild of cached answers and a nightly materialized effective-permission table turned out to be one job with two names~~ — and D-114 deleted one of the two things that were "one job", so what remains dissolved is the materialization alone: a effective_permission table now has to arrive on its own merits, at D-112's trigger, and be named for what it is.

    The nightly rebuild — ~~How every cached authorization answer stays true: one policy, three triggers — rebuilt (a) nightly in full, (b) immediately on any write to the ruleset, (c) on explicit request from the permissions page~~ (D-111). Deleted by D-114, and what it deleted was the thing underneath the policy: there is no stored answer to rebuild. Out went ~~permission_cache, its key (person_id, permission_code, scope_type, scope_id, at_bucket), the at_bucket date truncation~~ — no such table is in this schema, and the names in that struck list are the vocabulary of a deleted object, not columns anyone may add — along with the operand cache that sat beside it, keyed ~~(person, selector_kind, selector_arg_id[, item])~~, the nightly full job, the manual bump it and warm it, and both lists that decided which write bumped a version. That second key is the one worth staring at: the operand cache was keyed per person too, which is why no narrowing of a bump set could have rescued it and why the only cross-request caching D-114 leaves standing is a roster — keyed by a name, holding dated rows, with no person anywhere in the key. Why the entry stays printed: D-111's design hung on one sentence — "the line between those two lists is the entire safety property" — and the lists named eight kinds of write: six tables, the D-109 flag and a merge (access_grant, audience_operand, guardianship_permission, permission, role_holder, person_group_member), with membership on neither — while membership.terminate ⛔, held by safeguarding_officer, is the most safeguarding-shaped write the catalog has. An officer ends a member, MEMBERS shrinks, nothing rebuilds, and a cached true behind MEMBERS * … stands until morning; one tier lower the same shape, with event_registration declared not a ruleset write. The list could not be repaired by adding the missing row, because the correct invalidation set is every table any operand can read transitively — not a policy boundary but the read path itself, and a hand-maintained copy of the read path is a second source of truth about what an expression means (D-081, D-104). ~~What may therefore be a night stale is only what moves without anybody writing: a birthday crossing OLDER(n)/YOUNGER(n), the 1 January membership sweep, a period ending on its own date, attendance behind the event tiers. Every withdrawal a person performs is a write, so the safeguarding case is immediate and the calendar case waits at most one refresh~~ — "up to one night stale" is deleted from this corpus. A birthday, an expiring period, a registration write and a membership termination all move the answer at the instant they happen, so D-107's "I know, that the time will change, how the rules resolve and IT IS FINE" still stands with nothing left for it to be fine about. What survived: the version integer, its bump set cut from the eight kinds of write D-111 named — six tables, the D-109 flag and a merge — down to audience_operand and selector_type, and its meaning redefined in place so that "ruleset" now means the text of the rules and not anything an expression can read; and the rule that inside a check an expression compiles to a predicate on the person being asked and never materializes as a set — ~~the rebuild may be a set, the check may not~~ there is no job beside the check any more, and the check still may not. What the deleted cache was for is done by three things, each printed below.

    Compiled expression — The only thing in this system that may be cached across requests, and it is not an answer. Parse the stored tree once, resolve operand kinds once, emit the parameterized predicate once — keyed by the expression's root plus ruleset_version, and holding no person and no item, because both of those arrive as parameters. That is exactly why a compilation survives across people and an answer cannot: nothing in it depends on whom or where it is being asked about, so the only writes that can stale one are a change to the words and a change to the seeded vocabulary they are written in — which is the whole of ruleset_version's remaining bump set. A named roster may still be cached across requests too — ROLE(x) over role_holder, GROUP(x) over person_group_member, keyed by the name — on two conditions that are the entire difference from what was deleted: the key is the thing the write touches, so invalidation is local to the row that changed and never global, and the cache holds the dated rows, or the set with its validity ranges, never the set as of today, so the checked date is still applied at check time and a period ending at midnight needs no job to be seen. A roster is also a row somebody maintains and can point at, which an answer never was. And an answer may never be stored again: invariant 19, TIME-BOMB — no row, table or field may map a person, or a person-and-code, or a person-and-item, to a permission answer (D-114). Half of that guard is structural, which is the best kind this corpus has — the schema now has nowhere to put one — and the residue is honest: anyone who wants a cache back must arrive with a decision entry carrying a per-person invalidation handle, a stated list of watched tables, and that list derived from the operand registry rather than from a list of ideas; until then it is a convention, not a guard (D-081).

    The request memo — The part of the cache that was doing real work, kept, and confined to one request. One indexed read fetches the checked person's live grant rows; every code, item and date that request asks about is evaluated against them in memory, and a named operand that recurs — ROLE(leader), GROUP(cubs) — is resolved once per request instead of once per check. The deprecated | hidden | disabled flag is read the same way: once per request, memoized for that request, consulted live rather than compiled in. Lifetime is the request, so it has no invalidation policy and no staleness window at all — there is nothing left to invalidate, because nothing outlives the answer it produced (D-114).

    The batch reads — can_many(person, codes[], item?) and which_of(people[], code, item) — the entry points that replace what the cache was actually saving. A screen's worth of affordances is answered from one resolution; a list's worth of people from one pass. The cost that made a cache look necessary was never one check, it was the N+1 across a screen, and these remove it directly instead of deferring it to a job — which is the difference between the deleted cache and its replacement: mostly when the work happens. The per-item preview and the permissions tab are written against these, not against a loop of single checks (F-15, D-109). What is genuinely lost with the cache: a nightly job used to pre-pay exactly these screens, so the preview, the tab and the "who holds this" listing now do their work on open. At the size D-112 states that is affordable, and it is recorded as a dependency, not a measurement (D-114).

    Deprecated / hidden / disabled — The three states a named thing gets instead of being deleted, and the rule that keeps them three different things: only disabled changes an answer. Nothing a stored expression can name — a selector_type row, a role, a person_group, an audience — is ever deleted while a rule may name it (D-109):

    State Still resolves? Offered when writing a rule? Flagged?
    deprecated yes, unchanged no — not addable, not introducible by an edit yes, highlighted in the editor wherever it appears
    hidden yes, unchanged no no — not obsolete, just not everybody's business
    disabled no — the empty set no yes, and this is the only state that changes an answer

    The invariant that stops deprecated drifting into disabled: deprecating or hiding what an expression names must not change the set the expression resolves to. Disabling is the loudest switch in the module — it stops every rule naming that thing, in every scope, at once, and re-enabling restores all of them without anybody editing a grant — so disabling and re-enabling are audited writes that print the rules they touch, resolved before and after. And the flag takes effect now, which is the half that makes it true today — but not by the means this entry first gave: ~~flipping disabled bumps the ruleset version — one integer carried by every cache entry, see The nightly rebuild — so no cached true survives it~~. There is no cache entry to carry a version and none to survive (D-114): resolution consults the flag live, once per request and memoized for that request — a state is not compiled in to the compiled form, which is exactly why the flag left ruleset_version's bump set while still meaning now. The check worth writing survived the mechanism it was written against: if a flag ever is compiled into a cached form, "deprecating and hiding must not change the answer" keeps holding while "disabling takes effect now" silently stops being true (D-111). Its upside is the answer to a question this corpus kept meeting: "how do I turn off that whole naming without editing forty grants?" One exception, and it is the only way left to make a broken leaf: PERSON(x) names a person, and D-088 lets a redaction require the row to actually go — erasure wins. A person is not a selector, and no convenience owed to an expression author survives a former member's right to be deleted.

    The permissions tab — One place on the permissions page that lists rules worth a writer's attention, and it has exactly two sections. It replaced the standing reports about rules and their expressions (D-109: "No real report is needed, there can be a tab on the permissions page listing all the 'broken' permissions"). Broken — a live rule whose expression names a deprecated, hidden or disabled thing, or fails to resolve at all; this is D-106's failing-leaf list arriving on a page instead of a report, and after D-109 its dominant cause is a redacted person. Unusual — a rule that resolves correctly and merely looks odd (a grant naming ROLE(x) with no MEMBERS), which is F-13's population and is kept out of Broken on purpose: Broken says something happened to your rule behind your back, Unusual says your rule means what you wrote. The two sections make different claims about the writer and must not be merged. It is a convention, not a guard (D-081) in a stronger sense than the reports it replaced: nothing it lists is blocked, and nothing is refused because it is listed — what it buys is that a report has to be remembered and a tab is where the person already is. ~~The manual cache rebuild (The nightly rebuild, trigger (c)) lives on the same page.~~ — trigger (c) left with the cache: there is no answer to rebuild and nothing to warm, so what is left on that page is the tab and nothing else (D-114).

    Delegation — Temporarily granting someone your authority (a leader going on leave). Modeled as an ordinary grant with its provenance set: is_delegated, delegated_from naming who it came from, grant_reason, and a mandatory valid_to. Round 10 made it easier rather than changing it: because a grant is one permission code, "do not lend more than you hold" is a set-membership test instead of a comparison of levels. A deny cannot be lent and cannot be lent around, and system.admin is never delegated. ~~role.is_delegatable~~ went with the rest of the role configuration in D-100, which lists the surviving columns as code, name, description, is_system, sort_order — so lending is said on the grant, not on the role (an assumption; see the index in open-questions.md).

    Events¶

    Event — A dated occasion that people register for: a camp, a trip, a hike, a jamboree. It has a start and an end, an optional capacity, an optional fee, and a set of organizers. It has no status column — visibility is the permission model, and cancellation is a recorded fact rather than a state (D-064). ~~It belongs to exactly one unit~~, and ~~an event is an artifact of its unit rather than a child of it, so the unit's roles reach the unit's own events~~ — both withdrawn by D-102. Nothing owns an event. There is no event.org_unit_id and no can_event(); the people connected to an event are named by selectors that bind to it (see Bound operand). An event is addressed by being the item of an item-scope grant, which is the only way anything reaches it now.

    A weekly meeting is not an event. It never was: forty meetings a year are not forty events, so there is no recurrence, no series and no occurrence table (D-058, whose owned by exactly one unit half D-102 reversed and whose not an event half stands). What the meeting has now is a group to be in — a dated roster, not a calendar of evenings. "Was Jan at the meeting on 14 March?" is answerable by nobody: it used to be answerable by nobody in the events module and by unit_participation outside it, and the row that answered it was deleted by D-097. A group says who is in it between two dates, not who walked in on a given evening.

    Organizer — Someone running an event. A roster entry on the event, with an optional label ('lead', 'cook', 'driver') and an order — not an access_grant (D-062). ~~It is the event-scoped analogue of a position assignment~~ — withdrawn by D-100, which deleted positions; being an organizer is now analogous only to being a member of a group. Authority to act on the event no longer arrives from an owning unit: it arrives from one seeded grant template — event.answer.view, scoped to an event, aimed at the bound operand ORGANIZERS — instantiated per event by whoever creates it (D-102). An event created without that grant has organizers who can do nothing, which is why the events with no answer-view grant report exists.

    Registration — One person's answer about one event: attending, or not_attending. "Unanswered" is not a value — it is the absence of a registration row, so "who has not replied yet?" is the eligible set minus the registered set (D-059).

    Eligibility — Who may register. This is capability, not a rule engine: event.register_self is an ordinary permission, allowed by a live allow grant and blocked by any live deny, granted either on the unit or on the event itself; opening a camp to members is one access_grant naming an audience whose expression contains MEMBERS (D-060, D-099). ~~event.register_self requires contribute on the event~~ — withdrawn by D-099 with the level ladder; an age-restricted camp is a camp opened to a group.

    Waitlist — What happens when a full event gets a self-service registration: the person is written down and ordered, never refused. Organizers may exceed capacity outright, and the overrun is reported rather than prevented — the leader who knows there are two spare tents is trusted (D-065).

    Presence — Whether someone actually turned up, marked by an organizer after the fact. Three-valued: NULL (no register taken), true, false. For a camp this is the record of who was in the organization's care, and it cannot be reconstructed from registrations (D-067). Not the same as registration — a no-show is a registration with was_present = false. Because it is NULL until somebody marks it, there is no PRESENT selector: a selector that meant "came" would silently mean "was not marked absent" on every event nobody took a register for, so D-102 refuses it and the four tiers below are what you may name instead.

    The four attendance tiers — Four argument-less selectors that bind to the event a grant is scoped to, so a sentence written once reads correctly on every event it is copied to. From widest to narrowest:

    Selector Who it reaches, on the bound event
    ATTENDED anyone whose response is attending — asked, said yes, whether or not there was room
    PLACED those attending who were not waitlisted — the set the capacity actually let through
    CONFIRMED PLACED and is_confirmed — somebody has since looked at each of them
    ON_WAITLIST written down, ordered, not yet placed — waiting, not attending
    ORGANIZERS the event's organizer roster — the reason a per-event answer-view grant is seeded

    They are not one set with flags: ATTENDED is wider than PLACED precisely because a full event says yes to more people than it can seat, and CONFIRMED is narrower than PLACED because a seat is not a headcount (D-102). WHITELISTED is not one of these, and is not a selector at all — see EV-6.

    Money¶

    Payment request — A liability rendered payable: an amount, a recipient account, a start, and an optional expiry. The request, not the payment, is the entity the finance module is built around, because it is the only thing that connects "we expect money" to "money arrived" (D-075). It is not an invoice; the organization may also owe people invoices, which is still open.

    Variable symbol — The number a payer types into their bank, and the number the bank reports back against the money. Unique across every symbol ever issued, not merely across live ones, and taken from a sequence — never derived from a birth date, a member number, or anything else a stranger could guess. Anyone can type eleven digits into a payment form; guessing a symbol puts a stranger's money into a child's financial record.

    A QR code is a rendering, not data. It encodes the symbol, the amount and the recipient that already exist. Nothing stores it and nothing invalidates it.

    There is no card gateway. The organization collects, but by bank API rather than checkout: no card tokens, no disputes, no chargebacks, no processor reconciliation, and no pending or failed payment status — a bank transfer the bank has reported does not later fail. A refund is a second payment going the other way, never a reversal of the first.

    Unmatched money — A transfer whose symbol resolves to nothing live: mistyped, belonging to a cancelled event, already settled, or the wrong amount. It waits for a person (D-076). It is never auto-applied by payer identity — a parent with two children pays one amount is the normal case, not the edge case, and the fallback would be wrong on the common path.

    Communication¶

    Direct message — One person to one person. Everyone can send one; there is no permission for it (D-077).

    Broadcast — The only messaging permission, and an ordinary scoped one: a grant like any other. ~~Granted on a unit it reaches that unit, granted organization-wide it reaches everyone~~ — that pair was one code at two scopes, and after the sub-units went there is only one scope to have (D-097): held at the unit, message.broadcast reaches everyone. The narrower reading the pair used to buy now has to be a grant naming a group, reaching that roster — a shape D-099 makes available and D-077 could not have. What survives untouched is why there is no global broadcast flag: a permission that has to be granted is one that can be given to some people and refused to others, whereas the flag would be all-or-nothing and would leave a section leader choosing between an organization-wide power and typing the same sentence twelve times. Whether broadcast should ever be grantable on an item is messaging's open question, not the model's.

    Delivery channel — Email, push, or in-app, chosen by the recipient, not the sender. The sender chooses who; the recipient chooses how it arrives. This is why the system is the medium rather than a mail relay: a bounced email is a failed delivery on one channel, not a lost message.

    Age is not a sender concept — and it is not a recipient concept either, which is the part worth saying out loud. Nothing restricts who may address a child (D-077); what stops a private message to a child from being unnoticed is mirroring (D-082): the guardians get a copy, the sender is told before sending that they will, and no check in the resolver ever looks at an age.

    Mirroring — A copy of a direct message addressed to a minor, delivered to that minor's guardians whose right is view or write — the three-value right of the guardianship channel, not a grant level, which no longer exists (D-099) — fixed to that set at the moment of sending. Visibility, not approval: nobody has to agree before the message is sent. A guardian at none receives nothing, because none is the organization having already decided that this party sees nothing about the child — and a minor whose guardians are all none is the same alertable shape as a minor with no guardian at all, so it is reported together with it.

    Time¶

    Temporal row — Any row with valid_from / valid_to. valid_to IS NULL means "still true". All permission checks and history queries filter on these. The full pattern is specified in data-model/00-conventions.md.

    Point-in-time query — A question of the form "who/what was true on date D?" — "Who was in the group schuzka2026-1 on 1 June 1998?", "Was this person a member when they attended camp?" These are first-class requirements, not an afterthought, because you have four decades of history to preserve. Note that the answer now depends on which named set the question is about, and there are only two kinds left to be about: a group answers it from its dated roster, and an audience cannot answer it at all — its expression is evaluated live, and its one past is the audit trail. That includes a name typed straight into the expression: since D-105 a PERSON(x) leaf carries no dates of its own (the dated rows it replaced are deleted), so "was she in this audience on 1 June 2024?" is a recomputation over audit payloads that expire after 24 months (D-088), and the same is true of D-103's nested audiences.

    As-of vs audit time — Two different times, both needed. As-of is the date the fact was true about the world (a membership that ran 1990–2010). Audit time is when the row was written in the system. A membership backdated during a 2026 data-entry session has as-of 1990–2010 and audit time 2026. Do not conflate them.
