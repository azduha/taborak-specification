    Glossary — Authorization terms¶

    The authorization half of the glossary: the words a grant, an audience or an expression is written out of. Terms defined elsewhere in the glossary — person, group and office, guardianship — are not repeated here.

    Authorization¶

    Permission — The atomic unit of authority: a coded capability such as membership.request.approve or person.view. Permissions are the only things the authorization layer actually checks. Each row carries applies_to (unit, or unit_and_item — where the code may be granted at all, checked when a grant is saved, so a grant of event.create written on an event is refused rather than evaluated) and right_kind (read | write, which exists for exactly one customer: the guardianship channel). Codes are stable identifiers, never display strings, and there is no <resource>.admin wildcard and no prefix match (D-068). Specified in data-model/05 §2.

    Role — An office, not a bundle: see Organization. The one sentence people get wrong — a role confers nothing, and the codes it carries are the live grants naming ROLE(x). Roles are runtime data: the organization invents them, composes them out of grants and deletes the unused ones, except for the one seeded role (D-052).

    Scope — What a grant applies to: the unit, or exactly one item (scope_type + scope_id), never a nested body and never a choice between several items. Items are registered in grant_scope_type — event, person_group, role, and document deferred — and unit is the one row that is not item-scoped. Inheritance, stated once: a grant at the unit reaches every item of every type, because nothing is left between them and there is no owner to test; a grant on one item reaches that item and nothing else (domain/06 §2).

    Grant — The whole of the grants channel, and its shape is the whole definition: grantee + scope + one permission_id + effect + period, in access_grant. The grantee is a person or an audience, never both and never neither; the scope is the unit or one item; the effect is allow or deny; the period is valid_from / valid_to, live on the checked date. Plus provenance — granted_by, granted_at, grant_reason, and for lent authority is_delegated with delegated_from. One code per grant, not a set and not a level, which is what makes "what exactly does this person have?" a list of rows and "who else can do this?" the same list read the other way. Nothing adds a membership test to a grant — see Selector below. See domain/06 §5.

    Allow / deny, and the decision rule — Allowed when some live allow applies and no live deny applies. That is the whole of precedence, and everything else in the model is elaboration (domain/06 §7). A deny is absolute: it is checked before anything else, at any scope, so no item is local enough to override a suspension. A deny is legal at the unit as well as on an item, and is never conditioned on membership in either direction. - inside an expression is not a deny and the two are not interchangeable: subtracting a set narrows one grant's reach, while a deny defeats every grant that applies to the person, written anywhere by anyone — "she must not have this, whatever else she is given" is only ever a deny. The hygiene rule that comes with it is a report, not a constraint: a live deny whose grantee, scope and code match no live allow is dead weight, and a deny that denies nothing is how a stale restriction survives its reason.

    Channel — There are three, and one absolute deny in front of them: grants, guardianship, and resource ownership. Guardianship is reached when the person the act is about is not the person performing it, which nobody has to remember, because the shape of the call decides — not the permission's name, since a parent editing their child's address is an ordinary person.contact.edit and a name-based rule would have skipped it (D-079). Corollary, and the part people get wrong: the guardianship channel can only say yes. When there is no guardianship, or the right is too low, it says nothing and the person's grants are still tried — otherwise a parent who is also the event registrar would be blocked from the roster by their own view over their child. Prohibitions are not made here; they are explicit denies. Roles and groups are not channels: they are sets that grants point at.

    Audience — The organization's word is permission group; the table is audience; both mean one thing. An audience is an expression over named sets of people, stored as a tree of binary rows and aimed at by a grant (D-101). Author in sets, evaluate per person: a check asks about one person, so the resolver compiles the expression down to a predicate and never materializes a set. The named sets are the whole vocabulary — GROUP(x) (a dated roster), ROLE(x) (an office), AUDIENCE(code) (another saved expression), PERSON(x) (one person, and the only way to write a set somebody typed by hand: PERSON(a) + PERSON(b)) — plus EVERYONE, MEMBERS, the two age operands OLDER(n) and YOUNGER(n), and the five event operands. The operators are + - *; the age operands are ordinary named operands composed by those three, so MEMBERS * OLDER(13) * YOUNGER(19) is an ordinary term. GUARDIANS is not in that list, in either spelling: guardianship is a channel and not a set, so a kept list of contact adults is a GROUP(x) (D-113). A count of the operands belongs only in the registry table itself (data-model/05 §8). Fail closed in every direction: an operand that does not resolve is the empty set, and an expression that cannot be evaluated reaches nobody. The direction is the half people get backwards: a failed operand on the + or * side narrows the set and a failed operand on the - side widens it, so EVERYONE - PERSON(x) with a dead x is fail-closed and locks out everybody, in one step, which is not a safe state. An audience carrying a leaf that does not resolve is listed for its owner, and no save is refused because a leaf is broken. A missing value on the person being asked about is not a failure at all — a person with no birth date is simply not in OLDER(18), exactly as a person with no membership row is not in MEMBERS. Because a grant's reach is not visible in the row that grants it, whoever edits a grant or an audience is shown the expanded set of people — for a specific item — rather than a name (domain/06 §6.2).

    Selector — One operand of an audience expression: a named operand, which means a row in the seeded registry selector_type (code, takes_argument, binds_to_item, argument_type). The parser reads its reserved words from that table, the editor builds its dropdown from it, and the save path checks arity against it. A row is not removable out from under a rule: since D-109 a named thing a rule may still name is retired as deprecated or hidden, both of which keep resolving, and only disabled changes the answer — see Deprecated / hidden / disabled. What is required of an operand is that it be named, in the registry and not merely in a sentence — and that holds of prose too: every selector name that appears in these documents must be a selector_type row, which is what a count is a good alarm and a bad oracle looks like. The registry is a closed language: every row ships with the platform, the table is organization-read-only, and a new operand is a decision entry plus a seed row, never a row somebody types into a screen. That closure is the line that keeps D-060's refusal of a rule engine true: OLDER(13) is a row, and AGE >= 13 is not one and cannot be, because the language has no >= anywhere to put it. Eligibility stays capability: the system applies no gate on its own; a person may write one with the vocabulary that ships, and membership is never implicit: it is a term the writer puts in, so requiring it is MEMBERS * ROLE(x), waiving it for a whole office is ROLE(x) alone, and waiving it for one named person is MEMBERS * ROLE(x) + PERSON(y). A grant naming ROLE(x) with no MEMBERS authorizes a lapsed member, silently and forever, and nothing enforces the term — a convention, not a guard (see The permissions tab, and the seeded grants and templates that carry MEMBERS). Why: the resolver applies no membership test of its own at any scope, so the term is either written into the expression or it is nowhere (D-100).

    Operand The set, on the checked date Argument
    EVERYONE Any person, including those with no account and no membership none
    MEMBERS Holds a membership live on the checked date none
    OLDER(n) / YOUNGER(n) n or older / strictly under n, both read from the birth date on the checked date — so this set gains and loses people at midnight with no write, and can(..., on date) for a past date answers for that past date.     PERSON(x) One named person — a set of one, which is a set, so it is an ordinary row of the registry like every other operand. Summed, it is also the hand-typed set: PERSON(a) + PERSON(b) a person

    Refused, and worth naming because a reader reaches for them: PRESENT(e) — a predicate over the check-in box would revoke a leader's access to their own camp photos whenever somebody forgot to tick it; WHITELISTED — no stored state to evaluate (EV-6). Both are refusals rather than type errors, and neither is seeded (D-107).

    A hand-written set is a sum of PERSON leaves. It inherits the properties a named set needs: belonging to this one expression, invisible in the group browser, dying with the audience, and editable one name at a time — (GROUP(kitchen) + PERSON(honza)) - PERSON(a) - PERSON(b). What has no spelling is a date on a name: a sum of leaves carries no valid_from / valid_to, so a time-windowed exception is a second grant with its own period, and a reviewer asking what does this person have? must find two grants rather than one audience. Since every hand-named person is a selector_arg_id, and that column deliberately has no foreign key, a merge must repoint the leaves that name the absorbed person (F-10).

    Names are one namespace across role, person_group and audience.code, and every selector_type.code is a reserved name — a group called MEMBERS is refused at the save rather than silently rewriting every expression in the system. No single constraint spans three tables, so the write path carries the rule and an integrity check asserts the write path exists (D-081). What is stored is always kind + id, so a rename never rewrites a grant. Bare names do not resolve to audiences: AUDIENCE(veterans) is written out (D-103). And nothing a stored expression can name is ever deleted while a rule may name it — a selector_type row, a role, a person_group, an audience is given a state instead of a DELETE — because the namespace has to be stable against renames, deletions and quiet retirements alike, all three of which rewrite what a rule means without anybody editing the rule.

    Operand, operator, and the canonical form — An expression is built from + union, - difference, * intersection, with brackets. * and - bind tighter than +; * and - bind equally, left to right; brackets override — arithmetic-shaped on purpose, with one deliberate departure: - does not sit beneath *, because the first expression anybody writes in this language, MEMBERS - GROUP(schuzka2026-1) * COUNSELORS, is meant the left-to-right way. The canonical stored rendering prints every bracket, always — (MEMBERS - GROUP(schuzka2026-1)) * COUNSELORS — the editor rewriting what is typed into that form before it saves, so that a grant read two years from now means what its author meant and a later change of precedence changes nothing already stored. Storage is a binary tree, one row per node (operator + left_id + right_id) or leaf (selector_kind + selector_arg_id), with audience_id denormalized onto every row; one parent per node, never a DAG, because a shared node would let one edit silently widen every grant using it. The tree is stored exactly as its author wrote it — one row per node, left to right, no rebalancing. Cycles are refused at the save by a reachability walk over the reference closure, not just the cheap parent-chain one, because a forest of trees pointing at each other does cycle. That walk is the only structural guard: an expansion is bounded by acyclicity alone — no bound on height, no bound on breadth, and deliberately no cap on the read path, because a cap there would become the thing that decides who a grant reaches. So "a check costs a bounded amount" is not a property of this system, and what is accepted instead is a stated size: a few hundred people, a few dozen registrants on a typical event, a big camp on the order of two hundred (D-112), with the two rules that must survive any growth being a check compiles its expression to a predicate and never materializes the set and a listing is paginated. Nothing lists the widest or deepest audiences — the permissions tab lists only what is broken and what looks odd — and that is a convention and not a guard either way. See F-21.

    AUDIENCE(code) — The operand that names another saved audience's expression, evaluated live. It is what makes a rule namable: GROUP() and ROLE() name rosters, so without it the advice "name the composite once and aim grants at it" had nothing to name, and it is the only arm whose argument is a decision about access rather than a roster — "the same people that grant may see the fee ledger, plus the accountant". A reference names a code (optional, unique-where-set) and may not point at an audience with none; an audience may not be ended or deleted while a live grant, or a live audience reachable from a live grant, names it, because "ended" would otherwise be a way to silently empty an operand and an empty operand behind a - makes the result wider; naming an audience requires reading it and grants no read of it to the people inside it. What to do instead of deleting one: retire it as deprecated or hidden, both of which still resolve, and use disabled only when emptying it is the intent — see Deprecated / hidden / disabled. What it costs: editing a referenced audience is a retrospective edit to every grant naming it, which is why saving such an edit shows which grants name it, at what depth, and the set it resolves to before it commits. The record of whom a grant reached is the expression, the preview is logged as a rendering on a date, and a history of inner expressions is not kept; so "who could see this child's data in 2024?" is reconstructable when the grant pointed at a group, whose membership rows are dated, and is not owed when it pointed at a nested audience (D-110). Recomputation for a past date stays possible and is never promised.

    Bound operand — The argument-less form of an operand that means relative to the item being checked: PLACED on the event under check, not PLACED(1204). The form exists to kill a specific flaw — one grant per event with the event's own id typed into it is a thing you do 400 times and forget on the 401st — and two rules keep it safe, both enforced when the grant is saved, with the operand named in the refusal: a bound operand is legal only on an item-scope grant (at the unit there is nothing to bind it to) and only on a matching item type (PLACED on a group grant is refused, because that item is not an event — selector_type.argument_type checked against grant_scope_type). The consequence: the set a grant reaches is per-item, so the editor's preview is computed for an item and says so on screen. A Compiled expression holds no item precisely because the item arrives as a parameter, so the item is bound at resolution, not at compilation — and nothing resolved while checking one item (a bound operand's set, a preview's expansion) may ever be served for another. Anything that does cache a resolution, including a roster cache or an expansion keyed per (audience, item), is keyed by the item or is wrong on the 401st event. selector_type.binds_to_item is true exactly for the five event operands — ATTENDED, PLACED, CONFIRMED, ON_WAITLIST, ORGANIZERS — which cannot be evaluated without the event the grant sits on, and is false for everything else. A bound operand never meant the subject of the item and never will; there is no third binding kind.

    admin — A word to avoid: readers bring three different meanings to it and only one of them exists here. There is no grant level (access_level, permission_level are not in this schema, and a grant does not rank) and no per-resource wildcard (person.admin, event.admin are not codes and nothing matches a prefix). What survives of "can do everything": system.admin — the only wildcard permission, seeded applies_to = unit, so it can never arrive through an item grant — and roles and audiences, the bundles the organization composes, where "someone who can do everything about events" is written out as a couple of dozen grant rows rather than implied by a resolver rule.

    is_system — The flag meaning the platform shipped this, so a screen must not destroy it. It sits on permission (a shipped code can neither be deleted nor renamed), on role (counselors ships with it so an editor refuses to delete the set every safeguarding convention hangs from), on the two seeded audiences everyone and all_members, and on every selector_type row — where its job is specifically to make the registry refuse an edit, which is what makes "a closed language" true of a table rather than merely asserted. The same protection extends to organization-named things differently: a role, a group or an audience nobody shipped is never deleted at all, only retired as deprecated, hidden or disabled. It is not a sensitivity mark and not a category: applies_to and right_kind grade what a code does, and the catalog's 🔒 marks grade what it exposes (D-086).

    What is seeded — Membership ships one row (member in membership_type, kept as a table so a second kind is a data change rather than a migration); no group ships at all, because a seeded group would be an invented one; and one role ships, counselors, marked is_system, because a permanent organization-wide population is the opposite of what a group is. Plus the platform's own vocabulary: the permission codes, the grant_scope_type rows, the selector_type rows, and the two audiences everyone and all_members. "Every event's answers go to its organizers" ships as a grant template rather than a row — a bound operand is only legal on an item-scope grant, so the event-creation path instantiates that grant beside each event. Roles, groups and their rosters are runtime data in every other case (D-052).

    Effective permission — The union of everything a person may do right now, computed from live grants whose expressions contain them on the checked date, plus ownership and guardianship. Never a table, always derived, so it cannot drift out of sync with the grants that produce it: a check resolves against live rows every time, and the only things that may be kept are a Compiled expression, The request memo and The batch reads. The historical question points at no table either, because the record of whom a grant reached is the expression, not a snapshot of people; a materialized effective_permission table would have to arrive on its own merits, when the stated size says it must, and be named for what it is (F-7).

    Compiled expression — The only thing in this system that may be cached across requests, and it is not an answer. Parse the stored tree once, resolve operand kinds once, emit the parameterized predicate once — keyed by the expression's root plus ruleset_version, and holding no person and no item, because both of those arrive as parameters. Nothing in it depends on whom or where it is being asked about, so the only writes that can stale one are a change to the words and a change to the seeded vocabulary they are written in: ruleset_version's entire bump set is audience_operand and selector_type. A named roster may also be cached across requests — ROLE(x) over role_holder, GROUP(x) over person_group_member, keyed by the name — on two conditions: the key is the thing the write touches, so invalidation is local to the row that changed and never global, and the cache holds the dated rows, or the set with its validity ranges, never the set as of today, so the checked date is still applied at check time and a period ending at midnight needs no job to be seen. An answer may never be stored: invariant 19, TIME-BOMB — no row, table or field may map a person, or a person-and-code, or a person-and-item, to a permission answer (D-114). Half of that guard is structural — the schema has nowhere to put one — and the residue is honest: anyone who wants a cache back must arrive with a decision entry carrying a per-person invalidation handle and a stated list of watched tables derived from the operand registry rather than from a list of ideas; until then it is a convention, not a guard.

    The request memo — In-process only, and confined to one request. One indexed read fetches the checked person's live grant rows; every code, item and date that request asks about is evaluated against them in memory, and a named operand that recurs — ROLE(leader), GROUP(cubs) — is resolved once per request instead of once per check. The deprecated | hidden | disabled flag is read the same way: once per request, memoized for that request, consulted live rather than compiled in. Lifetime is the request, so it has no invalidation policy and no staleness window at all (D-114).

    The batch reads — can_many(person, codes[], item?) and which_of(people[], code, item) — the entry points a screen's affordances and a list's people are answered from: one resolution, one pass. The cost that made a cache look necessary was never one check, it was the N+1 across a screen. The per-item preview and the permissions tab are written against these, not against a loop of single checks, and they therefore do their work on open; at the size stated above that is affordable, and it is recorded as a dependency, not a measurement (F-15).

    Deprecated / hidden / disabled — The three states a named thing gets instead of being deleted, and the rule that keeps them three different things: only disabled changes an answer. Nothing a stored expression can name — a selector_type row, a role, a person_group, an audience — is ever deleted while a rule may name it (D-109):

    State Still resolves? Offered when writing a rule? Flagged?
    deprecated yes, unchanged no — not addable, not introducible by an edit yes, highlighted in the editor wherever it appears
    hidden yes, unchanged no no — not obsolete, just not everybody's business
    disabled no — the empty set no yes, and this is the only state that changes an answer

    The invariant that stops deprecated drifting into disabled: deprecating or hiding what an expression names must not change the set the expression resolves to. Disabling is the loudest switch in the module — it stops every rule naming that thing, in every scope, at once, and re-enabling restores all of them without anybody editing a grant — so disabling and re-enabling are audited writes that print the rules they touch, resolved before and after. The flag takes effect now because resolution consults it live, once per request and memoized for that request: a state is not compiled in to the compiled form, which is why the flag left ruleset_version's bump set while still meaning now. The check worth writing: if a flag ever is compiled into a cached form, "deprecating and hiding must not change the answer" keeps holding while "disabling takes effect now" silently stops being true. Its upside is the answer to "how do I turn off that whole naming without editing forty grants?" One exception, and it is the only way left to make a broken leaf: PERSON(x) names a person, and redaction can require the row to actually go — erasure wins. A person is not a selector, and no convenience owed to an expression author survives a former member's right to be deleted.

    The permissions tab — One place on the permissions page that lists rules worth a writer's attention, and it has exactly two sections. Broken — a live rule whose expression names a deprecated, hidden or disabled thing, or fails to resolve at all; its dominant cause is a redacted person. Unusual — a rule that resolves correctly and merely looks odd (a grant naming ROLE(x) with no MEMBERS), kept out of Broken on purpose: Broken says something happened to your rule behind your back, Unusual says your rule means what you wrote. The two sections make different claims about the writer and must not be merged. It is a convention, not a guard in a strong sense: nothing it lists is blocked, and nothing is refused because it is listed — what it buys is that a report has to be remembered and a tab is where the person already is. What is on that page is the tab and nothing else.

    Delegation — Temporarily granting someone your authority (a leader going on leave). Modeled as an ordinary grant with its provenance set: is_delegated, delegated_from naming who it came from, grant_reason, and a mandatory valid_to. Because a grant is one permission code, "do not lend more than you hold" is a set-membership test. A deny cannot be lent and cannot be lent around, and system.admin is never delegated. Lending is said on the grant, not on the role (an assumption; see the index in open-questions.md).
