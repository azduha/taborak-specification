    Open Questions¶

    Everything still unresolved — fifteen questions, each with its schema impact, what changes once you answer, ordered roughly by how much they block.

    Marked: 🔴 blocking something else · 🟡 important · 🟢 can be deferred.

    A question that has been answered is not kept here. Its entry is deleted; what remains is one row in § R. Resolved naming the question, the decision that settled it and what the answer cost, and the reasoning lives in that decision entry. Nothing is silently dropped: a row in § R carries a short anchor, so a link written years ago against F-7 still lands somewhere true.

    The section letters are names, not a sequence. Sections B, C and D held only closed questions and are gone, but the ids they held — B-2, C-5, D-9 — are still how § R means them, and E-6 is still the question it was when it was filed.

    § I lists every assumption the documentation is standing on; § How to answer says which of these is worth answering next.

    A. Blocking the core design¶

    A-4 (remainder) 🟢 The organizational calendar¶

    Answered. The fee expires on 1 January (D-047) and the renewal window is 60 days (D-048).

    What is left is not a question but a discipline. Both values live in membership_type as columns — expiry_month, expiry_day, renewal_grace_days — so either can be moved by an UPDATE. The one way to lose that property is a scheduled job or a front end that contains the literal 60 or 1. Worth one line in code review: do these values come from the row?

    See: data-model/03 §1, domain/05 §6

    E. Guardianship¶

    E-6 🟡 A guardian may appoint a guardian¶

    Raised by closing E-5. D-073 lets a guardian add another guardian, so anyone with authority over a child can hand that same authority to a second person with no approval from the organization. The ordinary case is a mother adding the father. The design does not prevent the non-ordinary case; it attributes it — who added whom, when, from which account, audited, with the other party notified.

    The open question is whether attribution is enough, and specifically whether adding a guardian over a minor should also notify somebody at the organization beyond the audit log. A child gaining a second legal stranger with write over their records is not the same kind of event as an adult adding a co-parent.

    Recommendation. Notify a safeguarding contact on guardianship.assign where the subject is a minor, and nothing else. Notification is visibility, not approval, so the parties still do not need the organization's permission — which is the whole point of D-073.

    Impact. One notification rule. No schema change.

    E-3 🟢 Guardian acting for an adult child¶

    Question. Can a parent register their adult child for an event, with the child's consent?

    Recommendation. An explicit consent-based delegation, not a guardianship — the legal basis differs and access_grant.is_delegated already supports it.

    Note. Now that grants can target audiences (D-042), the event-wide version of this question — "register everyone in this group" — is answered without touching guardianship at all. What remains is the individual case.

    F. Authorization¶

    F-8 🟢 Retention policy for access_log¶

    Question. How long do the login and authorization-decision rows live?

    This entry used to answer itself, in the wrong direction. It said: partition monthly, archive to cold storage, never deletion. That is right about the rows and wrong about everything else, and the mistake is the same one D-088 made salient for audit_log: a retention policy stated as one number silently decides what the row contains, which is the part with the person data in it.

    Proposed — and it is a proposal awaiting you, not a position these documents hold: access_log takes the same window as audit_log's payloads, 24 months, with rows kept and their contents emptied on the same schedule, because a trail of who was allowed to see what that outlives the trail of what was actually changed answers the interesting half of an incident and not the other half. Monthly partitioning survives as the mechanism; it is how the window gets applied cheaply, and it was recommended long before anybody decided on a window.

    Why the question is structurally weaker here than it is for audit_log. 05-authorization §9 keeps the contents of a read out of this table to begin with — it records that a permission was exercised, not what was on the screen — so there is no payload-expiry argument and no long-history loss to weigh. What remains is the ordinary tension: a security review wants the login trail back to the start of the system, and a privacy review wants a record of somebody's late-night browsing to stop existing eventually. Inverting the arithmetic is worth noticing: the original "never deletion" made this table outlive the person record it describes; the proposal makes it shorter-lived than the business rows, which is a different objection and a defensible one.

    Needed. Your confirmation of the 24 months, and the same legal review assumption 49 registers — which now has both clocks in front of it rather than one.

    Impact if unanswered. Neither table can be shrunk afterwards without a migration over the two largest tables in the system, and the first time anybody wants to answer "how long have people been able to read this?" the honest answer is that nobody wrote down when the logging started.

    See: data-model/05 §9, D-088.

    F-9 🟢 Should membership_type stay a table with one row?¶

    Question. D-037 kept membership_type as a table with a single seeded member row, on the principle that vocabularies are rows and not enums — at the cost of an FK that always holds one value.

    Impact. A deliberate trade, not an oversight. Worth confirming it is the trade you want, since it is the one place the design pays a structural cost for a hypothetical future.

    See: data-model/03 §1

    F-10 🟡 Which invariants the ORM cannot express, and who writes those¶

    Question. D-072 moved the schema to an ORM. Most of what the deleted DDL asserted an ORM will generate from a model definition. Four shapes it generally cannot, and each appears dozens of times in these documents.

    The four are not equally bad, and round 10 moved them in opposite directions, which is the reason this entry changed colour. Shapes 1, 2 and 3 gained instances — the round that deleted the hierarchy spent itself inventing new temporal tables and a new expression language. Shape 4 lost its only instance, and shape 4 was the entire reason this question was red.

    1. No two open rows overlap — the temporal invariant under memberships, guardianships, grants, and — new — person_group_member (D-098) and role_holder (D-100). On PostgreSQL this needs an exclusion constraint over a range built from two columns. The two new ones are stricter than the idiom is usually generated: both state "may not overlap, counted over all rows and not only the open ones", which is the opposite of the partial-index habit this shape normally pairs with. An ORM that generates the familiar "one open row per key" version silently permits the overlapping closed row that a group member's history is full of, and the imported corpus guarantees one.
    2. Unique, but only among rows satisfying a condition — "one open request per person and type", "one primary contact among open rows", and new: audience.code unique where set (D-103), because naming an audience is optional and most audiences are a grant's private addressee list. These need a partial unique index; a plain unique constraint over the same columns is a different and wrong rule, forbidding the second NULL that history requires — and here it would forbid every unnamed audience, which is most of them. Gone: "one live exposure per unit and assignment", with scope_exposure (D-097).
    3. Anything whose answer comes from another table or from the resolver — this is where round 10 worked, and it had five instances where it had three; round 11 added a sixth, and it is the first one whose subject is a column that deliberately has no key to follow:
    4. Gone: the holder limit (max_holders, deleted with positions, D-100), the nesting rules and the exposure direction (both D-097). Naming them keeps the count honest.
    5. Kept: per-scheme identifier uniqueness (D-080 item 1 is this class), and one current value per (person, definition), whose condition lives in attribute_definition.allow_multiple — a uniqueness rule some definitions want and others forbid (data-model/09-attributes.md §3). It stays the cleanest example in the list: the same two columns are unique for one definition and repeatable for the next, so no partial index can be written without knowing which definitions exist at runtime.
    6. New — requiredness conditioned on another table: an audience_operand leaf must carry selector_arg_id exactly when its selector's takes_argument is true, and must leave it null exactly when the operand binds to the item (D-102). The condition is a value in selector_type, three tables away from the row it constrains, so NOT NULL cannot state it and the arity check has to.
    7. New — a check that reads another table: an AUDIENCE(...) leaf may not point at an audience whose code is null (D-103; §11 check 12 of data-model/05). Nothing about the operand row tells you this; only the row it references does. The same class carries the name namespace, now spanning role, person_group and audience.code with every selector_type.code reserved against it — no single constraint spans three tables and a fourth table's values, so the write path carries it and check 12 only asserts that the write path exists.
    8. New, and the mirror image of the rest: two rows that must coexist. Creating an event obliges an instantiated event.answer.view grant aimed at ORGANIZERS (D-102) — one row in one table requiring a row in another, in one transaction, with nothing in either schema stating the debt. A constraint can forbid a missing child or an orphan parent; it cannot make an INSERT in event write a row in access_grant. The backstop is the events with no answer-view grant report, and an organizer whose event arrived without its grant is a person with a title and no permissions — which reads as a bug report, not a constraint violation. The legacy import owes one per event row, so the first occurrence of this is expected in bulk, not at the margins.
    9. New in round 11, and the first whose subject is a column with no key on purpose: a person merge or a redaction must repoint every audience_operand leaf whose selector_arg_id names the absorbed or erased person (D-105). The column carries no foreign key by design (D-011's accepted trade-off), so there is no cascade to inherit and no key for an ORM to walk: the merge reassignment table gains the row, the erasure path gains the scan, and both have to reach all five argument kinds — person, role, person_group, event, audience. Nothing in the schema notices a missed leaf. The nightly orphan check becomes the backstop rather than the only mechanism, and the failure is this module's quietest one: a subtraction that stops subtracting, because a leaf resolving to nobody is the empty set and an empty set behind a - makes the result wider. Until this round the hand-named people lived in a table that did have a key, which is the only reason the instance is new rather than long overdue.
    10. A column that may not change once it is set — its only named instance is gone: org_unit.parent_unit_id (D-036) was deleted by D-097, so this shape is currently empty. That is a fact about the corpus, not a proof, and the two candidates left are worth naming because they were previously hiding behind the dead one: permission.code — the catalog's own words are "adding one is free, renaming one is a migration", and nothing in a schema enforces a migration — and audience.code once something names it, since AUDIENCE(code) resolves through that column and a rename rewrites every expression that reached it, which is exactly what rule 5 of the name namespace promises does not happen for names.

    What made this shape different from 1–3 was that there is no report, and that is the half worth re-examining rather than inheriting. A reparent left no offending row: every row stayed individually valid while the meaning of a subtree's history moved under it, and the path the damage would show up in was itself derived and would simply be recomputed to match. audience.code has a report — which grants name this audience, and at what depth — and D-103 requires the editor to show it before saving an edit. So shape 4 is no longer the shape with no evidence, provided that listing is cheap: see F-16. permission.code needs the same query over a different table and nobody has written either. This entry is 🟡 rather than 🔴 on exactly that condition, and 🟡 is a bet, not a measurement.

    Why this is a question and not a task. The fallback an ORM offers for all four is validation in the model layer, and data-model/00 §6a explains why that is not the same guarantee: an import, a data fix, and a second client all bypass it. The legacy import is the specific case that makes this urgent rather than theoretical — the corpus arrives through exactly the path that model-layer validation does not cover, and an overlapping membership written by the importer is nearly impossible to diagnose afterwards, because the two rows look like two memberships and only one of them was ever real. Round 10 gave the import two more of these: consecutive dated periods for the same person in whatever table ends up holding them (shape 1), and one answer-view grant per event row it creates (shape 3) — a debt the importer owes in bulk, whose first failures will therefore also arrive in bulk. Add the third new graph rule to shape 3's list while the section is open: a cycle across audiences is refused at save by a reachability walk over the reference closure (D-103), which is a rule about a graph spanning two tables and is the least constraint-shaped thing in the model.

    Needed. A decision per shape — hand-written migration, database check, or application check plus report — before the first migration is generated, not discovered during one. The recommendation in data-model/00 §6a is that shapes 1 and 2 are hand-written migrations, and that this is the condition on which using an ORM is safe rather than an argument against using one. Shape 3 needs one more thing than a decision: its checks are the only enforcement the selector language has, so the arity and null-argument checks have to be written before the first expression is saved, and the §11 list in data-model/05 — renumbered for round 10, several checks having lost their subjects — is a review instruction rather than a test.

    Impact if unanswered. Not a crash. A system whose constraints are quietly absent, whose integrity checks (every "Invariants to verify" section) then start reporting rows, and whose first report is read as a data-quality problem rather than as the missing guarantee arriving on schedule.

    See: data-model/00 §6a, appendix/erd.md's inventory, which marks every temporal entity.

    F-11 🟢 Does the identity reviewer need to read the person record to settle a claim?¶

    Question. D-089 routes a colliding sign-up to identity_reviewer and resolves it with person.merge. The seed role holds person.merge, import.view and import.apply — and not person.view. So a reviewer decides "is this the same child?" from match signals, names and dates, without opening either record.

    Why this is a question now and was not one before. Merging already needed a human, but the alternative to merging was doing nothing: a wrong call left two rows side by side, visible in the nightly duplicate report, correctable next quarter. The activation gate changed the stakes. A reviewer who rejects a hypothesis lets an account go live; one who confirms it joins two lives, including a login, into one row. Both directions now have a person waiting on the far side of the answer, which is a different job from tidying a queue, and a job the permission set does not obviously cover.

    Positions. - Leave it — the signals are the decision, and a reviewer who needs to read both records is a reviewer who is about to merge on a hunch anyway. Cheapest, and consistent with "identity is what the match says, adjudicated by a person". - Grant person.view to identity_reviewer — settles it, and quietly makes an identity role one of the roles that can read a child's whole record, which is a bigger thing than the role's name suggests and is exactly what the catalog's top tier is reserved for. - A narrower read the catalog does not have yet — the resolver code that would expose only the two candidate rows and their signals, which would be a new code and therefore a decision entry, not an edit to this file. It is not proposed here, and would need arguing.

    Recommendation. Leave it, and watch the queue rather than the design. If reviewers start resolving hypotheses at a suspiciously high confirmation rate without asking for context, that is the sign the signals are not enough — and the honest response is a person.view grant discussed as a top-tier membership, not a new code invented at a desk.

    Needed. Nothing before implementation. Revisit when the first real import has produced a real queue.

    F-18 🟢 Does a role record elected versus appointed, and what does an election write?¶

    Raised as a loss by D-100, not as a feature request. position carried is_leadership, min_age, max_holders and a position_assignment row, and the corpus's organization keeps an elected committee. Those columns are deleted, so the model now has one kind of office and cannot distinguish "elected for a two-year term by a vote" from "appointed by whoever holds role.assign". role_holder has valid_from, valid_to, granted_by and note, which is nearly enough to describe an election — and granted_by names the person who wrote the row, which for an election is a secretary, not a mandate.

    Question. Does a role say elected or appointed; and if so, what does an election write — one role_holder row whose note says who voted, a document attached to the row, or a row in a table that does not exist yet?

    Why it might be nothing. An election is an event in the world that the system records, not a process it runs: there is no ballot, no candidate table and no quorum rule anywhere in these documents, and D-043 settled that this system does not hold background checks either — it is not the registry of everything true about a volunteer. note, valid_from, valid_to plus a document answer "we voted and here is the minutes", and is_system keeps the seeded offices distinct from the organization's invented ones. A convention, not a guard: nothing will ever refuse to appoint someone to an office the bylaws reserve for an election.

    Why it might be something. Two things the deleted tables answered by construction now have no answer at all: the vacancy question (max_holders made "two chairpersons is one too many" a check) and the term-limit question (role.max_term_months is a per-role cap, not an electoral rule — a two-year term renewable once is a different claim from "may hold for at most 24 months"). Both recur in a Scout-like body with annual meetings, and both will arrive as one-off questions.

    Positions. Nothing — note and a document (cheapest, defensible, and consistent with the round's direction); a boolean on role that says only how the office is filled, which answers the bylaws question and writes nothing new; or an election/appointment record, which is a new module and therefore a decision entry with an actual argument in it, not a column added here.

    Schema impact. Either zero, one boolean on role, or a table plus its permission codes plus its audit behaviour. The boolean is not free either: it invites a check ("may a non-elected holder be written for an elected office?") which D-100's posture — opt-in, review by report — would refuse to enforce, and a column nobody enforces is a column that lies after the first exception.

    Needed. Not before implementation. Before the first AGM, which is when somebody asks whether the system can show that the current chair was elected.

    G. Events — designed, with three things left open¶

    The module is written: domain/07-events.md and data-model/08-events.md. Fifteen questions have been answered (D-058 … D-067, plus EV-1 and EV-5 in a later round); what remains below is what those answers did not settle. None of it blocks implementation.

    EV-2 🟡 Retention of event_answer¶

    The question, restated once so it stays answerable. It used to read "a dietary answer citing a medical condition is health data, and health data has a retention obligation" — and D-083 removed the second half of that sentence's subject: there is no class of health data with its own obligation, only personal data, of which a dietary answer is one instance. The question survives, and it is now the ordinary version of a question this system has not answered anywhere: event answers end with the event, "end" is not a defined policy, and a fact recorded on a form is the same kind of fact as the same sentence recorded as a person_attribute (data-model/09-attributes.md), which raises the sharper form of the question — should an answer about a child be deleted on a schedule at all, when the same information held as an attribute is kept until someone retires it? The paragraph at the end of this item is where that doubling is worked out; what changed here is only that the question can no longer be filed under a category of data that no longer exists.

    Why it is not trivial. D-061 made an event form the route by which a camp leader learns a child has a nut allergy. That is a deliberate widening, and it puts health-adjacent data in a table whose protections are weaker than the health record's — and in round 6 that gap widened twice over: there is no longer a dependent.medical.* code marking which data is the sensitive kind (E-7), and the audience entitled to read an answer is whoever runs that event, a set sized by whoever creates it. The retention policy is the remaining half of that trade.

    Needed. Legal input, of the same kind B-3's indefinite retention still needs. The technical question — where does it live, who may read it — is answered; whether it must be deleted, kept, or archived is not.

    Changed in round 7, in a way that strengthens the case rather than settling it. D-083 made every fact about a person one class of data held on the person, so an event answer naming an allergy is now the same kind of fact stored in two places by two different rules — one on the person, where it is audited, curable and erased with the person; one in an event's table, readable by whoever runs that event, with no retention policy. The retention question and AT-1 are the same question from two directions: an answer worth keeping is an attribute that was collected at the wrong address, and an answer not worth keeping should be deleted on a schedule rather than surviving because nobody wrote a policy. Until one of the two is decided, the weakest-protected copy of a health fact is the one that is freshest.

    EV-4 🟢 was_present as a safeguarding record¶

    The question. Presence is the record of who was actually in the organization's care (D-067). Does it need its own retention, or its own permission?

    Why it might. It is the record that answers "was this child at camp on the day of the incident" — the question insurance and any safeguarding review will ask. That is a heavier thing than "who said they were coming", and it currently shares both a table and a permission with the registration.

    Why it might not. It is meaningless without the registration it belongs to, and splitting it would put two rows in two places describing one fact.

    Impact. Splitting it is a table; keeping it is nothing. The argument for splitting is permissions — a volunteer who takes the register at camp is not the same trust as one who may alter the roster. See EV-5, which is the same tension.

    EV-6 🟡 WHITELISTED — the word people reach for, with no stored state to name¶

    Question. D-102 named four attendance tiers a grant may bind to and refused a fifth (PRESENT, refused because was_present is NULL until somebody marks it). WHITELISTED keeps coming up in the same sentence and there is nowhere in the model for it to read from: no flag on event_registration, no roster on event besides organizers, no column anywhere that says this person was let in. Should the language refuse the word, or should the model give it state?

    The half that is already answered, and should be said before anything is built. "These named people may register for this event" needs no new anything: it is an allow grant on the event naming event.register_self, aimed at an audience whose expression is the hand-typed PERSON(a) + PERSON(b) or names a GROUP — one row per sentence, revocable, audited, and dated by the grant rather than by the names, since the dated rows those names used to live in are deleted (D-099, D-102, and D-105 for the deletion; F-20 settles which of the two spellings a given sentence wants, and this entry does not). That is the D-102 answer to the sentence, and it is better than a flag because a flag cannot be aimed at a group, cannot expire, and cannot be refused by a deny. Anyone adding is_whitelisted is reimplementing grants badly.

    The half that is not answered. There is a different sentence that sounds identical and is not about permission: "she was invited, ahead of the public opening, and she has not answered yet." That is a fact about a person's standing on one event, and the model cannot state it. Worse, the tiers actively misreport it: PLACED is the capacity set, so an invited person who registers onto a full event is ON_WAITLIST — the model says waiting where the organizer's head says has a place, we made room — and D-065 records an organizer exceeding capacity by report, not by state. So the overrun is real, visible in a report, and invisible to any selector, which means a grant aimed at "the people we actually have room for" and a grant aimed at "the people we invited" are the same four tiers stretched over two different questions.

    Positions. - Refuse the word — say plainly, where the tiers are listed, that there is no WHITELISTED and that the sentence it wants is a grant. Consistent with D-102's refusal of PRESENT, costs nothing, and leaves the invited-but-unanswered person described the way unanswered already is: by the absence of a row (D-059). This is the default these documents hold today. - Give it state — a boolean or small status on event_registration, which makes it a fifth tier and forces the tier algebra to be restated: is an invited person PLACED by right, or only once capacity lets them through? Whatever is chosen, PLACED's definition — the set the capacity let through, the one the index already serves — stops being one predicate, and that is the cost, not the new column. - Make it a group — the invitation list is a person_group (D-098), named, dated, and separately answerable across time. This is cheap and it is almost right: it says who was invited, which is the fact people actually ask for later, and it is answerable on 1 June 2024 in a way an audience never is. It is wrong for the per-event case, because it makes an invitation a thing with its own lifetime.

    Needed. Not before implementation, and not before someone asks — but the word should be refused in writing now, in the same paragraph that refuses PRESENT, because "no WHITELISTED" is a sentence that has to exist somewhere before the first person implements it as a column. The PRESENT refusal is the precedent to copy: it was refused for a representable reason (nothing is stored until a person marks it), and this one needs its reason stated rather than inherited.

    Impact. Refusing costs a paragraph. The column costs a restated PLACED, a re-derived index, and a report that now has two ways to be full.

    Ga. Personal attributes — designed, with four things left open¶

    The module exists: domain/08-person-data.md and data-model/09-attributes.md, decided by D-083, D-084 and D-085. It arrived instead of the health-records module, because the question "what is a health record here" answered itself a size larger: everything about a person except name, gender and age is one class of data, defined by the organization at runtime, with no privileged subset.

    AT-1 🟡 Should an event question bind to an attribute code?¶

    Raised by D-085, which made the event form the fallback rather than the ceiling.

    The situation. A returning camper types their allergy into every event form, every season, and the answer lives in event_answer.value for that event only. Meanwhile the organization may already hold the same fact as an attribute on the person, with a name, a value_type, and a recorded author. Nothing connects them.

    Why this is a question and not an obvious improvement. Prefilling an answer from the record makes the record the thing a leader relies on, and the record is: whatever was typed, by whoever, possibly two years ago, with no obligation on anyone to update it. Today's arrangement is worse data and better epistemics — the form asks now, about this event, and the answer is dated today. Bind the two and an out-of-date attribute becomes a wrong answer that looks authoritative, which is the one failure mode a health fact has that makes it dangerous.

    Recommendation. Prefill as a suggestion the registrant confirms or corrects on the way in, and write the confirmed value back to the attribute with today's date and this person as its author. Then the form stays the authority for the event, the record gets refreshed by the act of using it, and nobody ever relies on a two-year-old entry without a human having just looked at it. The variant to reject is reading the attribute directly into the organizer's view without the registrant's confirmation pass — that is the same convenience with the safety removed.

    Impact. One binding on the question definition, one write-back rule, and a rule about which is authoritative when they disagree. No new permission.

    See: D-061 (as amended), data-model/08 §7.

    AT-2 🟡 Who curates the vocabulary, and how long may a proposal sit?¶

    Raised by D-084, which made attribute.define a permission instead of a release.

    An open vocabulary is only as good as somebody tending it, and "somebody" is currently nobody in particular. Two halves:

    • Who holds attribute.define. The starting role set (permission-catalog §11) is the place this gets answered, and the honest answer is that a volunteer organization has one or two people who will do this and not a committee. Too narrow and the proposal queue is a bottleneck that teaches people to file facts into notes instead; too wide and it is not a curation.
    • How long a proposal may sit before it is promoted, merged or discarded. The check exists (09-attributes §8 checks 1 and 2) and the number does not. A quarter is a defensible default: long enough that a seasonal field is not judged off a single use, short enough that a proposal is answered inside the season that produced it.

    Recommendation. One named role, a stated age limit on proposals, and a standing decision that an expired proposal's values stay — the curation is about the name, never about deleting what was recorded.

    Impact. Configuration and one role, no schema change. The thing it prevents is the slow failure: a vocabulary nobody curates stops being queryable and the layer keeps storing everything, which looks like success for years.

    AT-3 🟢 Do attribute labels need translation?¶

    Inherited from the multi-language row in §H, and sharper here than anywhere else because the vocabulary is runtime data: if a code's label is Czech-only, a Slovak-speaking volunteer cannot pick the field at all. Whether the answer is a translation column on attribute_definition, a separate label-per-language table, or "the organization is single-language, stop asking" is unrecorded. The cost of not answering is asymmetric — a label table added later is a migration; a body of codes accumulated with one label each is a data-cleaning project.

    AT-4 🟡 Is one permission enough to guard a trail that holds person data?¶

    Raised by the reversal recorded in D-083, which decided the audit trail does hold a person's descriptive values — because D-085 made that trail the only history such values have, and stripping them would have made the history empty while quietly grading health-shaped fields above a change of address. What protects the copy is then one thing: audit.view, graded ⚠⚠ beside person.view (permission-catalog §8). Three positions exist and none is recorded as taken:

    • The guard is enough (current documents). One permission, marked honestly, and a rule that whoever holds it is reading person data. Simple, and it makes the auditor role — which has audit.view and not person.view — a reader of the entire person record (permission-catalog §11 note 3).
    • Per-entry filtering in the history view: show an audit row's values only where the reader could also see the live field. Consistent with everything §9 of data-model/05c-access-log-and-resolver.md says about channels, and it was considered and left unimplemented, because it means running the resolver per audit row against rows that may no longer exist — a grant withdrawn last year is exactly the case a history is read for.
    • Split the permission: the trail's shape (who changed which table, when) apart from its payloads. Honest about what an auditor usually needs, and it is the option that reintroduces a sensitive subset through the back of the catalog — which is why the documents do not recommend it.

    The cost of not answering is asymmetric in the uncomfortable direction: the first position is already implemented by default, so leaving the question open is choosing it, and the choice is made invisible — nobody signs off on "volunteers with audit.view can read children's medical history" because there is no moment at which anybody has to say it.

    How to answer¶

    Nothing here blocks anything. No question on this page is marked 🔴, and none has been since round 4: the model is settled enough that most answers here change data rather than structure, which is what makes them worth a moment rather than a decision meeting. Seven are marked 🟡 and eight 🟢. The list below is what to answer first — roughly half of what it was before round 13 closed four of its nine items, which is worth saying before the list is trusted again.

    1. F-10 — which invariants the ORM cannot express, and who writes those it cannot. The shape list is now the shortest path to everything the write path has to carry: an event row that obliges a grant row, requiredness conditioned on a value in selector_type, a check that reads a referenced row, a name namespace across three tables, and exclusion constraints over all rows rather than over open ones. Round 13 handed it a shape it had never had — an invariant over the documents rather than over the data: every selector name that appears in a document must be a selector_type row, and a check should be able to say so (D-113, minted when the fourteenth selector turned out to be a prose idiom). That is a lint of prose against a seed list, so it belongs to F-19's machinery rather than to the write path — and it is now the only check that would have caught it.
    2. F-18 — does a role record elected versus appointed, and what does an election write? A property of the deleted position tables that a real elected committee still needs a place for.
    3. AT-2 / AT-4 — who curates the attribute vocabulary day to day, and whether one permission is trusted to guard a trail that holds person data. These are one question seen from two ends: the data that lands in the trail arrives through the vocabulary.
    4. EV-2 — how long an event answer is kept once the event it answered is gone. A sharper question now that the audit payloads which also held those values have a clock on them (see B-3).
    5. F-19 — the one item in this file that could be settled this afternoon. A script extracts the code-shaped and selector-shaped words from these documents and diffs them against both registries, in both directions. It costs no schema. Prose and a seeded registry disagreed four times in ten rounds, and round 13 made it five — the fifth is the argument that reads hardest: GUARDIANS was a name in six pages of prose and a registry row that should never have been minted, so both sides of the diff were wrong at once. What was adopted instead is a hand-run report: a convention, not a guard (D-104).
    6. The legal review, which is not a question this register can answer and which now has a concrete policy in front of it: named membership and fee rows kept indefinitely, audit payloads kept 24 months. Assumption 49 is its subject — and since D-093 the indefinitely-kept population includes thousands of imported people who never used the system, which is the same judgement at a scale the review has not yet been shown.

    Everything else can be answered as we go.

Four rounds are worth reading for what their entries do not say: rounds 7 and 8 settled identity and history without changing a table, round 9 settled the organization and produced two lessons larger than its decisions, round 10 redesigned the model instead of answering anything, and round 13 closed questions by removing the machinery that raised them.
