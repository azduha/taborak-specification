    Identity Resolution and Legacy Data¶

    How the system decides that two records describe the same human, and how four decades of paper and spreadsheet history gets brought in without corrupting live data.

    This is the highest-risk area of the project. Get it wrong and either the history is unusable, or worse, two people's records get merged and you leak one member's data to another. The design below is deliberately conservative.

    1. Why this is hard¶

    A 1994 row has: a name, maybe a birth date, the period that person was a member, and, on most rows, a legacy member number (D-090), with free text — an address, remarks — in columns beside it. This opening sentence used to read "a unit, a year"; the corpus (D-092) names no unit at all, which costs §7 more than it costs this section. What this section used to open with instead — "no unique key" — was written before the corpus had been described and was wrong in the useful direction. What is still true is that none of it can log in: the number keys the organization's paper, not a session, and it is missing from precisely the rows that most need the heuristics below — those predating the numbering, and those where it was written illegibly. Meanwhile:

    • Names change (marriage, adoption, correction of a misspelling).
    • Names repeat. In a youth organization spanning decades, "Jan Novák" is many people.
    • Diacritics and transliterations vary across decades of manual entry (Novák / Novak).
    • Birth dates are often wrong by a digit, or recorded as year-only.
    • The same human appears in multiple rosters under slightly different spellings.

    So: automatic matching will produce both false positives and false negatives. False positive (two people merged) is a privacy incident. False negative (one person split) is a data-quality annoyance. The design must therefore bias hard toward false negatives — never merge on a guess.

    This document is about one half of a person. Everything it resolves is identity: the fields that make a row a particular human — names, birth date, the account, contact points, issued identifiers. Everything the organization records about that human beyond those is descriptive data, and it is deliberately absent from every table below: no descriptive value is a match signal, and none may become one. A clothing size or a dietary habit identifies nobody, and making matching depend on one would make the reliability of a person's history depend on what the organization happened to collect about them (08-person-data.md, D-085). The line between the two halves is drawn by what a machine may act on without a human, not by how delicate a fact feels; the test is stated in 08-person-data.md.

    2. The three record classes¶

    Every person row carries record_origin:

    Origin Meaning Confidence Has account?
    self_registered A human created this by registering high yes
    staff_created A registrar entered this person high maybe
    legacy_import Imported from historical records low no

    Legacy-imported people are provisional. They are fully queryable — "who was in Troop 12 in 1998" must work — but they are marked as unverified, they cannot be logged into, and they are the only rows eligible to be absorbed by a merge.

    Rule: a merge always absorbs the lower-confidence row into the higher-confidence one. An import can never overwrite a self-registered person's data.

    3. Identity hypotheses, not merges¶

    The core mechanism. An identity hypothesis is a proposal that two person rows are the same human. It is a first-class table row, with a confidence score, evidence, a status, and a human decision.

    The row holds the pair as person_a_id and person_b_id, stored in one order: person_a_id names the lower id, and the two are never the same person — a pair has one canonical spelling rather than two.

    confidence is the matcher's score between 0 and 1; match_signals records which fields agreed and how strongly, which is what a reviewer reads to see why it was proposed. status is one of proposed, needs_review, confirmed, rejected, superseded. proposed_by says where the proposal came from — import_job, match_job (the nightly batch matcher), person or staff — and proposed_at when. reviewed_by, reviewed_at and review_note are the human part, and a hypothesis may not reach confirmed or rejected without a reviewed_at. At most one live hypothesis exists per pair — live means proposed, needs_review or confirmed.

    Lifecycle¶

      matcher proposes ──> proposed ──┬── staff confirms ──> confirmed ──> [merge executed]
                                  ├── staff rejects  ──> rejected (the pair becomes
                                  │                       proposable again — see §4)
                                  └── person asserts ──> needs_review → staff confirms/rejects

    Three entry points:

    1. Import or background matching proposes hypotheses automatically at low confidence.
    2. A registering person asserts their own history — "I was a member 1990–2010" — which creates a hypothesis against the candidate rows, usually with proposed_by = 'person'.
    3. A sign-up that strong-matches a person who already exists raises one by itself, without anyone asserting anything, and that hypothesis holds the new account out of active (§4, D-089). The queue is the same queue and the review is the same review; what is new is that a person arriving at a registration form can put a row in it.

    A person's own assertion is evidence, not proof. It must still be reviewed, because claiming someone else's history is the obvious attack on an import-based system — and the prize is that person's data. Never auto-confirm a self-asserted link.

    4. Match signals¶

    The matcher scores candidate pairs on independent signals. Recorded in match_signals so a reviewer sees why the system proposed a match:

    Signal Strength Notes
    Exact legacy member number decisive The corpus carries these (D-056). Same number, same person — matched, not scored.
    Exact birth date + exact normalised name strong Still not automatic — twins with similar names exist.
    Birth date within ±1 day or year-only match + name moderate Common with hand-written records.
    Name match after normalisation (diacritics, case, spacing) moderate Necessary but weak alone; "Jan Novák" is not unique.
    Same unit + overlapping membership period moderate Corroborating, not identifying.
    Same postal address at the same time moderate Strong if the address is distinctive. Read from person_contact history — there is no household table (D-057).
    Guardian name match weak Useful for minors in old records.
    Name-only fuzzy match weak — never sufficient alone

    The decisive row changes the matcher's job. With a number on the record, the question is not "are these two the same person?" but "does this number already point somewhere?" — a lookup. The scored signals below are then for the rows the number cannot resolve: legacy records predating the numbering, records where the number is illegible, and people who were never issued one. At thousands of rows (D-056) this split is what makes D-004 affordable: the numbered rows are the trustworthy set and the rest is a work queue.

    Normalisation¶

    Applied before comparison, and the normalised form is stored so matching is reproducible:

    • Unicode NFKD, strip diacritics, casefold, collapse whitespace.
    • Common nickname/legal-name equivalences (configurable per locale — Jan/Jano, Josef/Josip).
    • Typo distance (Levenshtein/Jaro-Winkler) above a threshold counts as a partial match, never a full one.

    Thresholds¶

    • confidence < 0.60 — not surfaced. Stored only for diagnostics.
    • 0.60 ≤ confidence < 0.90 — proposed, queued for human review.
    • confidence ≥ 0.90 — still requires human confirmation, but flagged as high priority. There is no auto-merge threshold. A privacy incident from a bad merge is worse than any amount of manual review.

    This paragraph used to rest the no-auto-merge rule on merges being "irreversible in practice", and that premise has been withdrawn: D-087 made unmerge a required operation (§6). The rule does not need the false premise. It rests on what the reversal cannot reach — the merged window stays one row's history — and on the union of permissions being exercised from the moment the merge commits, whatever is discovered afterwards.

    Assumption, now settled: no automatic merging at all, even for near-certain matches (D-004). This document left an escape hatch open on the theory that the volume of historical data might make manual review infeasible, and offered an opt-in auto-merge tier for that case. The volume is now known: thousands of rows arriving in two exports (D-092), most of the people rows carrying a number that makes the match a lookup rather than a judgement (D-090). The hatch is not needed, and it is not reserved.

    The same signals at sign-up, split at the top of the band¶

    Until now these signals were read only by machinery working over the import. A registration form collides with them too. A sign-up that strong-matches an existing person raises an identity_hypothesis and is then held out of active by it — an account may not reach active while an unresolved hypothesis names its person (D-089). The sign-up's own rows are created exactly as any sign-up creates them and stay pending_verification; what "creates nothing" rules out is the outcome — nothing is merged, nothing is linked, nothing is activated. The address the person typed stays on the pending account as the credential it was offered as, which matters because the reviewer's other answer is "this is a different person", and that answer should leave the applicant finishing the sign-up they started rather than starting it again (01-identity.md §4, 02-actors.md §3). Confirming the hypothesis merges the two rows under §5 and the pending account goes with the row that was absorbed; rejecting it lifts the gate and the account activates.

    Here the threshold is not a tuning parameter, it is the entire answer. The band is split at its top, and only the top reaches the gate:

    Signal at sign-up Raises a blocking hypothesis?
    Exact legacy member number yes
    Exact birth date and exact normalised name yes
    Birth date ±1 day, or year-only + name no
    Name-only fuzzy match, shared unit, shared address, guardian name no

    The soft band raises nothing at sign-up. It keeps doing what it always did — the nightly matcher may propose the pair for review, at a confidence that queues it rather than blocking anybody. The tempting rule, "raise one whenever the score is high", produces the failure this design would most deserve: a stranger's registration blocked by a fuzzy score. The cost of the version chosen is bounded and self-correcting (a true duplicate slips through, is matched nightly, and is reviewed); the cost of the tempting version is a child who cannot sign up for a camp on the day the form closes, which is not self-correcting at all.

    Two obligations come with the gate, and neither is a queue-management nicety:

    • A hypothesis that is blocking an account gets an age check, and it alerts. A stalled identity queue used to be a data-quality problem with no victim you could name. Now its victim is a specific person who cannot log in and whose only remaining channel is to telephone someone. The check is shaped like field_conflict's thirty days (01-identity.md §10); the alerting is the part that changed.
    • The holding message confirms nothing. It says the request was received and will be checked. It says nothing about whether the name, the birth date or the number matched anything, because a sign-up form that can answer "yes, that person exists" is an existence oracle, and this system's roster is children.

    "I have a new e-mail address" is not this case and must not be routed through it: account_credential holds many credentials for one account, so a new address is an addition to the login a person already has. The collision case is narrower than signing up twice — it is a person arriving through a door the system did not know they had. Resolving the claim is confirming or rejecting a hypothesis, so it is person.merge, held by identity_reviewer (../appendix/permission-catalog.md §2); there is deliberately no account.link code, because an automatic link would be D-004 under another name.

    5. The merge operation¶

    Merging is a soft merge: the absorbed row survives as a tombstone.

    person (surviving, id=100)          person (absorbed, id=847, tombstoned)
        ▲                                     │
        │                                     ▼
        └────────── person_merge ────► merged_into_person_id = 100
                 (id, surviving_person_id, merged_person_id,
                  merged_at, merged_by, hypothesis_id, reason)

    What moves¶

    Table Action
    person_contact Reassigned to survivor. Conflicts (two is_primary of the same type) resolved by review, not by rule.
    person_identifier Reassigned. Duplicate scheme+value collapses to one.
    person_attribute Reassigned. Two current values for one code is a review item, never a choice made by rule — this table holds one value per person and code with no validity period to keep both, so unlike two past addresses a merge cannot leave the pair standing: the pair is raised as a field_conflict and a human decides, exactly as two is_primary contacts of one type are decided. Where the definition allows several values (allow_multiple), the two rows simply coexist and nothing is decided (../data-model/09-attributes.md §3).
    person_name Reassigned, keeping validity periods — this is how a maiden name survives.
    account Reassigned to survivor. If the survivor already has an account, the absorbed account is closed, not deleted.
    membership Reassigned. Overlapping periods flagged for review — with one type (D-037) and a person-level no-overlap rule, an overlap here means a bad merge. That used to be the only way to make one; since D-093 an imported period can produce it too, because the source states its own periods and nothing in the file knows that one human appears on two rows. Two imported periods covering the same date are outside the exclusion constraint — it watches the live statuses only — so the merge review is where they surface, and the honest reading of a pair is "the source said this", not "somebody decided this".
    person_group_member Reassigned, with the same overlap check. Two rows for one person in one group over overlapping dates is a review item and not a dedupe: two people who turn out to be one may genuinely have been put in the group twice, on different evidence, and the honest repair is one row whose dates a person chose.
    role_holder Reassigned. An office held twice by one person over overlapping periods is the same review case, with the same refusal to guess silently — and it is read out loud rather than filed quietly, because this table's rows are exactly what ROLE(x) reaches, so a duplicate holder row is a duplicate audience member.
    guardianship (as dependent) Reassigned.
    guardianship (as guardian) Reassigned.
    access_grant Reassigned. Union of permissions results — this is the security-critical step. Merging two people silently merges two sets of authority, and since D-099 that union is literally the rows both people leave behind: no rank resolves a collision, so a deny on the absorbed row survives alongside the survivor's allows rather than being deduplicated away, which is the outcome to read out loud at review. role_assignment is gone from this table — the office rows moved to role_holder above, where an overlap is already a review case
    audience_operand, where selector_kind is PERSON Repointed, not reassigned, and this row is newer than the rest of the table. D-105 deleted audience_member, so every hand-named person in the system is now a selector_arg_id — a column with deliberately no foreign key (D-011), which is why this row is a requirement on the merge and not something the database notices. The failure is asymmetric and it is the loud one: a leaf that resolves to nobody is the empty set, so a grant reading EVERYONE - PERSON(x) where x was merged away stops subtracting anybody and denies everybody. An orphan scan is the backstop; the merge is the mechanism. The same walk covers the other four argument kinds — an absorbed person_group, a deleted role, an event, a nested audience — because none of them has a key either
    person_merge Row inserted here.
    field_conflict Populated for anything that could not be resolved automatically.

    What does not happen¶

    • Nothing is deleted. The absorbed person row keeps its id and gains merged_into_person_id. All historical foreign keys pointing at it still resolve, so "who was in Troop 12 in 1998" keeps working.
    • Overlapping memberships are never silently deduplicated. Two overlapping memberships after a merge is suspicious — it may mean the merge was wrong. It becomes a review item. (This bullet used to say child memberships, a distinction the model stopped making with one membership type and ageless membership (D-037, D-038); what it described — two open periods on one person — is the same suspicious shape.)

    The cycle is refused here, not at the read¶

    Chains are permanent (§6): merged_into_person_id is never rewritten forward, so A→B→C stays a chain and resolve_person() walks it. A chain that loops would make that walk never end, so a merge is refused when the person chosen as survivor resolves — by walking the chain — to the person being absorbed (D-087). That subsumes the case already ruled out, nobody merges into themselves, and catches the one that actually happens: absorbing B into A when A has already been absorbed into B.

    Checking on the way in is the whole guard, and a read-side step limit is not an acceptable substitute. A hop bound does not prevent the cycle; its only achievement is deciding which of two wrong answers to return, and the wrong answer it returns is a tombstoned id — the exact failure resolve_person() exists to prevent.

    The other half of the same rule is softer and needs a net under it. "Every place that accepts a person id from outside must call resolve_person()" is a rule application code can forget, and the schema carries no way to catch it (D-072). What catches it is a report of live rows that reference a tombstoned person, which is the only form of this failure that is visible after the fact (../data-model/01-identity.md §10).

    The security rule that must not be skipped¶

    Merging unions the two people's permissions. If a bad merge is performed, the survivor gains the absorbed person's access. Therefore:

    1. A merge is always performed by a human with person.merge permission.
    2. The merged result's effective permissions are re-displayed for confirmation before the merge commits.
    3. A merge can be undone (§6). The merged period cannot: access_grant rows created during it cannot be attributed to either human. Hence rule 2 — an unmerge repairs the record afterwards and does not repair this.
    4. ~~Merges invalidate cached permissions.~~ There are no cached permissions to invalidate — D-114 deleted the answer cache, and a person merge is one of the writes that dropped out of the surviving bump set with it: a merge changes which grant rows and operand leaves name the person, and both are read live now. What the merge does to an answer is therefore just what the next check computes, from the survivor's grant rows and the PERSON(x) leaves the merge repointed. That is the same fact that makes rule 2 load-bearing rather than courteous: with nothing stored, the re-displayed effective permissions are the only place the merged answer is computed before the merge commits. See 06-authorization-model.md.

    6. Reversing a merge¶

    Unmerge is a required operation, not a courtesy for mistakes an organization hopes it will not make (D-087). It is also the reason merge chains exist, so it is worth being honest about which way the justification now runs. Chains were argued for because flattening would make a reversal impossible — an assumption resting on a capability nobody had asked for. Somebody has asked for it now, so the capability is the requirement and the chain is its consequence. Over a chain a reversal is a replay of pointers; over a flattened pointer it would be a reconstruction of facts nobody recorded. Flattening is therefore closed off, not merely slower, and "flattening is faster" is no longer a trade against tidiness — it is a trade against a feature someone named.

    A permanent chain needs two rules, and neither is enforced on the read:

    • No cycle, checked at the merge (§5). Resolution deliberately has no hop limit.
    • Chain length is a signal, not an error. Resolution is unbounded; a chain longer than three hops is reported. Four merges in a row on one line of people does not mean the row is odd — it means the matcher is producing duplicates faster than a human can settle them, which is a statement about the queue and not about the row. With chains decided, a survivor that has itself been absorbed is normal; only the length means anything (../data-model/01-identity.md §10).

    The reversal is four steps, and they restore the row split:

    1. Clear person.merged_into_person_id on the absorbed row. The tombstone comes off.
    2. Reassign rows created before merged_at back to the absorbed person.
    3. Flag rows created after merged_at for human review. Those are the ambiguous ones, and they are reviewed rather than split by a rule.
    4. Record the unmerge on the person_merge row as reversed_at, reversed_by and reversal_note. person_merge is append-only, so an unmerge records that a merge was undone rather than erasing the record of the mistake.

    The access_grant rows are the specific hazard. Any grant created during the merged window might belong to either human. Flag every one of them; do not guess.

    The asymmetry, stated plainly: the merge is reversible and the merged window is not. Steps 1, 2 and 4 repair the record. Step 3 is an admission. Whatever those two humans did while they were one row stays one row's history, because the grants exercised in that window were really exercised by somebody the record can no longer separate — which is precisely why the confirmation-before-commit rule in §5 is not made redundant by the existence of a reversal.

    Unmerge needs no permission code of its own. Undoing an identity correction is the same act as making one, performed by a named human, so it is person.merge (../appendix/permission-catalog.md §2). A person.unmerge code would grade undoing a mistake as safer than making one. Like the merge, the reversal splits a person's permissions back in two — and since nothing maps a person to an answer any more (D-114), there is nothing to invalidate: the split is simply visible on the next check, which is the same §5 rule stated on the way back.

    7. Legacy import pipeline¶

    Staged, reviewed, idempotent. Never a direct write into live tables.

    What is actually arriving is two exports, and neither is a roster (D-092). This section spent two rounds describing roster rows — people attending units — and the whole pipeline hung off that word, because a roster is evidence of presence in a unit and that is not what the source says. It says two things:

    Half What a row says What the batch writes
    The membership export a name, a birth date, and when that person was a member of the root unit a person, and a bounded membership — both ends dated, data_confidence = 'low', batch id on it — which brings its root-unit participation row with it (D-093)
    The attendance export an event, and who came to it an event owned by the root, and one event_registration per attendee at attending / was_present = true (D-094)

    Neither names a sub-unit, and nothing is assigned to one. The parser keeps its parsed unit code — an export that surprises whoever runs it with a unit column is exactly what that field and its unit_not_found warning are for, and keeping them costs nothing — but nothing reads them, no org_unit is created by any batch, and no sub-unit unit_participation row is ever imported. Two halves also means two shapes of batch on one import_batch: a people batch and an events batch, each carrying the field map and the checks that fit its half, rather than one batch holding two mappers that can never both apply.

    Say where it hurts, because somebody will ask this question. The historical record this import leaves behind contains no sub-units. "Who was in Pack 12 in 2011?" has no answer at go-live — not a wrong one, none — and the first answer anyone reads will be a roster somebody typed in by hand. Nothing in the model is missing: participation rows, historical units and unit relationships have always been able to hold it. It is what the source can say, and it is written here so that the first person to ask finds the sentence rather than the absence.

      source file(s)
       │
       ▼
  [1] staging_import          ── raw rows, verbatim, with the source file + line ref
       │
       ▼
  [2] staging_import_row      ── parsed: normalised name, parsed birth date,
       │                          detected unit, parse warnings
       ▼
  [3] matching                ── for each row, find candidate person rows and score them
       │
       ▼
  [4] review queue            ── human confirms: link to existing | create new | discard
       │
       ▼
  [5] apply                   ── a people batch writes person + bounded membership (+ its
       │                          root participation row); an events batch writes event
       │                          (owned by the root) + event_registration. Never an
       │                          org_unit, never a sub-unit participation row, never an
       │                          open-ended membership
       │
       ▼
  [6] import_log              ── what was applied, by whom, when; reversal instructions

    Requirements¶

    • Idempotent. Re-running the same import must not duplicate people. Each staged row carries a deterministic source_row_hash; re-import matches on it.
    • Reversible per batch. import_log records every row created or modified in a batch, so a bad batch can be rolled back without touching other data.
    • Never overwrites self-registered data. If a legacy row matches a self_registered person, the import may add what the export evidences — a bounded historical membership period and its root participation row, a registration on an imported event — but never alters names, contacts, identifiers, or descriptive values the person maintains themselves. The distinction is that an added closed period contradicts nothing the person typed: it is a dated claim about the past, sitting beside their live row rather than over it. An open-ended period would be a different thing entirely, which is why D-093's surviving ban is exactly that one.
    • Preserves provenance. Every imported row records import_batch_id and source_reference (file + line), so any value can be traced back to the paper it came from. When someone disputes a historical record, you can show them where it came from.
    • Records uncertainty rather than guessing. A row that cannot be parsed cleanly goes to the review queue with the raw value intact — it does not get a best-effort guess written as if it were fact.

    Raw columns become codes¶

    The pipeline was drawn when a legacy column could only ever be a name, a date or a unit. Old rosters also carry columns about the person — a remarks column, a "medical" column, a list of shirt sizes — and each of those arrives at step [5] as a value against a code, not as a blob beside the record (D-084). The corpus carries those columns alongside the identity columns, in the membership export — they are columns about a person, and the attendance export brings nothing that maps to an attribute code (D-090, D-092); the map therefore belongs to the people batch, and a code-to-registration map to the events batch, which is what "one batch, one map" survives as once a batch states which half it carries. That makes the first import the single largest event in the life of this vocabulary: several hundred codes out of one file. The rest of the rules for this live in staging (../data-model/09-attributes.md §7); three consequences follow from arriving all at once.

    • The map is authored and read before the batch applies, not after. That is precisely why the mapping is recorded on the batch as one object rather than per staged row: it is one artefact a person reads once, top to bottom, and the reading is what makes the codes it names trustworthy. Reading it afterwards would put the live vocabulary at the mercy of whatever the batch had already written. Changing an applied map is curation with an audit entry, not a migration.
    • A raw column that maps to no code stops the batch, unless someone maps it or marks it discarded with a reason. An unmapped column is a decade of something the organization used to collect vanishing quietly, which is the class of failure the rest of this document exists to prevent on the identity side.
    • A column nobody recognises becomes a code named from the column, never a guess at an existing code: an import-time merge is invisible and unreviewable, and the review queue above is for people, not for vocabulary. The codes the map names arrive live. This document said they arrived proposed, and the error is worth keeping in view because it costs more than the word: a curator has just read every name in the map, which is exactly the endorsement proposed withholds. proposed keeps its meaning of improvised later by a person at a desk — a fact with no code yet — and not "whatever the import did not understand". Several hundred codes landing proposed on day one would open the curation queue full, make its age signal meaningless before it had ever meant anything, and teach the organization that the attribute layer's warning queue is noise. Who holds attribute.define day to day, and how long a runtime proposal may sit, is a different question and stays open (AT-2).

    What each source row lands on¶

    This subsection was headed "Historical rows from imports" and said that an imported roster row yields a person, a unit_participation bounded to the roster's year, and no membership — the rule D-090 recorded, before there was a heading to withdraw. That frame is superseded in its second half, one round later, by D-092 and D-093, and the sequence is worth leaving visible: both rounds reasoned correctly from a file nobody had read. What the source states, and what arrives:

    What the source says It becomes Why
    "Jan Novák, born 1962, member 1990–1995" a person with record_origin = 'legacy_import', and one membership per period the source states — valid_from and valid_to set, data_confidence = 'low', import_batch_id set, the renewal chain left to whoever reconstructs it A stated period is evidence of membership and infers nothing; a closed period cannot gate anything (D-093)
    the same, with the year repeated in the file a second membership only if the source states a second period; consecutive stated years stretch to one row, a gap in the source opens another This row used to be the root participation row the membership obligation required, stretched by exactly this rule and carrying exactly one cost: the lapse stopped being visible in the participation table. D-097 deleted the row, and the cost with it — a lapse is now visible in one table, which is the table that means it (D-050, superseded)
    an event, and who came to it an event with no owner column at all, one event_registration per attendee at attending with was_present = true, and — since D-102 — the per-event instantiation of the seeded answers-view grant There is no unit to own it and no rule asking for a root: an event's people are named by selectors that bind to it, which is also the import-side saving D-097 counted (D-102)
    a person who might have come and did not nothing An export lists who came. Writing not_attending from silence puts words in a source that said nothing (D-059)
    a sub-unit nothing, ever — and there is now nothing that could be one The corpus names none (§7), and D-097 deleted the thing a name could have attached to. The row stays because it is the one a reader holding a 1998 spreadsheet will ask, and the answer has to be written down rather than inferred from absence
    money nothing — and no fee_obligation The corpus has no money in it (D-090)

    The ban that survives is one sentence, and it is the one that was always the point: never an open-ended membership, and never one inferred from an attendance row. The reason has not moved since the two were split apart — is_current_member() and the permission gate (D-039) both read membership, so a row running to no valid_to hands whoever signs up under that name authority in the present. What narrowed the ban is that it was written for a standing hazard — an import that infers, run by whoever holds the file, repeatedly, for years — and this is one reviewed batch whose map is read before it applies, on a corpus that will never arrive twice. A rule sized for a recurring process is too strict for a one-time one; the safety that mattered was the review, not the prohibition.

    What the withdrawn rule got right, kept in view because it is the part that still binds. The section was written against a real temptation — generate a membership for every historical row because that is what the old data model implied, then mark it inferred — and that refusal stands. The line the whole exchange turns on is evidence against inference: a row that says member 1990–1995 is evidence of a membership, and a row that says in Pack 12 in 1998 is evidence of presence and nothing more. D-093 moved only the first half, because it turned out the file states periods; the reading that "a 1998 roster row is participation, not membership" remains exactly true of a roster row, and there simply are no roster rows. Note too the observation that outlived the rule it justified: a conditional with one live arm is not a safe default — the old wording read "participation unless there is payment evidence", and since the corpus carries no payment data at all, the exception arm had an empty set to fire on while still inviting somebody to implement it.

    Four consequences that arrive with the memberships.

    • The root-participation obligation points at the import again. Round 8 corrected that section to say an ordinary import "touches neither arm of this pair"; with memberships arriving by the thousand it does, in the same transaction, which is what keeps who is in unit X one query (D-041).
    • No obligation, no payment, no balance. An imported membership creates no fee_obligation, and that has to be a rule rather than an accident: an obligation per historical year would land as unpaid and turn a decade of members into a wall of debt with a 1999 due_date for the lapse sweep to choke on. Related, and now load-bearing rather than incidental: outstanding_balance is null on these rows — a zero would be a claim of settled that no export supports.
    • The person-level no-overlap rule is back in the import's path. Round 8's tidy sentence — "with no imported membership there is nothing for it to violate" (05-membership-lifecycle.md §3) — is gone with the premise under it. One row per period the source states, not one per calendar year, is now a rule with a consequence rather than a tidiness.
    • These rows are the oldest layer of the skeleton D-088 keeps forever (D-088): thousands of named people with birth dates who were never asked and never used the system, held on an unverified legal basis. Same judgement as before, much larger population.

    What this makes true about reports. Every cross-decade member count is now a mixture of an export and a register, distinguished only by data_confidence and import_batch_id. A membership-rate chart spanning the import boundary compares a source that states membership with a system that records it, and nothing in the query knows the difference.

    A historical membership is also obtainable as a human act, and that route did not go away — it changed what it is for. Where the organization has a period the export does not state (a ledger found later, a written confirmation, a person who was a member in a year the file lost), the period is entered by hand under membership.backdate (../appendix/permission-catalog.md §4). Under the old rule this was the only way to get a historical membership at all; under D-093 the pipeline carries the stated ones and this remains the route for the unstated, still deliberate, still attributed, still outside the batch.

    End dates are still often unknown, and this is now the sharpest case of it. A period the export leaves open — someone last seen in a 2003 row with no end stated — cannot be written open: that is the one shape D-093 refuses outright, and an unbounded historical record is the same hazard wearing a different name. Convention, unchanged from before the corpus was known: end it at the end of the school/organizational year of the last row they appear in, marked as inferred. That remains the only date the import infers — the periods themselves are now stated rather than inferred — and it is precisely what the organizational year boundary is needed for, which is still open (A-4) and, with attendance and no money in the corpus, is the sharpest remaining import question. Document the rule you choose; it will be questioned.

    8. The returning-member case¶

    The scenario you described — member 1990–2010, returns 2024 — should be the smooth path, because it is a common and important one (returning alumni become adult leaders and donors).

    Flow:

    1. Person registers in 2024, self-asserting past membership.
    2. The system matches them against legacy rows and proposes hypotheses.
    3. A registrar reviews and confirms.
    4. The historical membership (1990–2010) and the current membership (2024–present) now sit under one person row.
    5. Their profile shows their full history; the gap is explicit data.
    6. Any seniority or "years of service" calculation reads across both.

    Step 4 is two membership rows, and one round ago this document said the opposite. It then held that what the import holds for 1990–2010 is unit_participation, so the profile read as twelve years of participation plus a membership from 2024 — "the truth of what the organization can evidence", with a historical membership appearing only if somebody typed one. That was true of a corpus of roster rows. The corpus is a membership export, which states the period, so the twelve years arrive as a bounded membership row, and one row only: D-097 deleted the root-unit participation row it used to be written beside in one transaction, so the pair this sentence described is now a single row (D-093); what the sentence is still refusing is the inference, and the assertion that survives unchanged is the one that matters for this case: a 1990–2010 membership appears only where the source states the period or somebody records it by hand on evidence (membership.backdate, §7). Note what did not survive, because it is the part a reader will otherwise rely on: the smooth path now restores a person's member history without a human typing it, which is what round 8's rule existed to prevent. What makes it safe now, and only now, is that the source states the period instead of the import guessing one.

    Steps 2–3 can be streamlined when the match is strong (exact old membership number), but the confirmation step remains, for the reason in §3.

    One thing D-089 changed about this path, and it is a cost rather than a clarification. The activation gate does not distinguish why a hypothesis exists: an account may not reach active while an unresolved hypothesis names its person, and a self-asserted claim names that person exactly as a collision does. So the smooth path now has a human between registration and login, where before the review happened after the person was already using the system. The mitigation is the same one the collision case gets — the age check that alerts (§4) — plus the fact that with a legacy number in hand step 2 is a lookup a registrar settles in seconds. What is not settled is whether a hypothesis the person raised about themselves should hold their account out of active at all, as against a hypothesis the system raised about a stranger who happened to match. Recorded in §9.

    Self-service without weakening the guarantee¶

    Rather than auto-linking, give the person a claim flow: they assert a past membership, staff confirm. Record the assertion on the hypothesis row, so that if the claim later proves false, you can see it was self-asserted rather than discovered.

    9. Open issues¶

    Still open:

    • Whether twins/siblings with similar names and the same birth date need a documented disambiguation procedure (recommend: yes, and never merge them automatically). The sign-up gate in §4 sharpens this rather than softening it: same birth date plus a normalised name is exactly the collision pair, and a collision now stops a registration instead of merely queueing a card for somebody else to settle later.
    • Whether a hypothesis a person raised about themselves should hold their account out of active, as against one the system raised because a stranger's sign-up strong-matched them (§8). D-089 decided the second case, and its wording covers both.
    • The organizational year boundary that historical end dates get inferred from (A-4). With stated membership periods, attendance, and no money (§7) this is load-bearing by itself rather than one of several open import questions — and since D-093 the inferred date now closes a membership row rather than only a participation row, so the number of rows it touches went up, not down.

    Resolved, kept visible so the reasoning survives:

    • Volume and formats of historical data — thousands of rows in two exports, people and events, 1990s to now, free-text columns alongside (B-1, D-090, D-092). "One export" is what this bullet said until round 9; the correction is only about shape, and the answers it produced stand. This section used to ask the question in order to decide whether manual review was affordable and to hold open a high-confidence auto-merge tier; the corpus is modest and number-bearing, so the tier is not needed and is not reserved (§4).
    • Whether the old system issued member numbers — it did, on most rows: imported verbatim into person_identifier under the legacy scheme and never reissued (D-056, D-090). §4's strongest signal is available, and where a number is present the match is a lookup rather than a score.
    • How much of an old roster becomes a membership — this round's answer is "the periods the export states" (§7), and it is the reversal of the previous one, which was none of it: the question was "how much", the corpus carried no payment evidence, and so the arm of the old rule that produced memberships could not be reached (D-041). D-093 reached the same conclusion by a different road — the source states a membership period directly, so no payment evidence was ever the only way in — and left the old rule's purpose intact as the ban described in §7. The stakes this section named are why that ban is shaped as it is: an inferred membership running to today would make a 2003 roster member a current member under the permission gate (D-039), which is exactly what a period with both ends dated cannot do.
    • Retention — both the erasure mechanism (redact the person, keep the financial and membership skeleton) and the durations: that skeleton indefinitely as the organization's own history, audit payloads emptied 24 months after writing (D-088). The half that stays open is not a design question — whether indefinite retention of named membership and fee rows has a legal basis, which a decision log cannot grant (B-3).

    Section B of the register — identity and history — is closed in full. §7's shape is registered in the organization section rather than here: C-5 closed the question of whether the corpus names units, and what it closed leaves the unit-level question in §7's "where it hurts" column with no system answer to give. All tracked in ../open-questions.md.
