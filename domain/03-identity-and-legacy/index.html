    Identity Resolution and Legacy Data¶

    How the system decides that two records describe the same human, and how four decades of paper and spreadsheet history gets brought in without corrupting live data.

    This is the highest-risk area of the project. Get it wrong and either the history is unusable, or worse, two people's records get merged and you leak one member's data to another. The design below is deliberately conservative.

    1. Why this is hard¶

    A 1994 roster has: a name, maybe a birth date, an address, a unit, and a year. No email, no unique key, no way to log in. Meanwhile:

    • Names change (marriage, adoption, correction of a misspelling).
    • Names repeat. In a youth organization spanning decades, "Jan Novák" is many people.
    • Diacritics and transliterations vary across decades of manual entry (Novák / Novak).
    • Birth dates are often wrong by a digit, or recorded as year-only.
    • The same human appears in multiple rosters under slightly different spellings.

    So: automatic matching will produce both false positives and false negatives. False positive (two people merged) is a privacy incident. False negative (one person split) is a data-quality annoyance. The design must therefore bias hard toward false negatives — never merge on a guess.

    This document is about one half of a person. Everything it resolves is identity: the fields that make a row a particular human — names, birth date, the account, contact points, issued identifiers. Everything the organization records about that human beyond those is descriptive data, and it is deliberately absent from every table below: no descriptive value is a match signal, and none may become one. A clothing size or a dietary habit identifies nobody, and making matching depend on one would make the reliability of a person's history depend on what the organization happened to collect about them (08-person-data.md, D-085). The line between the two halves is drawn by what a machine may act on without a human, not by how delicate a fact feels; the test is stated in 08-person-data.md.

    2. The three record classes¶

    Every person row carries record_origin:

    Origin Meaning Confidence Has account?
    self_registered A human created this by registering high yes
    staff_created A registrar entered this person high maybe
    legacy_import Imported from historical records low no

    Legacy-imported people are provisional. They are fully queryable — "who was in Troop 12 in 1998" must work — but they are marked as unverified, they cannot be logged into, and they are the only rows eligible to be absorbed by a merge.

    Rule: a merge always absorbs the lower-confidence row into the higher-confidence one. An import can never overwrite a self-registered person's data.

    3. Identity hypotheses, not merges¶

    The core mechanism. An identity hypothesis is a proposal that two person rows are the same human. It is a first-class table row, with a confidence score, evidence, a status, and a human decision.

    The row holds the pair as person_a_id and person_b_id, stored in one order: person_a_id names the lower id, and the two are never the same person — a pair has one canonical spelling rather than two.

    confidence is the matcher's score between 0 and 1; match_signals records which fields agreed and how strongly, which is what a reviewer reads to see why it was proposed. status is one of proposed, needs_review, confirmed, rejected, superseded. proposed_by says where the proposal came from — import_job, system, person or staff — and proposed_at when. reviewed_by, reviewed_at and review_note are the human part, and a hypothesis may not reach confirmed or rejected without a reviewed_at. At most one live hypothesis exists per pair — live means proposed, needs_review or confirmed.

    Lifecycle¶

      matcher proposes ──> proposed ──┬── staff confirms ──> confirmed ──> [merge executed]
                                  ├── staff rejects  ──> rejected (the pair becomes
                                  │                       proposable again — see §4)
                                  └── person asserts ──> needs_review → staff confirms/rejects

    Two entry points:

    1. Import or background matching proposes hypotheses automatically at low confidence.
    2. A registering person asserts their own history — "I was a member 1990–2010" — which creates a hypothesis against the candidate rows, usually with proposed_by = 'person'.

    A person's own assertion is evidence, not proof. It must still be reviewed, because claiming someone else's history is the obvious attack on an import-based system — and the prize is that person's data. Never auto-confirm a self-asserted link.

    4. Match signals¶

    The matcher scores candidate pairs on independent signals. Recorded in match_signals so a reviewer sees why the system proposed a match:

    Signal Strength Notes
    Exact legacy member number decisive The corpus carries these (D-056). Same number, same person — matched, not scored.
    Exact birth date + exact normalised name strong Still not automatic — twins with similar names exist.
    Birth date within ±1 day or year-only match + name moderate Common with hand-written records.
    Name match after normalisation (diacritics, case, spacing) moderate Necessary but weak alone; "Jan Novák" is not unique.
    Same unit + overlapping membership period moderate Corroborating, not identifying.
    Same postal address at the same time moderate Strong if the address is distinctive. Read from person_contact history — there is no household table (D-057).
    Guardian name match weak Useful for minors in old records.
    Name-only fuzzy match weak — never sufficient alone

    The decisive row changes the matcher's job. With a number on the record, the question is not "are these two the same person?" but "does this number already point somewhere?" — a lookup. The scored signals below are then for the rows the number cannot resolve: legacy records predating the numbering, records where the number is illegible, and people who were never issued one. At thousands of rows (D-056) this split is what makes D-004 affordable: the numbered rows are the trustworthy set and the rest is a work queue.

    Normalisation¶

    Applied before comparison, and the normalised form is stored so matching is reproducible:

    • Unicode NFKD, strip diacritics, casefold, collapse whitespace.
    • Common nickname/legal-name equivalences (configurable per locale — Jan/Jano, Josef/Josip).
    • Typo distance (Levenshtein/Jaro-Winkler) above a threshold counts as a partial match, never a full one.

    Thresholds¶

    • confidence < 0.60 — not surfaced. Stored only for diagnostics.
    • 0.60 ≤ confidence < 0.90 — proposed, queued for human review.
    • confidence ≥ 0.90 — still requires human confirmation, but flagged as high priority. There is no auto-merge threshold. A privacy incident from a bad merge is worse than any amount of manual review, and merges are irreversible in practice.

    Assumption: you want no automatic merging at all, even for near-certain matches. If the volume of historical data makes that infeasible, an explicit opt-in auto-merge tier can be added — but it should be a deliberate decision, recorded in the decision log.

    5. The merge operation¶

    Merging is a soft merge: the absorbed row survives as a tombstone.

    person (surviving, id=100)          person (absorbed, id=847, tombstoned)
        ▲                                     │
        │                                     ▼
        └────────── person_merge ────► merged_into_person_id = 100
                 (id, surviving_person_id, merged_person_id,
                  merged_at, merged_by, hypothesis_id, reason)

    What moves¶

    Table Action
    person_contact Reassigned to survivor. Conflicts (two is_primary of the same type) resolved by review, not by rule.
    person_identifier Reassigned. Duplicate scheme+value collapses to one.
    person_attribute Reassigned. Two current values for one code is a review item, never a choice made by rule — this table holds one value per person and code with no validity period to keep both, so unlike two past addresses a merge cannot leave the pair standing: the pair is raised as a field_conflict and a human decides, exactly as two is_primary contacts of one type are decided. Where the definition allows several values (allow_multiple), the two rows simply coexist and nothing is decided (../data-model/09-attributes.md §3).
    person_name Reassigned, keeping validity periods — this is how a maiden name survives.
    account Reassigned to survivor. If the survivor already has an account, the absorbed account is closed, not deleted.
    membership Reassigned. Overlapping periods flagged for review — with one type (D-037) and a person-level no-overlap rulet, an overlap here means a bad merge.
    unit_participation Reassigned, with the same overlap check.
    guardianship (as dependent) Reassigned.
    guardianship (as guardian) Reassigned.
    role_assignment, access_grant Reassigned. Union of permissions results — this is the security-critical step.
    person_merge Row inserted here.
    field_conflict Populated for anything that could not be resolved automatically.

    What does not happen¶

    • Nothing is deleted. The absorbed person row keeps its id and gains merged_into_person_id. All historical foreign keys pointing at it still resolve, so "who was in Troop 12 in 1998" keeps working.
    • Overlapping memberships are never silently deduplicated. Two overlapping child memberships after a merge is suspicious — it may mean the merge was wrong. It becomes a review item.

    The security rule that must not be skipped¶

    Merging unions the two people's permissions. If a bad merge is performed, the survivor gains the absorbed person's access. Therefore:

    1. A merge is always performed by a human with person.merge permission.
    2. The merged result's effective permissions are re-displayed for confirmation before the merge commits.
    3. Every merge is reversible via person_merge in the sense that the assignment can be moved back — but access_grant rows created during the merged period cannot be attributed. Hence rule 2.
    4. Merges invalidate cached permissions. See 06-authorization-model.md.

    6. Reversing a merge¶

    Support unmerge, because mistakes will happen. It is not symmetric — you cannot know which grants were exercised while merged — but you can restore the row split:

    1. Clear person.merged_into_person_id on the absorbed row. The tombstone comes off.
    2. Reassign rows created before merged_at back to the absorbed person.
    3. Flag rows created after merged_at for human review. Those are the ambiguous ones, and they are reviewed rather than split by a rule.
    4. Record the unmerge on the person_merge row, as reversed_at and reversed_by.

    The access_grant rows are the specific hazard. Any grant created during the merged window might belong to either human. Flag every one of them; do not guess.

    7. Legacy import pipeline¶

    Staged, reviewed, idempotent. Never a direct write into live tables.

      source file(s)
       │
       ▼
  [1] staging_import          ── raw rows, verbatim, with the source file + line ref
       │
       ▼
  [2] staging_import_row      ── parsed: normalised name, parsed birth date,
       │                          detected unit, parse warnings
       ▼
  [3] matching                ── for each row, find candidate person rows and score them
       │
       ▼
  [4] review queue            ── human confirms: link to existing | create new | discard
       │
       ▼
  [5] apply                   ── writes person / membership / unit_participation
       │
       ▼
  [6] import_log              ── what was applied, by whom, when; reversal instructions

    Requirements¶

    • Idempotent. Re-running the same import must not duplicate people. Each staged row carries a deterministic source_row_hash; re-import matches on it.
    • Reversible per batch. import_log records every row created or modified in a batch, so a bad batch can be rolled back without touching other data.
    • Never overwrites self-registered data. If a legacy row matches a self_registered person, the import may add historical memberships but never alters names, contacts, identifiers, or descriptive values the person maintains themselves.
    • Preserves provenance. Every imported row records import_batch_id and source_reference (file + line), so any value can be traced back to the paper it came from. When someone disputes a historical record, you can show them where it came from.
    • Records uncertainty rather than guessing. A row that cannot be parsed cleanly goes to the review queue with the raw value intact — it does not get a best-effort guess written as if it were fact.

    Raw columns become codes¶

    The pipeline was drawn when a legacy column could only ever be a name, a date or a unit. Old rosters also carry columns about the person — a remarks column, a "medical" column, a list of shirt sizes — and each of those arrives at step [5] as a value against a code, not as a blob beside the record. Two consequences for staging, where the rest of the rules for this live (../data-model/09-attributes.md §7):

    • A raw column that maps to no code stops the batch, unless someone maps it or marks it discarded with a reason. An unmapped column is a decade of something the organization used to collect vanishing quietly, which is the class of failure the rest of this document exists to prevent on the identity side.
    • A column nobody recognises becomes a proposed code named from the column, never a guess at an existing code: an import-time merge is invisible and unreviewable, and the review queue above is for people, not for vocabulary. The column→code mapping is recorded once per batch, and changing it is curation with an audit entry rather than a migration.

    Historical memberships from imports¶

    An imported roster row typically yields:

    • a person with record_origin = 'legacy_import'
    • a membership with valid_from = roster year start, valid_to = roster year end (or null if it was the last known roster and the member's status is unknown)
    • a unit_participation row linking them to the historical unit
    • data_confidence = 'low' on all of the above

    What a 1998 roster actually tells you is participation, not membership. This is the one place the membership/participation split (D-041) really bites on legacy data, and it is worth being deliberate about rather than generating both rows by reflex:

    The old record says It becomes Confidence
    "was in Pack 12 in 1998" unit_participation, 1998-01-01 → 1998-12-31 Reasonable — the roster is decent evidence of this
    "was a member in 1998" (a membership list, or a fee ledger) membership Good — a payment record is strong evidence
    "was in Pack 12 in 1998" with no fee evidence unit_participation and a membership marked data_confidence = 'low' Weak — this is an inference, and the discussion below applies

    The temptation is to generate a membership for every roster row, because that is what the old data model implied. Do it, but mark it as inferred, because is_current_member() and the permission gate (D-039) both read membership, and an inferred membership from 1998 is harmless while an inferred membership row that runs to valid_to IS NULL would make someone a member today. The no-overlap rule makes this concrete, whatever ends up enforcing it: a person-level "one open membership" rule means the import must not leave an open membership for someone whose only evidence is a roster.

    End dates are often unknown. For an imported member last seen in a 2003 roster, the membership should end at a defensible date — not left open, which would falsely make them a current member, and not guessed. Convention: end at the end of the school/organizational year of the last roster they appear in, marked as inferred. The participation row can be bounded the same way. Document the rule you choose; it will be questioned.

    8. The returning-member case¶

    The scenario you described — member 1990–2010, returns 2024 — should be the smooth path, because it is a common and important one (returning alumni become adult leaders and donors).

    Flow:

    1. Person registers in 2024, self-asserting past membership.
    2. The system matches them against legacy rows and proposes hypotheses.
    3. A registrar reviews and confirms.
    4. The person's two memberships (1990–2010, 2024–present) now sit under one person row.
    5. Their profile shows their full history; the gap is explicit data.
    6. Any seniority or "years of service" calculation reads across both.

    Steps 2–3 can be streamlined when the match is strong (exact old membership number), but the confirmation step remains, for the reason in §3.

    Self-service without weakening the guarantee¶

    Rather than auto-linking, give the person a claim flow: they assert a past membership, staff confirm. Record the assertion on the hypothesis row, so that if the claim later proves false, you can see it was self-asserted rather than discovered.

    9. Open issues¶

    • Volume and formats of historical data — determines whether manual review of every match is feasible, or whether a high-confidence tier is needed.
    • Whether the old system issued member numbers (would make §4's strongest signal available).
    • How much of an old roster becomes a membership and how much becomes participation (§7). This got harder when the two were split apart (D-041): the import must decide per record class, and an inferred membership running to today would make a 2003 roster member a current member under the permission gate.
    • Retention periods for members who request erasure — the shape is settled (redact the person, keep the financial and membership skeleton), but the durations need legal input. See ../open-questions.md.
    • Whether twins/siblings with similar names and the same birth date need a documented disambiguation procedure (recommend: yes, and never merge them automatically).

    Resolved and no longer open, kept visible so the reasoning survives: the erasure mechanism (redact the person, keep the financial and membership skeleton) — only the retention durations remain open.
