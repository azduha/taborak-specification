    Membership Lifecycle¶

    Register → request → accept → pay → member. Plus the parts that are usually forgotten: gaps, returns, multiple periods, and renewal.

    1. The flow you described¶

    "The people can register on their own to get access to the system, however they become member only if they request a membership, they get accepted and pay a yearly fee."

    Four distinct stages, each a separate record, because each can independently fail, stall, or be reversed:

    Stage Record Can be reversed? What it means
    1. Register person + account account can be closed "This person can log in."
    2. Request membership_request yes — reject/withdraw "This person applied."
    3. Accept membership_request.status = 'approved' yes — revoke before activation "The organization said yes."
    4. Pay fee_obligation + payment refund "The obligation is met."
    5. Activate membership opened yes — lapse/terminate "This person is a member."

    Why stage 5 is separate from stage 3. Approval and activation are distinct because payment sits between them. A person can be approved and never pay; the organization needs to see that state ("approved, awaiting payment") to chase it, and it must not appear as a member.

    Decided: membership activates on payment (D-040). membership opens at stage 5, and pending_payment is a real membership status so that "approved, awaiting payment" is a visible worklist rather than an invisible backlog.

    Two doors, one gate each¶

    The flow above is one of two ways a person ends up in the organization's records, and for eight rounds this document wrote as if it were the only one. There are two entry paths and each has exactly one gate, with no third control anywhere (D-095, whose reasoning D-098 transfers to groups intact — joining one is still somebody else's act):

    Membership of the unit A group
    How you get in You pay. Stages 1–5 above are this door, and it is the only self-service entry in the system — it opens a membership and nothing beside it Somebody else writes a person_group_member row
    What stops you The fee person_group.member.manage, held at the unit — which reaches every group — or granted on one group as an item
    Money The yearly fee Nothing

    Nobody writes themselves into a group. The people who run a group add people to it; that is the whole of the rule, and it is carried by a permission rather than by a workflow. person_group.member.manage has no self-scoped variant and none will be added — a grant is always "over this group's roster", never "over my own", so the shape of request that would let a person place themselves is not refused by a check, it is not expressible. The same holds for offices: nobody appoints themselves, and the catalog holds no code that could say otherwise (../appendix/permission-catalog.md §5).

    No fee below the membership, and no model wants one. The fee is the organization's; joining a group carries no money — "into the sub-units, there is no fee, it is entirely managed by the organization, including adding more members." That was said about units and it transfers unchanged, a group being among the things listed as having no fee (D-098). Putting a fee on a member row would immediately ask who sets the amount, who chases it, and what happens to a child whose family cannot pay a group's arrears, and none of those questions exist in the corpus or in any answer given. That is a refused model, not an unasked one.

    What the asymmetry costs. The group door has no membership check behind it — a counselor may add somebody who is not a member, and nothing in the write stops them, because D-095 refuses a third gate as firmly as it refuses the first two. What keeps that person from reading anything is MEMBERS in the grants that matter — the test D-039 was, now carried in the grant text rather than the resolver (D-100) — which is what that test was always for. A group membership is a fact about belonging, not a credential: a member of a group can do nothing by being one, and the tidier table does not quietly promote it (D-098). The other cost is directional and appears in the other direction at import: the membership door is the only place a person's own initiative can produce a record, which is why the legacy corpus could be matched against people and never against groups (domain/03 §7).

    And one cost is new, named where D-097 names it. Under the deleted hierarchy, access to a body's children had to be granted by that body, by people who recruited its leader and can be asked. A group's roster write is the same shape of permission and may be held at the unit, where it reaches every group in the organization, so what is left of the safeguarding story is a permission held by a named adult, an audit trail whose recorded values expire after two years (D-088), and a report of who holds the write — minus the part where the named adult was somebody the body chose. Nothing in the remaining model reproduces that property, and this document says so rather than letting the reader infer it.

    2. Membership request¶

    A request records the person it is for and the membership type asked for, and nothing else. status is one of draft, submitted, under_review, approved, rejected, withdrawn, expired, and submitted_at says when it went in. The review is carried on the same row: who took it up, when they decided, and on a rejection a rejection_reason_code with a free-text rejection_note beside it. An approved request points at the membership it produced; the link is not optional once the approval exists.

    A request asks for membership of the unit, and it names no group. requested_unit_id is deleted, along with the index over it (D-098). ~~The unit named on a request is a preference, not a door: a request opens the root and produces a root participation row, and the pack the applicant wrote down is a sentence addressed to whoever reviews it.~~ Withdrawn by D-098, which also deleted the table the column pointed at (D-097). The want behind the field is genuine — a reviewer would like to know where the applicant hopes to turn up — and it is served by the notes the row already carries, which is what the field always was in effect: a sentence, carrying no authority. Which group a new member lands in is a write on the group, not a preference on the request: one person_group_member row, written by somebody holding person_group.member.manage (§1), in a separate act with its own permission and its own audit entry. An approved request that produced no group row is a complete, ordinary outcome.

    Why a request is not a membership with a status¶

    Tempting to collapse them, but:

    • A request can be rejected, producing no membership at all. A membership row with status rejected would pollute every "list members" query forever.
    • A person may submit several requests over time (rejected in 2024, approved in 2025). One table for both would need a request-version column and every query would need to filter it.
    • Requests carry review data (who reviewed, why, what was checked) that has no meaning on a membership.
    • Requests have different permissions: submitting is self-service; reviewing is a staff capability.

    There are no request requirements¶

    Removed entirely (D-044). A request is accepted on identity and payment alone: no blocking checklist, no evidence collection, no waiver workflow, no requirement_code and no membership_request_requirement table.

    Your answer, asked what a request must contain to be accepted: "Just identity and the fee — nothing to verify." The option to keep the mechanism as empty infrastructure for later was offered and not taken, which is the right call — a checklist that is always empty is a checklist nobody maintains, and it would have implied a gate that does not exist.

    What this does not weaken. Two things that look like requirements are unaffected, because neither was a membership requirement to begin with:

    • Guardian consent for a minor's account is a fact about the guardianship relation, held in guardianship_permission, not a box ticked during an application (data-model/04).
    • Who may hold an office — a scoutmaster must be an adult — was never a property of membership, and after D-100 it is not a stored property of the office either: position.min_age is deleted with positions and role carries no age column, so what remains is is_minor() in a report (§7). Membership is ageless (D-038).

    And background checks do not exist in this system at all (D-043). If the organization introduces them, the home is a dated record with an expiry, and it belongs with the office rather than the membership gate — a lapsed check is a reason someone may not hold a role, not a reason they stop being a member. Which layer would hold it is unresolved, since the layer that used to hold eligibility went out with positions (D-100; §7 and §9).

    3. Membership¶

    A membership row belongs to one person and one membership type, and carries a status of pending_payment, active, lapsed, terminated, completed or suspended — always chosen deliberately, never defaulted. The period is valid_from and valid_to, and an absent valid_to means the period is still open: it is the current membership, not a row someone forgot to close. joined_via_request_id records the request it came out of, termination_reason_code the reason it was ended, data_confidence is high, medium or low, and import_batch_id is set on imported rows and on nothing else. **For one round these two For one round these two columns were dead on this table — the corpus carried no payment evidence, so the batch wrote person rows and left membership to the live register, and this paragraph said a membership row is never imported D-093 reversed that, because it turned out the source states a membership period directly rather than only attendance: the import now writes membership rows for the periods the export states, data_confidence = 'low' and import_batch_id set on every one of them, both ends of the period dated (domain/03 §7). What did not come back is the open-ended row: an imported membership never runs to today and none is inferred from an attendance row, which is the half of the old rule that was doing the protecting. So data_confidence on this table has two live sources again and they mean different things: on a backdated row it is a human's judgement (membership.backdate, attributed to whoever typed it), on an imported row it is the batch speaking for itself.

    Overlap rules — the invariant that matters¶

    A person must not hold two open memberships at the same time, and the rule is enforced by the database rather than by application code. Its precise statement is in data-model/03-membership.md §3; what it says, in words:

    • Two membership periods of one person may not cover the same date while either of them is pending_payment, active or suspended. Those three statuses are the whole of the rule's reach — a lapsed row, a terminated one and a completed one are outside it, which is why an ending period and a later beginning one sit beside each other without complaint.
    • Periods are half-open, so a membership ending on 30 June and another opening on 30 June do not collide.

    The constraint used to be scoped to membership_type_id, because a person might legitimately be an honorary and an adult member at once. With one membership type (D-037) that escape hatch is gone, and the constraint becomes the clean person-level invariant: one person, one open membership, ever. The type column was what made "am I a paid-up member?" a hard question; now it is one row, one status, one date. (This sentence cited D-041 until round 10; that decision was about belonging to a sub-unit, its table is deleted (D-097), and nothing about this invariant ever depended on it.)

    This is the invariant the import now writes against. For one round the sentence could be written the other way — with no imported membership, there is nothing for it to violate — and D-093 removed the premise, not the rule (domain/03 §7). Say exactly what fires, because the rule's reach is narrow: it watches the three live statuses, so what the exclusion constraint refuses at apply time is an imported period colliding with a live membership — an export period that runs into or past the person's current one, which an open-ended imported row would produce for thousands of people in one transaction. What it does not refuse is an overlap between two closed imported periods: the statuses are outside its reach by design, so a source file that double-books somebody across 1993 lands quietly, marked only as data_confidence = 'low'. That is the honest cost of the same decision that keeps a returning member's old period from gating anything today, and it is why the batch is reviewed before it applies rather than after.

    Assumption: btree_gist is available (PostgreSQL), because a temporal overlap is the one rule that can only be refused during the write that would create it. If the target database cannot express exclusion constraints, the fallback is a check inside the writing transaction plus the nightly consistency report — one of the strongest guarantees in the model degrading into the weakest class (data-model/00 §6a, open question F-10 shape 1): an application-level count is wrong exactly when two writers run at once, which is when an overlap is actually created, and the report finds it a day later in a form nobody can attribute. So it is not merely "weaker" and must be recorded as an accepted risk, chosen deliberately, rather than discovered in a migration tool's limitations.

    There is one membership type, and membership is ageless¶

    A type row is a code and a name, four calendar values, and a sort_order. The calendar values start at twelve months' duration, expiry_month 1 and expiry_day 1 — the fee expires on 1 January (D-047) — and renewal_grace_days 60, which is the renewal window and not a status (D-048).

    Seeded with exactly one row, member (D-037). Everything the old type carried — category, min_age, max_age, requires_background_check, requires_guardian_consent, fee_schedule_id — is gone, and what is left is the two parameters a period needs: how long it lasts, and how long after it the person may renew without re-applying. The fee itself is not a property of the type at all: it is a flat per-person amount that lives on fee_schedule and is snapshotted onto each obligation, so "what did we charge in 1998?" is answered from the obligation, not the type.

    Membership is ageless (D-038). No age floor, no age ceiling, no age-out, no child→adult transition. Age survives as a fact about a person (person.birth_date) and matters where the law makes it matter: guardianship and safeguarding (§7 was the age-transition section — there is no longer a §7). Being a counselor or a leader is a role, never a membership type (D-100).

    4. Multiple memberships over a lifetime¶

    This is your 1990–2010 / 2024–present case. It is not an edge case; it is the normal shape of a long-lived organization's data.

    person 100 — Jan Novák
  ├── membership #1   member   1990-09-01 → 2010-06-30   terminated  (left)
  │     └── (nothing here: the corpus names no groups, so nobody's data says where he was)
  ├── (gap 2010 → 2024 — 14 years, no membership rows)
  └── membership #2   member   2024-10-01 → NULL          active
        └── person_group_member  Thursday group  2024-10-01 → NULL
        └── role_holder          counselors      2025-01-01 → NULL

    Note what the two membership rows now share: the same type. They differ only in their period and their end reason. In the original framing this person was a child member and then an adult member; in this one they were a member, twice, and were a child and then an adult the whole time. The second framing is the true one, and it is why the memorable case still works: the person never changed, and neither did the type — only the period did.

    Half of this picture is reachable without a human, and the other half is not. Membership #1 and its dates are exactly what the membership export states, so the one-off import writes them — bounded, data_confidence = 'low', batch id set (D-093). The group row is the other half: the corpus names no groups either, so a 1990s roster exists only because somebody who remembers typed it, and the import owes no belonging rows of any kind (D-097, D-098). What the diagram used to draw beside each membership — a root-unit participation row mirroring the very same period — is gone with the table, and the picture is now about the two periods, which is the most the import can actually say. The 1990s belonging the deleted rows might once have held was never in the corpus either (D-092), so what is lost is a place it could have been written down, not a fact that was stored and then thrown away.

    One row of the new diagram is thinner than the row it replaced, and the difference is a fact about roles. position_assignment could name the body a person led; role_holder cannot, because D-097 left exactly one scope and there is no body to hold an office of — a role is always held at the unit. So "he was scoutmaster of that group" is no longer a stored pair: the office and the group are two rows about the same person, and the link between them is somebody's reading of them. D-100 records the neighbouring loss in the same register — the elected-versus-appointed distinction survives only as a role the organization invents — and neither is a hole this module can fill.

    Everything the system needs follows from this:

    • "Is this person a member?" → is there a membership with valid_to IS NULL (or covering today) and status = 'active'?
    • "How long have they been a member?" → sum of membership period lengths, not today − first join date. The gap must not count.
    • "How old were they when they joined?" → membership #2.valid_from − person.birth_date.
    • "Are they an adult?" → is_minor(person_id), computed from person.birth_date. In 2005 the same query said yes. The membership row is identical either way; only the person's birth date and today's date moved.

    Never store is_member, member_since, member_type, or is_child on person. Every one of those is derivable, and every one of them will be wrong the moment history is corrected or a person returns.

    5. Transitions and who can perform them¶

    From To Trigger Permission required
    — draft person starts an application own
    draft submitted person submits membership.request.create (own, or the guardianship channel at write for a guardian — D-074)
    submitted under_review reviewer picks it up membership.request.review
    under_review approved reviewer approves membership.request.approve
    under_review rejected reviewer rejects membership.request.approve
    any withdrawn applicant withdraws own, or guardian with permission
    approved membership pending_payment approval processed system
    pending_payment active payment confirmed system (or fee.record for manual)
    active lapsed fee unpaid past the organization's expiry date membership.lapse or the yearly sweep
    active terminated disciplinary / cause membership.terminate — separate, more restricted permission
    active suspended temporary hold membership.suspend

    There is no completed row any more. It existed only for ageing out, and ageing out no longer exists (D-038).

    Termination must not be the same permission as lapsing. A lapse is administrative (nobody paid). A termination is a decision about a person's standing in a youth organization, often with safeguarding implications. Conflating them means every volunteer who chases unpaid fees can also expel a member.

    A lapse has two faces, and only one of them is a human decision. A lapse triggered by non-payment is the yearly sweep doing its job — nobody decided anything, and "why did I lose access?" has a date for an answer rather than a person. A lapse triggered by a staff decision is a deliberate act with a permission behind it. Both land in the same status; access_log is what distinguishes them, which is another reason the lapse is recorded as an event and not just a column change.

    6. Renewal and the yearly fee¶

    The fee is flat, per person, yearly, and it expires on a fixed date — typically January (D-040). The membership-relevant part, in full:

    Renewal opens a new period, not an extended one¶

    Shape A, decided (D-016). The previous row closes and a new one opens, linked by membership.renewed_from_membership_id. Extending valid_to in place would be fewer rows and would silently lose the most commonly asked question about renewals — did they renew every year, or skip one and come back?

    The renewal window replaces the grace status¶

    The old design had a grace status: during it the person was still a member. That is gone. What replaces it is a window tracked as dates, not as a status (D-040):

    When What is true
    On 1 January The membership lapses. They are not a member, and member permissions stop.
    Inside the window (60 days after expiry) Still not a member — but they may renew without re-applying, and a payment now opens the new period at the expiry date, so the record shows no gap.
    After 2 March A payment opens a new membership at the payment date. The gap is real. Still no new application required.

    The 1 January date has a consequence that reaches past the membership table. The yearly sweep is the only bulk event in a system carefully built to have none: on a single day, every membership in the organization lapses at once. That makes it the day every MEMBERS-gated answer in the organization moves at once, and the day renewal traffic peaks. It is no longer also the day the permission cache is invalidated for everyone: D-114 deleted the stored answer, so the period ending and the lapsed status are the whole of the change, and each member's next check sees it. And it used to be, on the same day, the day the system administrator lost access to the system they administer, which is the hazard D-051 existed to close.

    That mechanism is deleted (D-100): there is no system.gate_exempt, no person_holds_gate_exempt, and no resolver clause left to hold a door open. The answer now sits in grant text. A grant written MEMBERS * ROLE(it) stops applying to its holder at the sweep; a grant written ROLE(it) does not, because MEMBERS is simply not in it — which is exactly how D-100 says a whole office is made exempt, and the same sentence that makes the administrator survivable is the one that makes a lapsed treasurer dangerous. Assumption: which offices are written without MEMBERS, and who decides, is not settled by D-100 — the standing report of live grants naming a ROLE(...) operand without MEMBERS is what makes each choice visible to a human (data-model/03 §6), and §9 lists the undecided half as a question.

    All four calendar values are columns — duration_months, expiry_month, expiry_day, renewal_grace_days — and that is a requirement rather than a convenience. The organizational year runs with the calendar, and 60 days was chosen for the window on the explicit condition that it stays easy to change (D-048): "Make it 60 days, but it should not be hard to modify." Because the window and the date live in a row rather than in a scheduled job, moving them is an UPDATE. The failure this prevents is a job that contains the literal 60 — invisible, and wrong the first time the organization changes its mind.

    Why dates rather than a status. Your own answers pointed two ways on this, and it is worth being honest about that: you described the window as existing "for them to pay to stay members" — which implies still being a member — and separately confirmed they are "expired now, but they can rejoin without re-applying." D-040 resolves it as not a member during the window, but the record shows no gap once they pay, because the alternative puts a third state into every permission check and makes a lapse undetectable until the window closes. If the softer reading is what the organization actually wants, it is a change to the renewal rule, not a rewrite.

    The backdating is the load-bearing detail. Without it, someone who pays in February for a January expiry is recorded as having a one-month gap and is misclassified as a returning member — which would pollute the returning-member query and, worse, is_current_member() would have said "no" for a month when the person considers themselves continuously paid up.

    So the rule to implement is one decision, not a formula on dates alone: the renewal period starts at the old expiry, not at the payment date, whenever the payment lands inside the window. Landing inside the window is what selects the expiry date, and it is what produces no gap; landing after it selects the payment date, and the gap stands.

    Note what the bulk lapse used to cost, and what it costs now. The lapse happens to everyone at once, on the same date, in a bulk sweep (D-039). ~~That is one cache-invalidation event touching every member rather than a steady trickle.~~ There is no cache-invalidation event at all: D-114 deleted the answers that event used to clear, and a bulk write that shrinks an audience shrinks it live — every grant written MEMBERS * … stops reaching a lapsed member on their next check, with the sweep as the only job in the sequence rather than the sweep and a rebuild. What the bulk shape still costs is computation, and it is now paid on open: a nightly rebuild used to pre-pay the expensive screens, so the tab on the permissions page, the per-item preview and the who holds this listing each evaluate a changed answer for every one of those people when somebody opens them — affordable at the size D-112 states, and precisely what that entry's trigger reopens, with D-114 naming the reopen answer: materialize effective_permission per person and call it that. See data-model/05 §10.

    7. There are no age transitions¶

    This section used to describe two automatic transitions — ageing out of a child membership, and becoming an adult member. Both are gone (D-038). In their place, one sentence: a person's membership does not change when they turn 18, because it was never about their age.

    What a system built around this must still handle — none of it a membership transition:

    Concern Where it lives now
    A guardian's authority at majority Guardianship — and the system does not notice the birthday. No auto-expiry and no review queue; a person ends the row (D-069)
    Who may be added to a group Nowhere in the data. org_unit_type.allows_minors was removed by D-095 and the units it was a column on by D-097; no column says which group takes children, and none will. The control is person_group.member.manage, held at the unit or on one group, in the hands of the people who run it (D-098 transfers D-095 intact), and domain/04-organization-structure.md owns it
    Whether a person may hold an office Nowhere either. position.min_age is deleted with positions (D-100) and role carries no age column, so what remains is is_minor() in a report over role_holder. That is a deletion rather than a move: the eligibility check this row pointed at no longer exists, and §9 carries the question
    Consent for a minor's own account guardianship_permission, not a membership requirement
    Planning around age groups A query over person.birth_date. It is a report, not a state change.

    One row of that table is emptier than it looks, and a membership document should say so out loud. This section could once answer "which units take minors?" from a flag on a type. That flag is gone, the units went with it a round later, and no column on person_group replaces it, so what this module has to offer on safeguarding is a permission held by named adults, an audit trail whose recorded values expire after two years (D-088), and a report of who holds the write. The argument that this is a stronger control than a boolean, and the plain statement of what it cannot answer, belong to the groups module (domain/04-organization-structure.md); what belongs here is the observation that the fee gate never protected a child either — it protects the organization's membership roll, and it was never load bearing for placement. What D-097 additionally takes is the part where the adult holding that write was somebody the body itself chose; it is recorded as a loss there and not argued away here.

    The edge case this removes. The old design had to reason about a person who was simultaneously finishing a child membership and starting an adult one, and about whether the overlap constraint would block it. That case cannot arise: there is one membership, it runs continuously, and turning 18 is an event in the person's life that the membership does not notice.

    8. Since 1985 — the ten queries this design must answer¶

    Stated explicitly so they can be tested. All are point-in-time queries and all must remain answerable indefinitely — with two exceptions this document now carries openly rather than editing away: query 3 has no answer for any group before go-live, because the corpus names no groups, and query 8 has no answer at all any more, because the thing it checked against no longer exists. A list of ten that quietly became eight is how a specification loses a requirement:

    1. Who is an active member today?
    2. Who was a member on any given date in the past?
    3. Who was in group G on date D? — a point-in-time read over person_group_member, whose rows are dated at both ends for exactly this question (D-098); the group's own lifetime is a second, different fact and both are kept. "Who was in the organization on date D?" is now a membership read with no belonging row involved: two queries, two shapes, and no "and everything nested under it" left to ask about (D-097). For a group this stops at go-live, and the stop is the corpus's, not the query's: the import names no groups either, so "who was in the Thursday group in 2011?" has no answer at all rather than a wrong one, and every group row from before launch is somebody's typed recollection (domain/03 §7, D-092)
    4. What is the full membership history of person P, including gaps?
    5. Who is inside the renewal window right now? (a case needing follow-up — the worklist that replaced "who is in grace")
    6. Who was approved but never paid? (a case needing follow-up)
    7. Which members are under 18 and have no guardian recorded? (a safeguarding gap, not a membership one — the old background-check query was retired by D-043)
    8. Which minors are in groups that do not accept them? — no longer answerable, and left numbered rather than renumbered away, so that the gap sits on the list instead of off it. It was a data-quality check against org_unit_type.allows_minors; D-095 removed that column and D-097 removed the units it was a column on, and a group is explicitly listed as carrying no such rule (D-098). So there is nothing to check a placement against, in either vocabulary, and no plan for there to be one. What stands in its place is upstream — nobody is added to a group by somebody who does not hold that group's roster write — plus a report of who holds that write, which is a question about grants, not about people (§7)
    9. Who are the returning members — more than one membership with a gap between?
    10. How many members did the organization have in each year since 1985? (historical reporting — must include closed memberships and must not double-count)

    Round 8 said four of these reach only as far back as membership rows reach, because the legacy import created none, and that for the decades arriving from paper queries 2, 4, 9 and 10 return participation. Half of that is now false (D-090, D-093). The source states membership periods, so the import writes bounded memberships and those four queries do reach into the corpus — over an export, though, not over a register, and the only thing telling the two kinds of row apart is data_confidence = 'low' and a batch id, which no report is obliged to notice (domain/03 §7). So query 10 is answerable in a form that should be labelled on the way out of the chart: a member count where the source states a period, and nothing where it does not.

    The wrong answer is still exactly one decision away, and the ban that stops it is one sentence. Generate a membership per attendance row and every historical query instantly returns a confident number built on nothing, while is_current_member() quietly hands today's authority to people last seen in 2003 (D-039). What stops that is not the column set but the rule: never an open-ended imported membership, never one inferred from attendance. What round 8 got right and kept is the reasoning underneath — evidence and inference are different rows, and a file that says member 1990–1995 is the first kind while a line-up of names at an event is the second.

    The question that did not come back is query 3 for anything before go-live. Importing membership rows says nothing about which group a person was in, because a membership is not a placement; the group answer stays empty until somebody types a roster in by hand — which is the only way a group row can be written at all, the corpus naming no groups (D-092, D-098) — and the honest reading of a pre-go-live roster is that it is somebody's recollection. What is not lost is the shape of the answer after go-live: the member rows are dated precisely so that "who was in the Thursday group in 2011?" stays a point-in-time read that survives the audit trail's payload expiry (D-097's second loss, which is the unit framing — the subtree question — rather than the roster).

    These stay on the list because they are answerable, indefinitely, for everything this system registers itself — and that indefinite half is itself a decision now, the accounting skeleton being kept as the organization's history (D-088). For the corpus, print which of the two answers a number came from.

    Query 10 is the one that fails in most systems, and it fails specifically because someone made membership a boolean.

    Query 1 is now slightly harder than it looks, and deliberately: it is not status = 'active' alone, because a membership also stops being current at valid_to. is_current_member() is the single place that decision lives, and every caller should use it rather than re-deriving the predicate — otherwise the queries disagree with the permission resolver, which is the worst kind of bug to find.

    9. Open issues¶

    • The organizational calendar: which date the fee expires on, and how long the renewal window is (§6). The mechanism is settled; the two numbers are a guess.
    • Which offices are written without MEMBERS, and who decides that an office survives its holder's lapse. The gate left the resolver for the grant text (§6, D-100), so the decision is made one grant at a time and nothing records the why — only whether the word is in the expression. The standing report makes the outcome visible; it does not make the choice. Carried also at data-model/03 §9.
    • Whether an age rule has any home now. position.min_age is deleted with positions (D-100), a role carries none, and a person_group is refused one (D-098), so "a scoutmaster must be an adult" survives only as a report over role_holder and is_minor() (§7). If the organization wants that rule enforced rather than reported, it needs a decision this round did not make.
    • What status an imported closed membership carries (§3). The enum offers pending_payment, active, suspended, lapsed, terminated, completed, and none of them says a source stated this period and nobody in the system decided anything: terminated claims an end reason the export does not give, completed claims a lifecycle the model no longer has (§5 says no completed row is produced any more, while the enum and data-model/03 §3 both still carry the value and use it in an example). This is a naming question with a reporting consequence — a status that reads as a decision will be reported as one.
    • ~~Which roles are exempt from the membership gate~~ — the question became "which grants name ROLE(...) without MEMBERS", which is per grant rather than per role and is a report rather than a setting (D-100, and the bullet above). It stays on this list because the decision is still owed, only the shape of it has changed.

    Resolved and no longer open, kept visible so the reasoning survives: activation on payment or approval (on payment, §1); per person or per household (per person and flat, §6 — and the household grouping has since been removed entirely, D-057); renewal shape A or B (A, §6); age-out timing and the child→adult transition (neither exists, §7); background checks as a requirement (§2); and simultaneous memberships of different types (impossible — one type, one open membership, §3). Two from round 9:

    • Whether a body may refuse a minor, and who may add a person to one — answered twice over, and the same way both times. No unit carried restrictions of any kind and entry was somebody else's act: the fee at the membership, unit_participation.manage everywhere below it, no self-scoped variant of either (D-095, C-6, §1). Round 10 deleted the units and D-098 carried the ruling across intact: a group carries no restrictions, nobody writes themselves onto a roster, and the gate is person_group.member.manage. What that gives up is stated in §7 rather than here twice.
    • Whether the one-off import may create memberships — it may, for the periods the source states, both ends dated, never open-ended and never inferred from attendance (§3). This was answered no one round earlier and the answer reversed with the description of the corpus, not with the reasoning about what a membership means (D-092, D-093).
