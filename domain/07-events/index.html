    Events¶

    Camps, trips, hikes, and the occasional jamboree: things that happen on a date, that people register for, and that somebody has to run.

    An event is an item in the permission system: grant_scope_type carries a row for it, a grant may be scoped to one event and mean that event alone (D-006), and audiences exist partly to express "tell everyone about this event" (D-042). Nothing owns an event (D-102): authority reaches one from a grant at the unit or a grant on it and from nowhere else, and the people connected to it are named by selectors that bind to the event being checked.

    Data model: data-model/08-events.md and data-model/08a (the bound selectors, the three queries, the integrity checks).

    Section numbers are shared across the two parts and never renumbered, because other pages cite them by number and their anchors are built from them: 07-events.md holds §1–§7, 07a §8–§13 (capacity and the waitlist, presence, cancellation, payments, the forms, open issues).

    1. What an event is, and what it is not¶

    An event is a thing with a date that people sign up for.

    A weekly meeting is not an event. It is a group — a person_group whose dated member rows already say "this child takes part in this Thursday group" (D-098). A Thursday group that meets forty times a year is already recorded, and making each meeting an event would create forty rows saying what one row says, with forty capacities, forty organizer rosters and forty chances to be wrong. Events are the things you register for: camps, trips, hikes, the summer jamboree.

    The boundary has a cost, and it is worth knowing before asking the question. "Was Jan at the meeting on 14 March?" is not answerable from the events module. That is presence tracking for a standing group, which is a different feature; what is modelled here is presence at a dated occasion.

    There is no recurrence. No event_series, no RFC 5545 rule, no occurrence table: a group that camps four weekends a year has four events. Recurrence is what makes event tables complicated, and the one thing that looked as though it needed it — meetings — is already modelled as a group.

    2. Nothing owns an event¶

    An event is an item, registered in grant_scope_type under the code event, like a group or a role is. It has no owner. There is no column on event naming a person, a group, or a unit, and nothing in the resolver tests one (D-102).

    Two facts are the whole of how authority arrives:
    A grant at the unit reaches every event there is, because after D-097 there is no scope between the unit and an item, and no ownership relation to test
    A grant on one event reaches that event and nothing else

    A joint camp is an event. The second group's children reach it because a grant names them, exactly like every other reader, and nobody has to arbitrate "whose event is this?" — the question has no column to be asked of.

    Creating one needs event.create, whose applies_to is unit: anybody holding it creates an event, and nobody else does. Writing event.create on an event is refused when the grant is saved, with the reason in the message — an event cannot be created twice, and a creation permission attached to a thing that already exists answers no question (D-099).

    The anti-pattern, named once. An event does not belong to a unit, a group or a person: event.org_unit_id is not in the schema, can_event() is not in the resolver, and there is no unit-authority rule to reach across (appendix/removed-concepts.md). An event is one dated row, and the question "who may see this event?" has one home, which is the grant table.

    Writing an event on one event¶

    Because a unit-scope grant reaches everything, an item grant is for the narrow case, and the two are not interchangeable. This is where the model refuses things at the save rather than discovering them at the check:

    Written Result
    event.edit on one event Allowed. A capability on the item — D-006 unchanged
    event.create on one event Refused at save, with the reason: applies_to is unit
    membership.terminate on one event Refused at save, for the same reason — a membership is a fact about a person at the unit, which no event owns
    A grant naming PLACED at the unit Refused at save, naming the operand — there is no item for it to bind to (§7)

    3. Registration: one record, two stored answers, and no "unanswered"¶

    A person's relationship to an event is captured in one row — event_registration.

    What you asked for How it is stored
    attending response = 'attending'
    not attending response = 'not_attending'
    unanswered no row at all

    "Unanswered" is not a value, and that is deliberate. It is the absence of a registration. Writing a row that says "this person has not answered" means writing rows for people who have not acted, which then have to be cleaned up when the event ends and which cannot distinguish "asked and silent" from "never asked" without a third table.

    Returning someone to unanswered means removing the row (DELETE, recorded in audit_log) — which is the honest act, and the one that leaves the least behind.

    Who counts as unanswered? (the people a live grant reaches on this event) MINUS (people with a registration). The question "who hasn't replied yet?" is a subtraction, and it is a query — data-model/08a §9. The reachable side is a set of people named by grants, which is what makes the subtraction a query at all.

    The registration window¶

    Registration is open inside a period and closed outside it, and the period is two columns on the event — registration_opens_at and registration_closes_at.

    NULL on the close means until the event starts — the unlimited case. NULL on the open means the window opens as soon as the event is visible at all: appropriate for a hike, wrong for a jamboree whose sign-up should not open before the dates have been decided.

    4. Who may register: capability, not a separate rule engine¶

    Registering yourself requires one permission: event.register_self, held on that event — either by an allow on the event itself, or by an allow at the unit, which after D-097 reaches the event along with every other item. Nothing else.

    Why there is no eligibility rule: the capability already is one. A person holding event.register_self on an event may register for it, and there is no second question behind that — no age table, no prerequisite, no eligibility row, nothing evaluated outside can() (D-060).

    There is no level to compare. view, contribute, manage and admin as ranks name nothing: a grant allows or denies one code at one scope, and the answer to a check is allowed when some live allow applies and no live deny applies (D-099). Four questions, four codes, each answered by its own row of access_grant:

    Question Code
    May they see it? event.view
    May they register for it? event.register_self
    May they run its roster? event.manage_participants
    May they mark the register? event.presence.mark

    Two of those codes being held by one person is two rows, not a person who sits at some level — which is what makes "what exactly does this person have on this camp?" a question with an answer.

    This is why audiences matter here. Registering 200 children one at a time is 200 grant rows — but opening the event to everybody who currently holds a membership is one: a grant whose scope is that event, whose grantee is an audience whose expression is MEMBERS, and whose effect is allow. That row is what D-042 was built for, and it is the normal way to run an open event. Per-person grants are for the exceptions.

    An age rule is an audience, not a rule¶

    The obvious objection is a camp for 11–14 year-olds: how does a grant express "only 11-to-14s"? A named set. The organization keeps a group of the older section and opens the camp to GROUP(<that group>) (D-098). An age-gated camp is a camp opened to a set whose members are that age.

    There is a second way to write that sentence, and it is the same kind of thing. OLDER(n) and YOUNGER(n) are seeded operands of the registry (D-107): shipped rows of selector_type, alongside MEMBERS, whose predicate the resolver owns, read against person.birth_date on the date being checked and composed by + - * like every other operand. So the camp for 11-to-14s is also writable as (MEMBERS * OLDER(11)) - YOUNGER(15) — one grant, one expression, no roster to keep current. The group is still the better answer where the section is a real group with a real roster behind it, because a maintained group says who is in it and an age expression only says who qualifies; the two are not rivals and an organization will use both.

    An age ceiling is writable, and it is not written. event.register_self on this event, aimed at MEMBERS * YOUNGER(15), is an ordinary grant that stops a 9-year-old registering for a camp for teenagers. Write no such grant and nothing in the system objects, because nothing in it could: an event has no age ceiling of its own. D-070 is therefore closed as a choice, not as a limit — the organization keeps the judgement in the roster a leader reads and in the attributed registration that records who let a child through. Read it as a decision every time the next restriction is requested.

    There are no min_age/max_age columns on event, and there will not be some.

    Why: a condition evaluated at registration time that the capability model does not know about is the first rule-engine rule, and the second is always cheaper than the first. Two years after that, the answer to "who can register for this?" stops being readable from access_grant. A ceiling written in a grant is readable there; a ceiling written on the event row is exactly the thing that is not.

    What makes the residual risk survivable is three properties the schema already has. The registration is attributable — source = 'staff' with a named created_by, so the judgement belongs to somebody. Age is one join away — person.birth_date, which the roster query in data-model/08a §9 already selects, so any list a leader works from can carry it without a new column anywhere. And age is one operand away, which is what makes the ceiling above a writing choice rather than a feature request. The ordinary case — the age-bounded camp — is handled twice over: open the event to the set whose members are that age, by a group somebody maintains or by the age operands themselves.

    A writer may not type AGE >= 13. The clause to quote is that an operand must be a named operand — a row of the registry — and this language has no comparison operator for AGE >= 13 to be assembled out of (D-107). OLDER(13) is a set of people: the ones who are thirteen or older on the date being checked, in the same way MEMBERS is the set of people who pass is_current_member and ROLE(x) is the set who hold that office. Every operand in the registry is a test with a set behind it (06a §6.2).

    Guardianship is deliberately not a selector, in either spelling (GUARDIANS and GUARDIANS(x)) (D-113): "True, GUARDIANS should not be selectable in the audience, guardians can perform ANY action (their read/write perms grant) on behalf of the child." A check about a child asks whether a live guardianship row links the two people and reads right_level ∈ none | view | write off it — a lookup over a relationship, not a set the language resolves — so an aimed-at-guardians operand would hand those people, one code at a time, a subset of what the channel already gives them.

    The registry is organization-read-only, so the next predicate anyone wants arrives as a deployment — a shipped row with its evaluator arm beside it — and not as a setting. Either way every answer to "who may register" keeps living in one table, which is the property worth defending.

    5. Organizers are appointed, not granted¶

    An event has a set of organizers — the people running it. That list is event_organizer, and it is the domain answer to "who is running this?", labelled, ordered, attributed.

    An event_organizer row grants nothing. access_grant remains what it is good at — showing a treasurer the books, opening the page to an audience, naming a parent who may register their child — and organizers are a separate concept: who is running this (D-062). An operand is a set, not a grant, so naming ORGANIZERS in an expression says who is in the set and confers nothing, in exactly the way that naming ROLE(leaders) or any audience has never conferred anything (D-005, D-042).

    The consequence worth understanding: an organizer is an appointment on the thing being run. It can carry a label ('lead', 'cook', 'driver') and an order, and it is not a permission bundle — it does not carry event.edit, it says who the organizers are. Like a role, an organizer is a person named against something, and the codes that follow from it are the live grants that aim at them (D-100).

    Why the two do not drift. The tempting design writes an access_grant whenever an organizer is added, so "organizers" is just a query over grants. It fails on the label (a grant has no room for "cook"), on the ordering, and on the reverse question — "who is running this camp?" should not be answered by reading a permission table. Two concepts, each doing its own job.

    What an organizer gets. Membership of event_organizer makes a person a member of the set ORGANIZERS. What that set may do is whatever a live grant aims at it, and the act that creates the event writes twelve of those grants in the same transaction as the event row — one item-scope allow per code whose applies_to admits an event, every one aimed at the bare operand ORGANIZERS, the set derived rather than hand-picked (D-151; §12). One of the twelve is event.grant_access: an organizer hands access to the event they run without holding access_grant.create at the unit, bounded by the scope they can name and by applies_to. Everything outside those twelve reaches an organizer the way it reaches anybody else — through a grant that person holds.

    6. How authority reaches an event¶

    Through a grant, and through nothing else. A grant at the unit reaches every event; a grant on one event reaches that event (§2). The resolver matches scope exactly, so a check for event.view at ('event', 42) looks for a grant at that exact scope, and a person whose job is running the camp succeeds there only because their grant sits at the unit — the widest scope there is, with nothing between it and an item (D-097).

    Nothing inherits, and nothing is bridged. There is no org_unit scope to hold a role at, no owner column for a check to read, and no wrapper adding a second can() call for events: can_event() is not in the resolver, and the artifact-and-unit distinction it existed to draw has no pair left to distinguish (D-102). The check is plain can(). What an organizer, an ORGANIZERS grant, and a unit-scope grant each mean is in §7 and in the table below.

    Nothing in the model reproduces "access was granted by the body itself". With one unit there is no body that chooses which named adult may reach its children, and no scope between the unit and the item for such a choice to live at (D-097; the same loss, stated for a person's whole record, is at 06 §2).

    Organizers still matter, and here is how¶

    Membership of event_organizer is what makes someone an organizer in the domain sense: the person on the list, with a label, whose name the parents see. Authority for acting on the event comes from grants, as it does everywhere else:

    Who How they may manage it
    The person who runs the organization's camps event.manage_participants at the unit — the grant reaches every event
    Somebody trusted with one camp the same code on that event, as an item grant
    An organizer, as such only what a grant aims at ORGANIZERS — the twelve the event's creation wrote, answer-view among them
    Somebody the organization has excluded a deny on the event or at the unit; deny is absolute and beats every allow (D-013)
    system.admin Everything — the sole exception, as everywhere

    The policy is stated positively and, where it must be, negatively. Positively: a person holds what the rows name, and there is no wider default to be exposed by and no narrow one to be trapped by. Negatively: where holding something must be impossible, a deny says so, and deny is absolute (D-013). A genuinely private event is therefore an act — an item grant, plus a deny at the unit when the audience is broad (§10).

    7. The people connected to an event¶

    Who is connected to this event? There is no question about which body may read a roster: with one unit and no exposure between bodies, the question has no subject (§6).

    Five sets answer it, and each is a selector that binds to the event being checked — written with no argument when the grant is written on an event:

    Operand On the event being checked means Explicit form
    ATTENDED said yes to this event ATTENDED(<event>)
    PLACED said yes and is not waitlisted — the capacity set PLACED(<event>)
    CONFIRMED holds a confirmed place CONFIRMED(<event>)
    ON_WAITLIST is waiting for a place ON_WAITLIST(<event>)
    ORGANIZERS runs this event ORGANIZERS(<event>)

    The four attendance sets are not four ideas; each is a definition over two columns of event_registration, and the definitions are stated with the columns in data-model/08a §8. They are ordered by containment rather than by rank — CONFIRMED ⊆ PLACED, PLACED and ON_WAITLIST partition ATTENDED, and a person who declined is in none of them — and no level or ladder is involved in any of them (D-099).

    The bound form is the important half, and it kills a specific flaw. ATTENDED(<event 1204>) written on event 1204 is one grant per event with the event's own id typed into it, so "every participant may see the other participants" is a thing you do 400 times and forget on the 401st. Bound, it is one expression written the same way every time. That is the whole reason the argument-less form exists, and the two rules that make it safe are write-path rules, stated as requirements in data-model/08a §8.

    PRESENT(<event>) is refused, and this is where presence tempts a reader. The obvious sixth operand is "the ones who actually turned up", computed over was_present (§9). It is refused, and the reason is the failure direction: was_present is NULL until somebody takes a register, so an operand built on it would revoke a child's access to their own camp photos because a volunteer forgot to tick a box. was_present is three-valued on purpose as a safeguarding signal — keeping it out of the selector language is what keeps it meaning "a person recorded this" rather than "this is who may see things", and D-067's reasoning for the third value is the reasoning for the refusal. And this consequence is the whole of the refusal, which is worth saying out loud: OLDER(n) reads person.birth_date (D-107), so nothing structural stops anybody writing PRESENT — there is only ever going to be this argument standing in the way, and it holds on its own (06a).

    WHITELISTED does not exist, and is not a selector. The nearest stored facts are source = 'staff' (an organizer entered them) and promotion off the waitlist. Either the word is to mean one of those or it names a new column on event_registration, which is an events decision and not a selector decision — and the events decision is now made: refused (D-148). Nothing here reserves the name, and the refusal is written where the presence tiers are listed (07a §9) rather than being implied by a word in a grammar.

    The screen must say which event it is talking about¶

    The audience preview is a requirement rather than a nicety: "the person saving the grant is looking at one row that mentions no person at all", so the editor must show the expanded people a grant reaches (D-091).

    The preview is computed for an item, and the screen says so. PLACED means different people on different events, so "who does this grant reach?" is not answerable from the grant row alone — for a grant whose expression contains a bound operand, it is answerable only as "on event 1204, these 84 people", and the screen must name the event it computed against rather than presenting the list as a property of the grant. The consequences are requirements on the editor and on the read path, not on the schema:

    • An expression containing a bound operand is previewed per item. Asking for a preview of such a grant at the unit is not a large computation, it is a refused one — the same refusal the save applies. The per-item preview carries no cap, no cache and no background job, on stated grounds: the system is specified against a few hundred people in the organization, a few dozen registrants on a typical event and a big camp on the order of two hundred, so previewing one item evaluates a couple of hundred predicates and nobody feels it (D-112). That size is stated, not measured — nothing in these documents has measured anything here — and the trigger for reopening it is a few thousand people, or a thousand registrants on one event. Nothing pre-pays this work: the preview computes it on open, still affordable, now with no fallback behind it (D-114).
    • Where no item is in hand, the editor says what the expression means, not who it reaches.
    • A compiled expression holds no item, precisely because the item arrives as a parameter, so the item is bound at resolution: nothing expanded or resolved while checking one event may be served for another, and any cache of a resolution rather than of a compilation is keyed by the item or it is wrong. The reason is the one behind the bound form — the same grant on two events has two answers (D-102, 06 §8).
    • A registration write is not a ruleset write, and that is a statement about compilations, not about answers: ruleset_version is bumped by a write to audience_operand or selector_type alone, and a compilation holds no person and no item, so being outside that set buys a check nothing. Marking a register, confirming a place, flipping is_confirmed or promoting somebody off the waitlist moves ATTENDED, PLACED, CONFIRMED and ON_WAITLIST, and the new state is visible on the next check, because resolution is live and there is no answer stored anywhere to be behind the roster; ORGANIZERS, the fifth bound operand, moves on its own rows with the same immediacy (D-114). And the D-081 answer to "who enforces this": nobody enforces "a registration change must reach an answer", because there is nothing left to invalidate (D-081) — the read path does it, and a read path cannot be forgotten.

    Fail closed as everywhere else in the model, with the direction stated rather than implied: an operand that resolves to nothing contributes the empty set, which narrows what a + or a * reaches and widens what a - takes away — EVERYONE - PERSON(x) with a dead x denies everybody, lockout and fail-closed being the same event (D-106). A person with no birth date is not that kind of failure: the operand resolves perfectly well and they are simply not in OLDER(18), exactly as a person with no membership row is not in MEMBERS. A broken leaf is not silent and still stops nothing — it is listed on the tab on the permissions page, and no save is refused for it (D-109). And that tab is nearly empty by construction, which is the point of the ruling: nothing a rule names is ever deleted. A retired office, group or audience is flagged deprecated, hidden or disabled and keeps resolving — only disabled changes the answer, and it changes it to the empty set because somebody asked for exactly that. The one thing still able to break a leaf is a person, because D-088 lets a redaction require the row to actually go and erasure wins: a redacted person is the corpus's only remaining way to produce a broken leaf.

    A preview is a statement about the day it was taken. OLDER(n) and YOUNGER(n) make that true of every age-bearing expression in the sharpest form there is: the 84 names on the screen are the people who qualify on that date, and a birthday moves the list with no write and no edit to the grant. Nothing is told about it and nothing needs to be: there is nothing to be stale, because every check resolves against live rows and a birthday moves the answer at the instant it happens (D-114). What the preview is a statement about is the date it was taken, and that is a fact about the calendar, not a lag to be closed.

    The preview is a workbench, not a record — and the record is the expression (D-110). The audit row written when a grant is saved says this person wrote these words, about this item, on this date. What the preview printed alongside it is logged as a rendering on a date, never as a statement of who was in it: a stored list of everyone a rule happened to cover, kept after the rule was withdrawn, is the roster table this corpus has deleted twice. So "whom did this grant reach in May?" is answered, if at all, by evaluating the words against the data of May — and these documents do not promise the answer. Words-only is affordable rather than brave only because of D-109: since nothing a rule names is deleted, the words keep resolving long after their reason is gone. What that concedes, so nobody finds it later: a shared audience holds a word, not a copy, so an edit to a shared audience is a retrospective edit to every rule that names it, and a re-evaluation for May uses today's inner expression. The control is the preview this very section is about — the editor prints the resolved set before an edit to a shared expression saves, so the person editing sees at least once what they are changing backwards in time. And a preview that cannot be computed still shows that it could not be computed, rather than an empty list.
