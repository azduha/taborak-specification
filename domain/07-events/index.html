    Events¶

    Camps, trips, hikes, and the occasional district jamboree: things that happen on a date, that people register for, and that somebody has to run.

    This module was the one the whole authorization model was built toward. access_grant shipped with scope_type = 'event' from the first draft (D-006), grant_scope_type has carried a placeholder row for it since, and audiences (D-042) exist partly to express "tell everyone about this event." Those hooks are now filled in.

    Domain model: data-model/08-events.md.

    1. What an event is, and what it is not¶

    An event is a thing with a date that people sign up for.

    A weekly meeting is not an event. It is unit_participation. A Thursday group that meets forty times a year is already recorded — "this child takes part in this Thursday group" — and making each meeting an event would create forty rows saying what one row says, with forty capacities, forty organizer rosters and forty chances to be wrong. Events are the things you register for: camps, trips, hikes, the summer jamboree.

    This is a genuine boundary and worth stating plainly, because the two are easy to confuse and the confusion has a cost. If someone wants to know "was Jan at the meeting on 14 March?", the answer is not in the events module. That is presence tracking for a standing group, which is a different feature; what is modelled here is presence at a dated occasion.

    The consequence: there is no recurrence. No event_series, no RFC 5545 rule, no occurrence table. A pack that camps four weekends a year has four events. This was considered and rejected in favour of the boundary above — recurrence is what makes event tables complicated, and the thing that needed it (meetings) is already modelled elsewhere.

    2. An event belongs to one unit¶

    event.org_unit_id is NOT NULL. Every event has exactly one owning unit, and for anything a pack runs, that is the pack.

    A joint camp between two packs is Pack A's event that Pack B's children register for. Pack B is a guest, and its access is written explicitly — an audience grant, or a named person, at the level that fits. Nobody has to arbitrate "whose event is this?", and one unit remains the author of the record.

    This is forced by D-049, not merely preferred by it. If an event could belong to two units, then the root's inability to read a sub-unit's events would have an exception shaped exactly like a joint camp, and there is no principled place to stop. One owner keeps the rule total.

    The root may not read a pack's events. This is the rule the whole no-inheritance design exists to produce, and events are where it is most visible: an organization-scoped registrar cannot see a camp's roster. That is not a bug to be reported — see §7.

    3. Registration: one record, two stored answers, and no "unanswered"¶

    A person's relationship to an event is captured in one row — event_registration.

    What you asked for How it is stored
    attending response = 'attending'
    not attending response = 'not_attending'
    unanswered no row at all

    "Unanswered" is not a value, and that is deliberate. It is the absence of a registration. Writing a row that says "this person has not answered" means writing rows for people who have not acted, which then have to be cleaned up when the event ends and which cannot distinguish "asked and silent" from "never asked" without a third table.

    Returning someone to unanswered means removing the row (DELETE, recorded in audit_log) — which is the honest act, and the one that leaves the least behind.

    Who counts as unanswered? (people who could contribute to this event) MINUS (people with a registration). The question "who hasn't replied yet?" is a subtraction, and it is a query — data-model/08 §9.

    The registration window¶

    Registration is open inside a period and closed outside it, and the period is two columns on the event — registration_opens_at and registration_closes_at.

    NULL on the close means until the event starts, which is the "unlimited" case you described. NULL on the open means the window opens as soon as the event is visible at all — appropriate for a hike, wrong for a jamboree whose sign-up should not open before the committee has decided the dates.

    4. Who may register: capability, not a separate rule engine¶

    Registration requires contribute capability on that event. Nothing else.

    This is the answer to the question D-006 left open. D-006 said "eligibility ... will be a separate concept when events are specified." Your answer was that people with contribute on the item should be able to register — and that turns out to dissolve the deferral rather than satisfy it: there is no separate eligibility concept, because the capability ladder already is one.

    view        may SEE the event           ─┐
contribute  may REGISTER for it          │  one grant on the event
manage      may ORGANIZE it              │  decides all three
admin       may control it               ─┘

    The rows are already in permission_level (data-model/05 §7):

    permission scope_type required_level
    event.view event view
    event.register_self event contribute
    event.manage_participants event manage
    event.presence.mark event contribute
    event.grant_access event admin

    This is why audiences matter here. Registering 200 children one at a time is 200 grant rows — but opening the event to members is one: a grant whose scope is that event, whose grantee is the all_members audience rather than a person, and whose level is contribute.

    That single row is what D-042 was built for, and it is the normal way to run an open event. Per-person grants are for the exceptions.

    An age rule is an audience, not a rule¶

    The obvious objection is a camp for 11–14 year-olds. How does contribute express "only 11-to-14s"? The built-in audiences. unit:<id>:participants is a rule the resolver evaluates, so an event for the older section is opened to that section's participants and nothing else. An age-gated camp is a camp open to a unit whose participants are that age.

    What this does not do, deliberately: it does not enforce an age ceiling on a registration for an open event. If a 9-year-old holds contribute on a camp for teenagers, they can register, and nothing in the system objects (D-070). There are no min_age/max_age columns on event and there will not be some, for a reason worth keeping in mind when the next restriction is requested: a condition evaluated at registration time that the capability model does not know about is the first rule-engine rule, and the second is always cheaper than the first. Two years after that, the answer to "who can register for this?" stops being readable from access_grant.

    What makes the residual risk survivable is not a check but three properties the schema already has. The registration is attributable — source = 'staff' with a named created_by, so the judgement belongs to somebody. Age is one join away — person.birth_date, which the roster query in data-model/08 §9 already selects, so any list a leader works from can carry it without a new column anywhere. And the age-bounded case, the ordinary one, is already handled: open the event to the unit whose members are that age.

    If the shape ever recurs often enough to hurt, the fix is not a column on event. It is another built-in audience — all_members is a rule the resolver evaluates, so members_aged_13_plus can be one too. That keeps every answer to "who may register" living in one table, which is the property worth defending.

    5. Organizers are appointed, not granted¶

    An event has a set of organizers — the people running it. That list is event_organizer, and it is the authority, checked directly.

    Organizers are not access_grants at manage level. This was the explicit choice. access_grant remains what it is good at — showing a treasurer the books, opening the page to an audience, naming a parent who may register their child — and organizers are a separate concept: who is running this.

    The consequence worth understanding: an organizer is an appointment on the thing being run, which makes event_organizer the event-scoped analogue of position_assignment (data-model/02 §7). Like a position, it can carry a label ('lead', 'cook', 'driver') and an order. Like a position, it is not a permission bundle — it does not grant event.edit; it says who the organizers are, and the event permissions say what organizers may do.

    Why the two do not drift. The tempting design writes an access_grant whenever an organizer is added, so "organizers" is just a query over grants. It fails on the label (a grant has no room for "cook"), on the ordering, and on the reverse question — "who is running this camp?" should not be answered by reading a permission table. Two concepts, each doing its own job.

    What an organizer gets. Membership of event_organizer is one input to the resolver, not a bypass of it. See §6, which is the part of this module that needed a real decision.

    6. The hinge: organizers and the scope mismatch¶

    This is the structural problem the module had to solve, and it is worth stating in full because it is easy to "fix" wrongly later.

    The resolver matches scope exactly (D-031, data-model/05 §10). A check for event.view at ('event', 42) looks for a grant or role at that exact scope. A unit_leader role is at ('org_unit', 7).

    unit_leader  ── holds role at ──►  org_unit 7  (the pack)
                                       │
                                       │  ✗ NO PATH
                                       ▼
event 42 "Summer camp"  ◄── owned by ──┘     resolver checks scope exactly

    So the pack's leader — a person whose job is running the pack's camp — does not pass can(leader, 'event.view', 'event', 42). Nothing is broken; the answer is simply no.

    The three ways out, and why two are rejected:

    Option Why not
    Give the leader an access_grant at manage on every event Correct but fragile: a grant per event per leader, and it must be written by someone who thought of it
    Let authority inherit from the unit down to the unit's events This is D-031/D-015, and it is reversed. Events are precisely the "own records of a sub-unit" the whole rule protects
    Let the event's own owning unit supply authority Chosen

    The chosen rule, stated precisely:

    A person satisfies a permission checked at ('event', E) if the permission would be satisfied at the event's owning unit — ('org_unit', E.org_unit_id) — and the event is owned by that same unit.

    This is not inheritance downward through the hierarchy. It is not inheritance at all. It is the recognition that an event is not a child of its unit; it is an artifact of it. The unit's authority already covers the unit; the event is a thing the unit made, and asking "may this person do this in Pack 7?" is asking the right question about the right scope.

    The distinction that makes this safe:

    Inheritance (rejected) Artifact rule (chosen)
    What it would let a leader do Act in the pack's sub-units too Act on the pack's own events
    Blast radius Grows when the hierarchy is restructured Fixed — the event's owner is one unit
    Reverses D-015, D-031 Nothing

    Restructuring the hierarchy still changes nothing about who may do what. Adding a sub-unit still grants nobody anything. The rule is exactly as narrow as it needs to be, and no narrower.

    And it is a two-way door. If the organization later decides that a leader should not automatically run every event their unit creates — that events should be opt-in even for leaders — the rule is one clause in the resolver, and turning it off makes organizers access_grant-based without touching a single row of data.

    Organizers still matter, and here is how¶

    Membership of event_organizer is what makes someone an organizer in the domain sense: the person on the list, with a label, whose name the parents see. Authority for acting on the event comes from the owning unit's roles, from an explicit grant, or from system.admin — normal, resolvable authority.

    Concretely, for the pack's camp:

    Who How they may manage it
    Pack leader unit_leader role at the pack → §6 rule → manage
    Assistant leader assistant_leader role at the pack → same
    A parent who volunteered to cook An explicit access_grant at manage, or a role — because no role of theirs reaches the pack's events
    The root's treasurer Nothing. Not without the pack exposing its scope (D-049)
    system_admin Everything — the sole exception, as everywhere

    That second-to-last row is the whole no-inheritance policy in one line, and it is correct.

    7. What the root can and cannot see¶

    Following directly from D-049:

    Question Answer Why
    "How many members do we have?" Yes The root owns the membership registry
    "What fees are outstanding?" Yes The root owns the fee data
    "What camps did the packs run in 2019?" Only if each pack exposed its scope Events belong to the sub-unit
    "Who is coming to Pack B's camp?" No Same

    A registrar holding event.view at organization scope cannot read a pack's camp. It reads the organization's own events — which is to say, almost none. Scope is an exact match. This will look like a bug and is not one; the fix, if the organization genuinely wants it, is the pack writing one scope_exposure row naming one assignment, which is a deliberate, dated, revocable act. Forty of those is forty deliberate acts, and that is the auditability, not the drudgery.

    8. Capacity and the waitlist¶

    An event may have a capacity — beds, bus seats, a fire-safety limit. When it fills:

    Registrations past capacity are accepted as waitlisted, not refused. A person who registers for a full camp appears on the list, ordered, marked as waiting, and an organizer promotes them when a place frees up.

    This is a real feature rather than a flag, and it is the honest model of how a camp with twelve beds actually runs: the thirteenth caller is not turned away, they are written down. See data-model/08 §5 for the ordering and promotion mechanics.

    And organizers may exceed capacity outright — deliberately, because a leader who knows there are two spare tents should not be blocked by a number they cannot edit mid-camp. The overrun is permitted, attributed, and reported (data-model/08 §10): an event running over its stated capacity is a fact the organization can see, not a state it cannot reach.

    So capacity is three things at once, and all three are intentional:

    Behaviour
    Self-registration when full Waitlisted — never refused, never admitted
    Organizer registration when full Admitted, the overrun reported
    Any capacity change An UPDATE, visible and attributed

    9. Presence: what actually happened¶

    The registration says what someone said. Presence says what happened.

    event_registration.was_present is a nullable boolean: NULL = not yet marked, true = turned up, false = marked absent. The three-valued column is not sloppiness — it distinguishes "we did not take a register" from "we took a register and they were not there", which are different facts and only one of them is a safeguarding signal.

    Why this matters more than it looks. For a camp, the end-of-week roster is the record of who was actually in the organization's care. That is the row that matters if the fee buys insurance (finance question 9: "member on the date of the incident"), and it is the row that cannot be reconstructed afterwards from registrations. It is also why the column is marked by an organizer and recorded with who marked it and when.

    Presence is not registration. A child who registered and did not come is a registration with was_present = false. A child who turned up without registering — a sibling along for the day — is not representable, deliberately: that child was not in the organization's care and must not silently appear to have been. Recording them means registering them, which means they have a guardian and the organizers have a name.

    10. Cancellation, and why there is no status column¶

    There is no lifecycle status. An event is a row with dates, a unit, and a set of grants.

    Visibility is the permission model. A newly created event has no grants beyond its organizers' own authority, so nobody else can see it. Publishing is not a state change — it is granting someone access: an audience, a group, a unit. This is your answer, and it is better than a status enum for the usual reason: a status column and a permission system that both claim to say "who may see this" will eventually disagree, and the one that gets enforced is whoever wrote the last IF.

    So the questions that a draft status would answer are answered by grants:

    Question Answer
    "Is this ready to be seen?" Has anyone been granted view?
    "Who can see it before it is announced?" Its organizers, and anyone already granted
    "How do I hide it again?" Revoke the grant — revoked_at set, row kept

    Cancellation is a fact, not a status — and this is where the logic diverges, because the audience is different:

    Three columns carry it: cancelled_at says when, cancelled_by says who — a person, so the decision has a name attached to it — and cancel_reason says why.

    Cancelling by revoking grants would hide the cancellation from exactly the people who need it. The registrants are the ones who must be told the camp is off; if the event is hidden by revoking their view, they see nothing at all — not "cancelled", just gone. So cancellation is recorded, and the event stays visible, marked.

    A cancelled event stops accepting registrations (the registration window closes implicitly) and keeps every existing row: those registrations are the record that the camp was planned and who was coming. That record is exactly what is needed afterwards — to notify people, and to refund them.

    completed is derived, never set. cancelled_at IS NULL AND ends_at < now(). There is no state to keep consistent, and no job to move events between states.

    One more thing, which is structural: there is no event.is_published and no cancelled status, but there is a cancelled_at. An event that is cancelled and an event that is merely past are distinguished by one NULL — and the "what needs attention?" query is WHERE cancelled_at IS NULL AND ends_at >= now(), ordering by start. That is the whole lifecycle.

    11. Payments on events¶

    An event may cost money, and payment confirms the place — but payment is never mandatory, because plenty of events are free.

    The rule, stated once:
    An event with a fee Registering creates an obligation; the place is confirmed on payment
    An event without a fee Registering confirms the place immediately
    An unpaid registration at capacity Holds a slot until the deadline, then releases to the waitlist
    Money The finance module's problem — events record that a fee applies and what it is

    The only new schema here is a pointer: fee_obligation.event_registration_id. It is one arm of fee_obligation's target CHECK, restored after D-057 removed the household arm, and this time it is a real arm with data behind it rather than a dormant hedge.

    The is_confirmed / deadline interaction is the one automatic transition in this module, and it is worth flagging as such: the design has spent effort avoiding automatic state changes (D-023, D-040), and payment-confirms-the-place reintroduces one. It is a deadline, not a sweep: it applies only to registrations at a full event with an unpaid fee, and only after the deadline the organizer set. data-model/08 §6 records it as an accepted cost with the reasoning, and open-questions carries it.

    12. The forms: questions and answers¶

    An event may ask registrants for details it needs — tent preference, dietary preference, T- shirt size, whether they are coming by bus.

    Questions are defined per event, and answers are rows.

    event_question          what this event asks
   │
   └── event_answer     what a person said    ──  one row per question per person

    Answers as rows rather than JSONB is the choice that makes the feature work: "who needs a gluten-free meal, and how many?" is a GROUP BY, not a JSON traversal. The cost is a row per answer, which is correct — the number of answers is the number of things the organization must actually act on.

    Questions may apply to attendees, organizers, or both, and may be required or optional. Required means the person registering must answer; it is not a global gate on registration, because a form that blocks sign-up is a form nobody completes.

    Reusable question libraries were considered and rejected. A shared pool of question definitions is structurally the requirement machinery removed in D-044, and it earns its keep only when the same question must mean the same thing across events. Tent preference does not: what one camp asks and what another needs are allowed to differ, and per-event definitions keep them from having to agree.

    Health data, and the widening this represents¶

    You confirmed that event answers are readable by that event's organizers, with no extra gate. Two consequences follow, and the second is the significant one.

    1. Only preference-level detail belongs on a form. A dietary preference ("vegetarian", "no pork") is an operational note for the cook. A condition ("coeliac", "nut allergy") is medical data, and nothing in the schema distinguishes them — the difference is what the organizer types into a label. So the discipline is in the form design, not the constraint: data-model/08 §7 states it, and it is guidance, not enforcement.

    2. This is the route by which a leader learns a child has a nut allergy, and that is a deliberate widening. A guardian's reach over a child is one value on the guardianship row — none, view or write — read by a channel of its own, entered only when the act is about someone other than the actor and never answered by a role or a grant (D-074, D-079; appendix/permission-catalog.md §9). The namespace that used to spell this out is deleted, the safeguarding property it carried is not:

    "A unit leader does not get these by virtue of leading the unit — that is a safeguarding property."

    Corrected in round 7, because this sentence was doing work it could no longer do. It used to read "so a camp leader cannot reach a child's medical record at all" — and after D-083 there is no medical record to be unreachable, so the sentence is not merely out of date, it describes a guarantee the model never had once the category disappeared. What is actually true, in the two forms that matter:

    • No role gets it by leading. The starting role set gives a unit_leader their unit's roster, positions and events — and no read over a person's data (permission-catalog §11). That is the safeguarding property, exactly as before: leadership is not guardianship, and the guardianship channel answers only for the person its row names.
    • It is now one grant away, deliberately. Whoever holds person.view over a child's unit reads the whole record, allergy list included. Nothing in the resolver will stop that and nothing should — but a document that promised it was impossible would have been trusted, and a promise the model does not make is worse than none.

    **The widening in the next paragraph is therefore unchanged in substance and better stated: the protection is a decision about a role, revisitable by whoever administers roles, not a wall."

    Which means: under the starting roles, an allergy recorded on a camp form is the only way the people running the camp will know. That is a coherent and defensible configuration — the parent tells the people present, per event, and nobody outside the family holds the fact — but it is a widening from "few people, via a guardianship-scoped permission" to "every organizer of any event the child registers for", and it is recorded as such in D-061, with the reasoning, so that nobody later "fixes" the apparent duplication between the two stores by tightening this one.

    Event answers are not the person's record. They are operational detail for one event, read by that event's organizers, and they end with the event — while the same fact, recorded on the person, is audited, curable and erased with them. D-083 made those two copies the same class of data, which is what turns the retention question below into a real one, and AT-1 into the alternative: collect the fact once, confirm it at the door. See §11 for the retention question, which is open.

    13. Open issues¶

    Moved to ../open-questions.md under G. Events, where they are tracked with the rest. The ones this module raises:

    • ~~The age ceiling on an open event~~ — settled: accepted as a risk, the grant is the rule, and closing it later would mean a new audience rather than a column (§4, D-070).
    • Event answer retention (§12). Answers end with the event — but a dietary answer naming a condition is data of exactly the same class as what the person record holds, held under weaker rules and fresher. No policy yet; see EV-2.
    • Cancelled events and fees (§10–11). Cancelling keeps the registrations; whether it releases the obligations, refunds them, or neither is the finance module's call.
    • Presence for minors and the safeguarding record (§9). was_present is the "who was in our care" record. Whether it needs its own retention or its own permission is unresolved.
    • Whether organizers should be an access_grant after all (§5–6). The rule in §6 makes the unit's roles reach its events; if the organization would rather organizers be opt-in per event, that is one clause to change.
