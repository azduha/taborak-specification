    Events: capacity, presence, payments and the forms¶

    What happens between a filled capacity and a completed event: the waitlist, attendance, cancellation, the money, and the questions an organizer asks.

    Section numbers are shared across the two parts and never renumbered, because other pages cite them by number and their anchors are built from them: 07-events.md holds §1–§7, 07a §8–§13 (capacity and the waitlist, presence, cancellation, payments, the forms, open issues).

    8. Capacity and the waitlist¶

    An event may have a capacity — beds, bus seats, a fire-safety limit. When it fills:

    Registrations past capacity are accepted as waitlisted, not refused. A person who registers for a full camp appears on the list, ordered, marked as waiting, and an organizer promotes them when a place frees up.

    This is a real feature rather than a flag, and it is the honest model of how a camp with twelve beds actually runs: the thirteenth caller is not turned away, they are written down. See data-model/08 §5 for the ordering and promotion mechanics.

    And organizers may exceed capacity outright — deliberately, because a leader who knows there are two spare tents should not be blocked by a number they cannot edit mid-camp (D-065). The overrun is permitted, attributed, and reported (data-model/08a §10): an event running over its stated capacity is a fact the organization can see, not a state it cannot reach.

    The way past the number is not the resolver: the waitlist fires only for source = 'self', and a registration written by a person holding event.register_other arrives as source = 'staff' and is admitted. event.register_other is a grant at the unit or on the event, like every other code (D-102).

    So capacity is three things at once, and all three are intentional:

    Behaviour
    Self-registration when full Waitlisted — never refused, never admitted
    A staff registration when full Admitted, the overrun reported
    Any capacity change An UPDATE, visible and attributed

    The set capacity is compared against has a name: it is PLACED, the attending non-waitlisted registrations of an event (§7) — the same set PLACED selects and the same index the capacity test already uses.

    9. Presence: what actually happened¶

    The registration says what someone said. Presence says what happened.

    event_registration.was_present is a nullable boolean: NULL = not yet marked, true = turned up, false = marked absent. The three-valued column is not sloppiness — it distinguishes "we did not take a register" from "we took a register and they were not there", which are different facts and only one of them is a safeguarding signal (D-067).

    Why this matters more than it looks. For a camp, the end-of-week roster is the record of who was actually in the organization's care. That is the row that matters if the fee buys insurance (finance question 9: "member on the date of the incident"), and it is the row that cannot be reconstructed afterwards from registrations. It is also why the column is marked by an organizer and recorded with who marked it and when.

    Presence is not registration. A child who registered and did not come is a registration with was_present = false. A child who turned up without registering — a sibling along for the day — is not representable, deliberately: that child was not in the organization's care and must not silently appear to have been. Recording them means registering them, which means they have a guardian and the organizers have a name.

    And presence is not an audience. The temptation to build PRESENT(<event>) on this column — to let "everyone who came" be a set a grant names — is refused, and §7 states why: the column is NULL until somebody takes a register, so an operand over it makes access depend on a volunteer's diligence, and it would turn a safeguarding record into an access control. Attendance for the purposes of a grant stops at ATTENDED, PLACED, CONFIRMED and ON_WAITLIST.

    10. Cancellation, and why there is no status column¶

    There is no lifecycle status. An event is a row with dates and a set of grants.

    Visibility is the permission model. A newly created event has no grants of its own, so whoever can see it can see it because of a grant they already hold — normally an event.view at the unit, which reaches every event and therefore every new one the moment it is created. Publishing is not a state change — it is granting someone access: an audience, a group, a role.

    Why not a status enum: a status column and a permission system that both claim to say "who may see this" will eventually disagree, and the one that gets enforced is whoever wrote the last IF (D-064).

    There is no narrow default, and that is the shape of the model. After D-097 the unit is the only scope there is, so a person holding event.view at the unit sees a half-typed event the instant it is committed, and there is no narrower scope a grant could have sat at instead. A genuinely private event is therefore an act — an item grant, plus a deny at the unit if the audience is broad — rather than the absence of one.

    So the questions that a draft status would answer are answered by grants:

    Question Answer
    "Is this ready to be seen?" Has anyone been granted event.view beyond the unit-scope holders?
    "Who can see it before it is announced?" Whoever a live allow names — including every unit-scope holder
    "How do I hide it again?" Revoke the grant — revoked_at set, row kept — or write a deny

    Cancellation is a fact, not a status, and the logic diverges from visibility here because the audience is different: the people who must be told are the people who registered, and they must be able to see the event to be told anything.

    Three columns carry it: cancelled_at says when, cancelled_by says who — a person, so the decision has a name attached to it — and cancel_reason says why.

    Cancelling by revoking grants would hide the cancellation from exactly the people who need it. The registrants are the ones who must be told the camp is off; if the event is hidden by revoking their view, they see nothing at all — not "cancelled", just gone. So cancellation is recorded, and the event stays visible, marked.

    A cancelled event stops accepting registrations (the registration window closes implicitly) and keeps every existing row: those registrations are the record that the camp was planned and who was coming. That record is exactly what is needed afterwards — to notify people, and to refund them.

    completed is derived, never set. cancelled_at IS NULL AND ends_at < now(). There is no state to keep consistent, and no job to move events between states.

    One more thing, which is structural: there is no event.is_published and no cancelled status, but there is a cancelled_at. An event that is cancelled and an event that is merely past are distinguished by one NULL — and the "what needs attention?" query is WHERE cancelled_at IS NULL AND ends_at >= now(), ordering by start. That is the whole lifecycle.

    11. Payments on events¶

    An event may cost money, and payment confirms the place — but payment is never mandatory, because plenty of events are free.

    The rule, stated once:
    An event with a fee Registering creates an obligation; the place is confirmed on payment
    An event without a fee Registering confirms the place immediately
    An unpaid registration at capacity Holds a slot until the deadline, then releases to the waitlist
    Money The finance module's problem — events record that a fee applies and what it is

    The only schema here is a pointer: fee_obligation.event_registration_id, one arm of fee_obligation's exactly-one rule — an arm with rows behind it, not a dormant hedge.

    The is_confirmed / deadline interaction is the one automatic transition in this module, and it is worth flagging as such: the design avoids automatic state changes (D-023, D-040), and payment-confirms-the-place is the one it accepts. It is a deadline, not a sweep: it applies only to registrations at a full event with an unpaid fee, and only after the deadline the organizer set. data-model/08 §6 records it as an accepted cost with the reasoning, and open-questions carries it.

    12. The forms: questions and answers¶

    An event may ask registrants for details it needs — tent preference, dietary preference, T- shirt size, whether they are coming by bus.

    Questions are defined per event, and answers are rows.

    event_question          what this event asks
   │
   └── event_answer     what a person said    ──  one row per question per person

    Answers as rows rather than JSONB is the choice that makes the feature work: "who needs a gluten-free meal, and how many?" is a GROUP BY, not a JSON traversal. The cost is a row per answer, which is correct — the number of answers is the number of things the organization must actually act on.

    Questions may apply to attendees, organizers, or both, and may be required or optional. Required means the person registering must answer; it is not a global gate on registration, because a form that blocks sign-up is a form nobody completes.

    Reusable question libraries are rejected. A shared pool of question definitions is structurally the requirement machinery removed in D-044, and it earns its keep only when the same question must mean the same thing across events. Tent preference does not: what one camp asks and what another needs are allowed to differ, and per-event definitions keep them from having to agree.

    Health data, and the widening this represents¶

    Event answers are readable by that event's organizers, with no extra gate (D-061). The mechanism is a seeded grant: event.answer.view on an event, aimed at ORGANIZERS (D-102). Two things follow:

    • "No extra gate" is literally true and visible. The seeded grant is the whole mechanism; there is no clause special-casing organizers. Anyone who wants to see who may read an event's answers reads a grant row.
    • The reach of that grant is per-event. ORGANIZERS with no argument binds to the event the grant sits on, so the seeded row cannot accidentally name the organizers of every camp at once.

    Two consequences of that reach follow, and the second is the significant one.

    1. Only preference-level detail belongs on a form. A dietary preference ("vegetarian", "no pork") is an operational note for the cook. A condition ("coeliac", "nut allergy") is medical data, and nothing in the schema distinguishes them — the difference is what the organizer types into a label. So the discipline is in the form design, not the constraint: data-model/08 §7 states it, and it is guidance, not enforcement.

    2. This is the route by which a leader learns a child has a nut allergy, and that is a deliberate widening. A guardian's reach over a child is one value on the guardianship row — none, view or write — read by a channel of its own, entered only when the act is about someone other than the actor and never answered by a role or a grant (D-074, D-079; appendix/permission-catalog.md §9). What a leader gets over a child's data is not of that kind:

    "A person does not get these by virtue of leading — that is a safeguarding property."

    There is no medical record to be unreachable. Personal data is one class (D-083), so a guarantee that a camp leader "cannot reach a child's medical record at all" describes a category the model does not have. What is true, in the two forms that matter:

    • No role gets it by leading. The starting role set gives a leadership role its rosters and its events — and no read over a person's data (permission-catalog §11). Leadership is not guardianship, and the guardianship channel answers only for the person its row names.
    • It is one grant away. Whoever holds person.view at the unit reads every child's whole record, allergy list included, and a grant at the unit has no narrower reading available, because the unit is the only scope there is (D-097). Nothing in the resolver will stop that and nothing should — but the model does not promise it is impossible either, and a promise the model does not make is worse than none.

    So the protection is a decision about a role, revisitable by whoever administers roles — not a wall.

    Which means: under the starting roles, an allergy recorded on a camp form is the only way the people running the camp will know. That is a coherent and defensible configuration — the parent tells the people present, per event, and nobody outside the family holds the fact — but it is a widening from "the few adults whose guardianship row names them, at one right_level" to "every organizer of any event the child registers for", and it is recorded as such in D-061, with the reasoning, so that nobody later "fixes" the apparent duplication between the two stores by tightening this one.

    Event answers are not the person's record. They are operational detail for one event, read by that event's organizers, and they end with the event — while the same fact, recorded on the person, is audited, curable and erased with them. Those two copies are the same class of data (D-083), which is what turns the retention question in §13 into a real one, and AT-1 into the alternative: collect the fact once, confirm it at the door. See data-model/08a §11 for the retention question, which is open.

    13. Open issues¶

    Moved to ../open-questions.md under G. Events, where they are tracked with the rest. The ones this module raises:

    • The age ceiling on an open event — settled: accepted as a risk. The grant is the rule, and a ceiling is writable today as event.register_self aimed at MEMBERS * YOUNGER(15) (§4, D-107). EV-1 stays closed as a choice the organization renews rather than a limit it inherits — nobody has to be told the ceiling cannot be stated, only whether it should be (§4, D-070).
    • What else should be seeded to ORGANIZERS. D-102 seeds exactly one grant, event.answer.view. Everything else an organizer needs — editing the event, working the roster — reaches them only through a unit-scope or item grant they happen to hold, and no decision says whether that is the intended shape or an oversight (§5, §6). This module does not seed more of its own accord.
    • Whether a bound selector makes its audience single-scope. An audience whose expression names PLACED cannot be aimed at a unit-scope grant, so a shared audience row can stop being reusable across scopes. The refusal lands on the grant, which is faithful to the rule as written; whether the editor should warn at the audience instead is unsettled (§7).
    • WHITELISTED. No stored state to name it, so it is not a selector (§7) — a question about whether the feature is wanted, and not one about whether the language could hold it: a predicate over a column is an ordinary operand now (D-107).
    • Event answer retention (§12). Answers end with the event — but a dietary answer naming a condition is data of exactly the same class as what the person record holds, held under weaker rules and fresher. No policy yet; see EV-2.
    • Cancelled events and fees (§10–11). Cancelling keeps the registrations; whether it releases the obligations, refunds them, or neither is the finance module's call.
    • Presence for minors and the safeguarding record (§9). was_present is the "who was in our care" record. Whether it needs its own retention or its own permission is unresolved — and §7's refusal of PRESENT is the sharp edge of why that matters.
