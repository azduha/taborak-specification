    Authorization Model¶

    The permission system end to end. This is the document to read if you read only one.

    The whole model, stated once.

    Scopes. There are two. The unit — the organization, of which there is exactly one, and which is a scope and never a row — and one item: an event, a group, a role, later a document. A grant at the unit reaches every item of every type; a grant on an item reaches that item and nothing else. There is no tier between them and nothing to test.

    A grant is: a grantee (a person or an audience) + a scope + one permission code + an effect of allow or deny + a period.

    The answer. Allowed when some live allow applies and no live deny applies. There is no level, nothing is inherited downward, and no nearer grant beats a further one — because once the answer is boolean, there is nothing left to order.

    Roles and groups are not sources of authority. They are named sets of people that a grant aims at.

    1. The three channels of authority¶

    Every question of the form "may person P do X?" is answered by consulting three mechanisms. They are separate because they have different lifetimes, different granters, and different revocation semantics.

    # Channel Question it answers Where the answer comes from Reaches
    1 Grants What has this person, or this set of people, been given? access_grant, aimed at a person or an audience The unit, or one item
    2 Guardianship What may this person do regarding this child? guardianship.right_level compared with permission.right_kind One dependent
    3 Resource ownership Is this their own data? (derived) The row's owner

    Channel 3 is not a table. It is the rule that a person may always act on their own profile, their own requests, their own memberships. It is resolved by comparing person_id before the other channels are consulted.

    What a channel reaches once one of them says yes. Each of the three answers whether, never how much of a person: there is one read rule and one write rule over a person's record, and no narrower tier standing beside them, so a grant that reaches the record reaches all of it. What that consists of, and why there is no sensitive corner left inside it to withhold, is 08-person-data.md — worth reading before proposing a fourth channel that reaches "only" one kind of fact.

    Why the channels stay apart rather than one unified grants table. They differ in ways that matter:

    • Channel 2 has a legal character — guardianship is a fact about the world, often court-ordered, and the organization records what it was told rather than deciding it. It does not expire by itself. A guardianship ends when a party to it says so, and nobody else — not a job, not a birthday. Why: a parent's authority over health and school records does not end at 18 in many jurisdictions, and in some it ends at a different age, so any date the system computed would be a claim it cannot support (D-069).
    • Channel 1 is the only channel that can name a set rather than a person (D-042). A guardianship is between two named people and an owner is one person. That is a boundary, not an oversight — it is why an office can be aimed at but never held by a rule (§4, F-12).
    • A deny lives in channel 1 and defeats all three (§7.1). Nowhere else can one be written, and nowhere else can one be argued with.

    Ownership and guardianship carry no scope, no membership condition, and no level. Nothing in §2 or §6 changes either channel.

    Roles and groups are not channels¶

    A role confers nothing by itself: it is a named set of people that a grant aims at, in the same sentence as a group, an event's attendees, and "everyone". The codes an office carries are the live grants whose audience names ROLE(x). Authority therefore has exactly one source besides ownership and guardianship, and it is D-005 said one level down: being something is not being able to do something.

    Whether a person's membership counts is not a channel either. It is a term inside a grant's expression — MEMBERS, evaluated live on the checked date (§6.1) — and never a clause the resolver adds on its own.

    2. Scopes — the unit and the item¶

    There are exactly two scopes.

    The unit is the organization. There is one. It is not a row, not a table, and not the top of a structure: it is the non-item scope that grant_scope_type already carries, whose code is unit, whose is_item_scoped is false, and at which a grant simply has no item to name.

    An item is exactly one thing: one event, one group, one role, and later one document. A grant on it names it.

    Reach is one-directional and there is nothing to test. A grant at the unit reaches every item of every type. A grant on one item reaches that item and nothing else. No item reaches another item, and no item reaches the unit.

    That is the complete theory of scope. There is no nesting, no path, no depth, no tier between the unit and the item, and no relation on the item — no owner, no parent, no covering body — that a check consults. A point-in-time question about people — who was in the Thursday group in 2011? — is a point-in-time read over person_group_member, whose rows are dated (D-098).

    Which codes may be granted where is data, not prose. permission.applies_to is unit or unit_and_item (D-099): event.create and membership.manage are nonsense on an event, and the column says so instead of hoping a reader agrees. It is checked when the grant is saved, not at check time. Why: a grant of event.create written on one event is refused with the reason in the error, because an event cannot be created twice and a creation permission attached to a thing that already exists answers no question. The system namespace is unit-only for the same reason, which makes this column who enforces now the rule that system.admin can never arrive through an item grant (D-081).

    Nothing in the model reproduces "access was granted by the body itself". There is one unit, so a person.view grant at unit scope reaches every member's whole record — and person.view is the widest read permission in the system (D-083, D-086). What stands between a person and that read is a person.view grant held by a named adult, an audit trail whose payloads expire after two years (D-088), and a report of who holds the write.

    Every grant is attributable to one locus of accountability. "Who gave this person access to this record?" is always answered by a named person at the unit, on a date. It is not necessarily a nearby name: nobody's authority is derived from the body a piece of data belongs to, because there is one body. There is likewise nothing to restructure, so a grant's blast radius cannot grow by reorganization.

    Nobody enforces a direction of authority, because there is no direction. The question "may the center read what happens inside a body?" cannot be asked — not "the application instead of the database", but unaskable (D-081; the rule it enforced was D-031). The real requirement behind that machinery was "tell everyone about this event", and it is one grant aimed at EVERYONE or MEMBERS (§6.2).

    2.1 system.admin¶

    system.admin is the one wildcard permission: a check for X.y is satisfied by an exact grant of X.y or by system.admin and nothing else (D-068). It crosses no boundary, because there is no boundary for it to cross.

    Two properties keep a wildcard from being the hole in the design, and both stand on their own:

    1. Its holders are alarmed on. The report listing current holders must notify on every change — a new holder is either a planned act or an incident, and either way someone should know within the hour.
    2. It is not delegatable (§9), and it is applies_to = unit, so it cannot arrive on an item.

    If system.admin becomes the mechanism by which ordinary organization-wide work is done, that is a signal about the catalog rather than about a boundary rule: the work is telling you it needs a permission that does not exist. Record it in the decision log instead of working around it.

    3. Permissions — the atomic unit¶

    A permission is a coded capability. It is the only thing the check function ever looks at.

    A permission row carries its code, the resource and action it decomposes into, a description, is_system — set for the codes the platform ships with, which therefore cannot be deleted — and two more columns: applies_to (unit or unit_and_item, above) and right_kind (read or write). right_kind exists for exactly one customer: guardianship (§1, channel 2) grades a guardian at none / view / write, and that channel needs something to compare a permission against. none covers nothing, view covers read codes, write covers both. It is a column whose only job is one other mechanism, and saying so is cheaper than discovering it later (D-074).

    Codes are resource.action and are stable identifiers, never display strings. The UI shows a human label; the code never changes. Renaming a permission code is a breaking change and must be avoided.

    Naming convention¶

    Pattern Meaning Example
    <resource>.view Read person.view
    <resource>.create Create (always applies_to = unit) event.create
    <resource>.<field>.edit Edit a field subset person.contact.edit
    <resource>.delete Delete (soft, and audited) document.delete
    <resource>.member.manage Write one set's membership person_group.member.manage

    There is no row here for acting on a supervised person, because there is no dependent.* namespace to name (D-074). A guardian's act is checked under the ordinary code for the thing being touched — person.view, person.contact.edit — and reaches the guardianship channel by the shape of the request, not by its name (D-079).

    Every capability is narrow and explicit. A check for X.y needs an exact grant of X.y, or system.admin. There is no <resource>.admin wildcard and no prefix match in the resolver (D-068).

    An office is a stack of rows, not one word. The catalog holds 66 codes, and a broad office is a couple of dozen rows (appendix/permission-catalog.md §11). Why: that is the price of "what exactly does this person have?" being answerable by reading rows, and the reason there is no word like manage whose contents a reader would have to look up. Roles and audiences are the bundle, and nothing else is (D-099).

    4. Roles are offices; groups are company¶

    Two tables of the same shape, and the difference between them decides who may edit the membership.

    Role (role, role_holder) Group (person_group, person_group_member)
    What it is An office: leader, finance, IT. Small, visible holders, expected to be seen Company: the Thursday group, the kitchen crew, this year's camp staff. People who do something together
    Holder row role_holder(role, person, valid_from, valid_to, granted_by, note) person_group_member(group, person, valid_from, valid_to, added_by, note)
    Held where At the unit, always. There is one scope, so there is nothing else to hold it at The group is its own item, and a grant can be written on it
    Permission that writes its membership role.assign, role.unassign — 🔒🔒, the most sensitive family in the catalog person_group.member.manage — ⚠
    In an expression ROLE(x), or bare x GROUP(x), or bare x
    Authority by being in it None None

    role carries code, name, description, is_system, sort_order — and no scope column, no membership flag, no term, and no delegation flag. Lending is said on the grant, not on the role (§9).

    Why two tables when they are technically the same shape — and they are the same shape, which is exactly what D-080 teaches a reader to distrust. The answer is the permission consequence: one table would merge those two codes into one. A single membership table with a kind column gives you a single permission that both adds a child to the Thursday group and puts the holder into SYSTEM_ADMINS. That is the merge to refuse. Same columns, different codes, and ROLE(x) / GROUP(x) distinct in the grant text so the distinction is visible to whoever reads the grant.

    A role confers nothing. The codes an office carries are the live grants whose audience names ROLE(x). That is what makes "what may a treasurer do?" a question with an answer: it is a query over visible grant rows, not a walk through a bundle table nobody reads. What "the treasurer's permissions" consists of:

    role         code = treasurer                (a name, a description, is_system = false)
role_holder  treasurer ← Ana, 2026-01-01 → open
access_grant audience = MEMBERS * ROLE(treasurers)   scope = unit
             permission = fee.view            effect = allow
access_grant audience = MEMBERS * ROLE(treasurers)   scope = unit
             permission = payment.record      effect = allow
access_grant audience = MEMBERS * ROLE(treasurers)   scope = unit
             permission = report.finance.view effect = allow
  … one row per code, and the list is the office

    Neither a role nor a group authorizes anything by existing, which is D-005 and D-019 restated one level down: participation is not authority, and storing the participation in a tidier table does not quietly promote it. A group additionally has no parent and no children, no type vocabulary, no code, no path, no nesting rules, no rename history, no split or merger edge, no leadership of its own, no roles, and no fee (D-098).

    Both are items. person_group and role are registered in grant_scope_type, so "these two may work this group's roster" and "these two may manage the counselors roster" are grants on one item — reachable only by someone who already holds the corresponding code at the unit (§2).

    Roles are runtime data, and the seed set is not a specification (D-052, D-053): the organization creates roles, composes them from grants, and deletes the unused ones. The real definitions live in appendix/permission-catalog.md §11. One role ships, and ships is_system so an editor refuses to delete it: counselors. A permanent, organization-wide population is the opposite of the organization's own definition of a group ("a temporary set of members that do events together"), and the table decides the sensitivity that protects the set every safeguarding convention hangs from. It is still only a set — being in it confers nothing until a grant names it (§6.2).

    An elected leader is a holder of a role, and the audience that names them is ROLE(leaders), meaning what its name says. What is genuinely not stored is the elected-versus-appointed distinction: it survives only as roles the organization invents (elected_council next to appointed_it) or not at all, which is the open question F-18. Where the appointment workflows end is 04-organization-structure.md §6.

    A role is held by exactly one person per row. A role cannot be held by a rule the way a group is: an office record that cannot answer "who was treasurer in 2023" is not an office record, and a derived holder list cannot answer that (F-12). Grants aim at roles rather than roles being filled by grants, which is the same fact seen from the other side.

    The holder period matters¶

    valid_from / valid_to on role_holder means an office ends on a date even if nobody remembers to revoke it. Leavers are the classic audit failure: the volunteer who stepped down in 2019 still has an active account with treasurer access in 2026.

    Nothing forces a holder row to end, and the report is the control (D-071). No other mechanism stands behind it: the report is carrying the whole weight of expiring authority (§6.1, §11).

    5. The grant — one permission, one effect¶

    A grant is the whole of channel 1, and its shape is the whole of this section:

    grantee + scope + one permission_id + effect + period.

    Grantee: a person or an audience, never both and never neither. Scope: the unit, or exactly one item. Effect: allow or deny. Period: valid_from, valid_to, live on the checked date.

    Plus provenance: granted_by, granted_at, grant_reason, and for a lent authority is_delegated with delegated_from naming who it came from (§9).

    One code per grant, not a set. That is what makes the answer readable: "what exactly does this person have?" is a list of rows, and "who else can do this?" is the same list read the other way.

    Writing a grant is itself an authorized act: creating one requires access_grant.create at the matching scope. Composing an expression grants nothing (§6.2).

    Allow and deny are not symmetrical¶

    Allowed when some live allow applies and no live deny applies. Everything else in this document is elaboration.

    A deny is legal at either scope. A deny at the unit is precisely the case an expression cannot serve: "exclude her from what the unit grants everyone."

    - in an expression is not a deny, and the two are not interchangeable. Subtracting a set narrows one grant's reach. A deny defeats every grant that applies to that person, including ones written elsewhere, later, or by somebody else. The first cannot express "she must not have this, whatever else she is given", which is D-013's whole reason for existing: a court-ordered restriction must not be beatable by a broader grant someone forgot about (D-099).

    One report comes with this, and it is the whole hygiene of the thing. A live deny whose grantee, scope and code match no live allow is dead weight, and it should be reported — a deny that denies nothing is how a stale restriction survives its reason. Nothing enforces it; the report is the mechanism (§11).

    Why scope_type/scope_id and not one column per item type¶

    scope_id has no foreign key — it is a polymorphic reference, and this is a deliberate, accepted trade-off (D-011):

    • Cost: no referential integrity. A grant can point at a deleted event.
    • Benefit: the permission system does not need a schema change every time a new grantable thing is invented, which — since documents and everything else are still deferred — will happen repeatedly.

    Mitigations, all of which are required: the grant_scope_type registry validates scope types; deleting an item of a granted type must close its grants (an application-level obligation, tested); a nightly job reports orphaned grants; ids are never reused. The heavier alternative — a grantable_item supertable every grantable entity references — is declined for flexibility, and is ../open-questions.md if anyone wants to overrule it (F-2).

    6. The resolver¶

    One function answers every authorization question. Written once, used everywhere — an authorization rule that exists in two places will diverge.

    The call names a person, a permission code, a scope, and a date. The scope is the unit or exactly one item: asking at the unit is how "may this person create an event?" is asked, and asking at an item is how "may this person edit event 1204?" is asked. at defaults to now. Where the thing being touched has an owner, the caller passes the owner too, because one step below needs to know whose record this is. The answer is allowed or denied; the function has no other output, and no part of it is stored. (Field names and the exact signature are in data-model/05 §10.)

    The steps run in this order:

    1. Deny — absolute, at any scope. If any live deny applies — its scope is the unit or the exact item being checked, its code is the code being checked, its grantee is this person or an audience whose expression contains them on the checked date — the answer is no. No scope outranks a deny, and neither does a channel: a per-event allow cannot restore what a unit-scope suspension removed (§7.1).
    2. Ownership. If the target resource's owner is this person → yes for the read, edit and self-service actions. Never conditioned on anything (§6.1): a lapsed person must still be able to reach the screen they renew on.

    3. Guardianship — entered by the shape of the request, and it answers with allows only. Compare the actor with the owner of the thing being touched. Same person, and this channel is not in play at all. Different people, and the question is whether the actor holds a live guardianship over that subject as of at whose right level covers the checked permission's right_kind — view covers read, write covers both, none covers nothing (D-074). Covered → yes. Every other outcome — no row, none, a level too low — is not a refusal: the channel says nothing and the resolver continues, where the person's own grants are tried exactly as they always were (D-079). A parent who is also the event registrar holds view over their own child and must not be refused the roster by their own guardianship.

    4. Nothing escapes by naming. The act that routes here is chosen by whose record it touches, so a permission written next year for a field a guardian must edit is covered the day the field exists. Dispatch never reads a code's prefix: an ordinary person.contact.edit must reach this step like any other act.

    5. An ended guardianship never opens this channel again. What a former guardian may still do is what any other person may do, through their own grants.

    6. The row is the whole test. The guard must not be rewritten as "the subject is a minor": the resolver contains no age branch. It contains an OLDER(n) operand, which is a set a grant has to name before anything is looked at, and the difference is the point: nothing consults a birth date unless somebody wrote a rule that needed it, and no rule is inferred — and D-070 and D-077 keep it that way.

    7. Allow — does any live grant apply? Two shapes, both boolean, neither ranked against the other:

    8. a unit grant of this code, to this person directly or to an audience whose expression contains them on at. It reaches this item because a unit grant reaches every item (§2) — there is no tier to walk and no relation on the item to test.
    9. an item grant of this code on exactly this item, to a person or an audience — event.view on event 1204 is how a helper sees one event and no other.

    Either → yes. 4. Otherwise → no.

    Steps 1–3 are not a precedence. With a boolean answer, two allows that both apply produce one answer, so there is nothing to order and renumbering steps 1–3 changes nothing about who can do what. Their order stands for two reasons that are not precedence — cheap checks before expensive ones, and step 2's "says nothing" fall-through — and neither order is load-bearing for the answer. What is ordered, and must stay so, is step 0: the deny is asked first and can defeat every allow below it (D-032).

    Points worth stressing:

    • Default deny. Step 4. Any new resource starts with nobody able to touch it.
    • Fail closed, in every direction — and closed is a direction here, not a safe place to stand. An operand that does not resolve is the empty set; an expression that cannot be evaluated reaches nobody; a scope type nobody registered grants nothing. This is the same posture as step 4 and step 0, and it is the direction every uncertainty in this model resolves in. Why: an empty set narrows an allow and widens a deny, so fail-closed arithmetic is what the expression says, not what safety wants — which is why the answer to a failing operand is a report and not a refusal (D-106).
    • A grant may name an audience instead of a person. The grantee is resolved by the expression in §6.2; the scope and the code are resolved exactly as for a personal grant.
    • Effective permissions are never stored. They are computed per request. A stored permission snapshot will drift from the grants that produced it — the only question is how long before anyone notices. There is a second reason to keep that question open: the snapshot is also the only artefact that could ever answer who could read this in 2023, since set membership is recomputed from state that keeps moving and the audit payload that recorded the change expires (§6.2, F-7).
    • Time is a parameter. at defaults to now, but the resolver accepts a historical time. This enables "could this person have done this on that date?" — needed for auditing past actions, and free if built in from the start. The dated rows behind MEMBERS, GROUP(x), ROLE(x) and the attendance selectors are all periods (§4, §6.2), so the recomputation has something honest to read.

    6.1 MEMBERS — the membership test, moved into the expression¶

    Whether a person's membership counts is a selector term, not a clause of the resolver. MEMBERS is evaluated live on the checked date like any other operand, and writing it or leaving it out is the grant writer's act (§6.2). The resolver has no membership branch and carries no exemption for one.

    The four things a writer needs to be able to say:

    To Write
    gate an office on a current membership MEMBERS * ROLE(x)
    leave a whole office ungated ROLE(x) — the same expression without MEMBERS
    waive it for one named person inside a gated rule MEMBERS * ROLE(x) + PERSON(y)
    apply it to nobody: ownership, guardianship, and any grant aimed at a person nothing — a channel has no expression, and a personal grant has none (§5)

    The third row is also writable without a selector: "and also him" is a second grant aimed at that person, which is how the waiver looks when the writer would rather not touch the expression everyone else reads.

    The test is opt-in per grant, and the failure mode must be named rather than buried. A grant written as ROLE(treasurers) with no MEMBERS authorizes a lapsed member, silently, forever. Nothing errors, nothing reports itself when it happens, and nothing about the grant looks wrong — a lapsed treasurer is still a treasurer. Three controls, none of which is the resolver:

    1. A standing report of live grants naming a ROLE(...) operand whose expression does not contain MEMBERS. An office held by a non-member is the case worth a human eye, and the report is narrow enough to actually be read.
    2. Seeded grants and any grant template carry MEMBERS, so the path of least repetition is the safe path.
    3. Import completeness. MEMBERS * COUNSELORS is only as correct as the counselor roster: an import that misses 300 counselors silently un-gates them. It fails closed — nothing breaks, which is exactly why nobody notices. The import reconciliation report of D-090 must count people marked as counselors in the corpus against role_holder rows written.

    Who the test should spare is a decision the organization has already made, and it decides the default: "everything except guardianship and adult volunteers." A volunteer whose own fee is unpaid still runs the Thursday meeting, and losing the roster on the night the fee lapses is the failure to prevent. So leaving MEMBERS out is the normal shape of a grant to a contractor, a photographer or a parent helper, not an exemption somebody must be granted. The line that judgment draws holds, and it holds hardest where only a report stands behind it: the report is how the organization notices; the resolver is not how it punishes.

    Why the test sits in the expression. A resolver clause applies to everything or nothing, and so has to carry its own exemptions; an expression says which grants are gated, in the text of the grant, where the person writing it can see it and a report can read it. A clause cannot answer "is this particular permission meant to require membership?", because it records the answer nowhere. Nothing enforces MEMBERS being present: a convention, not a guard (D-081).

    Who is gated:

    Channel Gated? Why, and how it is written
    Ownership (step 1) No Otherwise a lapse locks a person out of the screen they renew on. Not expressible in an expression at all
    Guardianship (step 2) No A legal relationship, not a membership
    Grants (step 3) Only if the expression says so MEMBERS * …. A grant to a contractor, a photographer or a parent helper who was never a member is written without MEMBERS
    A deny (step 0) Never, in either direction §7.1

    6.2 Audiences — an expression over named sets¶

    A grant aimed at a set of people names an expression over named sets — MEMBERS * ROLE(treasurers), EVERYONE - GROUP(x), PERSON(a) + PERSON(b) — stored as a tree of binary nodes, evaluated live on the checked date, and never materialized. The language and its operators, precedence and canonical rendering, the thirteen seeded selectors and the argument each takes, the rules that make the tree safe (cycles, the three states, keyless leaves, the editor's preview), and what a grant's reach is recorded in are a self-contained subject and live on their own page: 06a-audiences-and-selectors.md. What the resolver does with an audience is §6 above; what a grant is, and how allow and deny interact, is §5 and §7.

    7. Precedence¶

    7.1 Who decides — deny is absolute¶

    1. Deny          (a live deny applies at ANY scope)   ← WINS OVER EVERYTHING
2. Ownership     (it's your own row)
3. Guardianship  (the act is about someone else — allows only)
4. Any live allow (unit grant or item grant — §6 step 3)
5. Deny          (nothing matched)

    A deny is checked before anything else and is never subject to any ordering. A deny at the unit blocks access to one event even if a grant on that event explicitly allows it. There is no scope local enough to override a suspension (D-013; D-032's first half, which is the whole of its surviving content).

    A deny is also never subject to the membership test, in either direction. A deny blocks someone who is not a member, which is obvious. What is worth stating: a deny to an audience blocks the people in that audience whether or not they are current members, because the deny is evaluated against who the expression matches, not against whether they are paid up. Suspensions are absolute; MEMBERS is a condition on a grant of authority, and there is no authority here to condition.

    A deny is legal at the unit as well as on an item, and "exclude her from what the unit grants everyone" is the case only a deny can serve (§5).

    Nothing else orders. Two allows that both apply produce one answer, so there is no tie-break, no tier ladder, and no "is this amount high enough" question anywhere in the resolver: a grant says yes-or-no about one code. So a grant on an item applies at full force whatever the person holds at the unit, a unit grant keeps working for the unit's other items, and no grant can retract another — only a deny can (D-099).

    8. There is no permission cache, and what replaces it¶

    There is no permission cache. A check resolves; a request memoizes; the only thing cached across requests is a compilation. Nothing in this system stores a permission answer, and no part of can() is written to a table (D-114).

    What a check costs. One indexed read fetches the checked person's live grant rows — a few dozen rows at the size D-112 states — and each candidate grant's expression is evaluated against a handful of indexed point lookups in membership, role_holder, person_group_member, event_registration and the guardianship rows. The arithmetic is not the problem; the N+1 across one screen is: a 50-row list asking four codes per row is two hundred checks, and two hundred checks doing the same person's grant read two hundred times is the shape that makes a cache look necessary. The three mechanisms below fix that shape directly.

    1. A compiled expression is cached — parsed once, operand kinds resolved once, the parameterized predicate emitted once, keyed by the expression's root and ruleset_version. It holds no person and no item, because both arrive as parameters, and that is precisely why it survives across everyone in the organization while an answer never could: nothing in a compilation depends on whom or where it is being asked about.
    2. A request memoizes. The checked person's grant rows are read once and every code, item and date in that request is answered from them in memory; a named operand that recurs in one request (ROLE(leader), GROUP(cubs)) is resolved once. Its lifetime is the request, so it has no invalidation policy and no staleness window — there is nothing to invalidate.
    3. The bulk screens batch. can_many(person, codes[], item?) answers a screen's affordances from one resolution; which_of(people[], code, item) answers a list's from one pass. The per-item preview (06a §6.2, F-15) and the permissions tab (D-109) are written against these, not against a loop of single checks.

    The one thing cached across requests besides a compilation is a named roster: ROLE(x) over role_holder, GROUP(x) over person_group_member, keyed by x. Two conditions, and together they are the whole of the exception. The key is the thing a write touches, so invalidation is local to the row that changed and never global — the property an answer cache could never have, because an answer's key names a person and the write that changes it usually names nobody. And the cache holds the dated rows, or the set with its validity ranges, never the set as of today, so the checked date is still applied at check time and a period ending at midnight needs no job to be seen. A roster is also something a person maintains and can point at; an answer never was. The exception is exactly two selectors wide. MEMBERS is not a roster — nobody writes its rows, and the 1 January sweep rewrites the membership dates it reads for everybody at once. ORGANIZERS is not one either: it has holder rows, but it is bound to an item (D-102), so a cache keyed by it would be a stored answer to "who was at this event", which is the set D-102 declined to make permanent.

    What may never be stored again is invariant 19 (§11 of data-model/05-authorization.md): no row, table or field may map a person — or a person and a code, or a person and an item — to a permission answer. Its enforcement is structural, which is the best kind this corpus has: the schema has nowhere to put one. The residue is named rather than glossed — an implementer who wants a cache back is stopped by this page and by review, not by a constraint, so for them it is a convention, not a guard (D-081), and D-114 lists the three things a returning cache must carry: a per-person invalidation handle next to the global one, an explicit statement of which tables it watches, and that statement derived from the operand registry rather than from a list of ideas.

    What live resolution buys.

    • "The safeguarding case is immediate" is true by construction. A revoke, a deny, a closed period, an ended guardianship, a membership.terminate ⛔ — safeguarding_officer, the most safeguarding-shaped write in the catalog — or a cancelled registration — all visible on the next check, with no job in between and no list to remember to include them in. A terminated member stops satisfying MEMBERS at the instant the row closes: data-model/03-membership.md §6 asks for exactly that, and asks it of nothing, because MEMBERS reads membership live (§6.1).
    • The calendar cannot drift an answer. A birthday crossing OLDER(n), a period reaching its own valid_to, the 1 January sweep — each moves the answer at the instant it happens. D-107's ruling stands — "IT IS FINE" — and needs no mechanism to hold it.
    • ruleset_version is bumped by a write to audience_operand or selector_type only. A compilation depends on an expression's shape and the seeded vocabulary and on nothing else. access_grant is not in that set because grants are not compiled: a check reads the person's live grant rows, so a new, edited or revoked grant changes what that read returns and leaves nothing stale to drop; the deprecated | hidden | disabled flag is not in it because a state is not compiled in — resolution reads it live, once per request, memoized, which is also why disabled still means now (D-109); role_holder, person_group_member, guardianship_permission and a person merge are not in it because they move rosters and rows that are read live or invalidated locally. Concretely: adding a child to a group drops nothing.
    • "Whom does this change reach?" is answerable. A compilation is finite and enumerable over the audience tree, so "an edit to a shared audience invalidates everything" becomes "it invalidates the compilations that embed it" — a bounded walk over rows that already exist. One qualification, stated in the direction and not the spirit: it is not the walk the save path performs for cycles, which runs forward from the tree being saved. Invalidation needs the reverse direction, the one the delete-refusal check already runs. Same forest, same rows, opposite way — and the cost of listing that forest is registered as unverified at any size in data-model/05 §13, so what is claimed here is bounded, not cheap (D-103).

    A compiled form is bound to its item. Since a bound operand means the same expression yields different people on different items, a compiled form computed while checking one item may never be served for another: the item is a parameter of a predicate, and a predicate bound to event 1204 is not the predicate for event 1205 (F-6).

    What is given up. Nothing pre-pays the expensive screens. The per-item preview, the permissions tab and the "who holds this" listing do their work when somebody opens them, which is affordable at the stated size — but D-109's ruling has no fallback, and that is a dependency, not a comfort. At D-112's trigger the reopen answer is named rather than vague: materialize effective_permission per person, and call it that — a real materialization with a per-person invalidation handle and a stated staleness window.

    What is not given up is the rule stated in 06a §6.2, and it is the whole cost model of this page: inside a check, an expression is compiled to a predicate on the person being asked, never materialized as a set. No job anywhere is permitted to do the opposite.

    9. Delegation¶

    A leader going on leave, a treasurer handing over during an audit — the common need to lend authority temporarily.

    A delegation is an ordinary grant with its provenance set: is_delegated, delegated_from naming the person whose authority is being lent, grant_reason, and a valid_to. Because a grant is one permission code, "do not lend more than you hold" is a set-membership test (D-099).

    Rules:

    • A delegation always has an end date. Enforced on the write path.
    • A delegation cannot exceed the delegator's own authority — validate at grant time that the code being delegated is one the delegator currently resolves to, and re-check at use time, because the delegator may lose it mid-delegation.
    • The provenance is recorded, so an audit can answer "who really had this authority on that date?" — it lists both the holder and the delegator.
    • A deny cannot be lent and cannot be lent around. A delegation is an allow; the delegatee's answer is computed from their own rows plus the lent one, and their own denies still apply.
    • Delegation of system.admin is prohibited outright.

    Lending is said on the grant, not on the role: role has no is_delegatable column, and nothing about an office says whether its authority may be lent.

    Assumption: D-100 names is_delegatable in neither its deleted nor its kept list of role's columns. Recorded in ../open-questions.md; the open list carries it as item 55.

    10. Anti-patterns this design rejects¶

    Stated explicitly, because each is a common and expensive mistake:

    Rejected Why Instead
    person.is_admin boolean Cannot scope, cannot date, cannot audit, cannot delegate A grant at the unit, aimed at a role
    Role on person (person.role = 'leader') One role per person, no history role_holder, many per person
    Checking membership type in code (if type == 'adult') Rules change; the check is invisible and untestable A permission, granted deliberately. There is one membership type now, so this is doubly wrong
    Deriving access from holding a role or being in a group Being something is not being able to do something (D-005), and a role confers nothing by itself A live grant whose audience names ROLE(x) or GROUP(x) — §4
    A grant written as ROLE(x) when it meant MEMBERS * ROLE(x) It is a term the writer must type, and forgetting it silently authorizes a lapsed member The standing report of role grants without MEMBERS, seeded grants that carry it, and the import count — §6.1
    An access_level, or any comparison of "how much" a grant gives A level is a bundle of permissions under one word, and it re-imports the rank rule and a tie-break that have no consumers Roles and audiences as the bundle — what D-068 already said — and one row per code when you need it in writing. §3, D-099
    - in an expression used instead of a deny Subtracting narrows one grant's reach; it cannot say "whatever else she is given" A deny, which defeats every grant — §5, §7.1
    An expression whose reach nobody saw EVERYONE is a legal operand, and a bound operand means different people on different items The editor expands the set for an item at the moment of granting — 06a §6.2
    PRESENT(e) over was_present NULL until someone takes a register: a volunteer's forgotten tick would revoke a child's own photos The four attendance tiers, and presence stays a safeguarding signal — 06a §Event sets
    A GUARDIANS operand Guardianship is a channel with its own right_level over a child, reaching every action about that child; an operand would offer a subset of what they hold The guardianship channel (§1, §6 step 2); for a kept list of contact adults, a person_group — D-113
    A LIST operand, or a table of an audience's own names A hand-written set is not a different kind of thing from a named one PERSON(a) + PERSON(b) inside the expression — §Grammar, D-105
    Downward inheritance of authority through a unit tree, and scope_exposure rows Both need a hierarchy, and there is one unit A grant at the unit, which reaches every item — §2, D-097
    "Most specific scope always wins", including against a deny Only allows could ever be ordered, and allows are not ordered Deny absolute, and no ordering at all — §7, D-099
    A bulk "expose this body's scope" tool There is one unit and nothing to expose One grant at the unit, or one grant aimed at EVERYONE / MEMBERS — §2, D-045
    Treating an organization-scoped role as universal "so the registrar can work" Everything is unit data; there is no second side of that distinction If a permission is missing, add the permission — §2.1
    Role → permission mapping hardcoded in the app Cannot be reviewed by the organization, cannot change without deployment Grant rows naming ROLE(x) — readable, and the answer to "what may a treasurer do?"
    Deriving access from guardianship without a right Authorization by identity is unauditable and unsafe The explicit right level on the guardianship row, compared against right_kind
    Deleting a grant to revoke it Loses the record of what was revoked when valid_to, and a deny when the fact of the restriction matters
    A live deny that matches no allow A deny that denies nothing is how a stale restriction survives its reason The dead-weight-deny report — §5
    Two authorization implementations They diverge; the weaker one becomes the exploit One resolver, §6
    Unit leader sees all member data by default Fails safeguarding review Scoped grants, resolved per operation — and §2 on the protection that is not in the model

    11. Open issues¶

    • Which grants are meant to carry MEMBERS? Nothing enforces it (§6.1), and the tab that lists the answer is decided while its threshold is not — every ROLE(...) operand without MEMBERS, or only the offices marked sensitive? It lands in the tab's Unusual section either way, so what is still undecided is only how wide that section is (F-13, F-21).
    • The holder period. Nothing forces a role_holder row to end, and the report stands alone (§4, D-071).
    • Who may compose an expression? audience.manage covers creating and editing a set, and the catalog is silent on the edit that puts EVERYONE into a grant's audience (appendix/permission-catalog.md §12). No new code was invented for this — the gap is the finding.
    • What reopens the settled answers on this page. Two named conditions, both carried so nobody has to rediscover them: a materialized effective_permission table becomes worth building at D-112's size trigger, and then only as a performance structure with a per-person invalidation handle and a stated staleness window — never as a record of whom a rule reached (§8); and a historical check is answered by the same evaluator running over history as it is written, which is not a promise for the operands whose set is a current fact (06a, F-7, F-17).
