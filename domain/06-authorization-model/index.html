    Authorization Model¶

    The permission system end to end. This is the document to read if you read only one.

    1. The four channels of authority¶

    Every question of the form "may person P do X?" is answered by exactly one of four mechanisms. They are separate tables because they have different lifetimes, different granters, and different revocation semantics.

    # Channel Question it answers Table Grants authority over
    1 Role assignment What may this person do in the organization? role_assignment Everything within its scope
    2 Guardianship What may this person do regarding this child? guardianship.right_level One dependent
    3 Access grant What may this person, or this set of people, do to this specific item? access_grant One record (event, document, unit)
    4 Resource ownership Is this their own data? (derived) The row's owner

    Channel 4 is not a table. It is the rule that a person may always act on their own profile, their own requests, their own memberships. It is resolved by comparing person_id before any other channel is consulted.

    What a channel reaches once one of them says yes. Each of the four answers whether, never how much of a person: there is one read rule and one write rule over a person's record, and no narrower tier standing beside them, so a grant that reaches the record reaches all of it. What that consists of, and why there is no sensitive corner left inside it to withhold, is 08-person-data.md — worth reading before proposing a fifth channel that reaches "only" one kind of fact.

    Why keep channels 1–3 apart rather than one unified grants table. They differ in ways that matter:

    • Channel 2 has a legal character — guardianship is a fact about the world, often court-ordered, and the organization records what it was told rather than deciding it. It does not expire by itself. An earlier version of this bullet said it "expires when a child reaches majority", which is exactly the claim D-069 withdrew, together with the review queue that asserted it more quietly: a parent's authority over health and school records does not end at 18 in many jurisdictions, and in some it ends at a different age, so any date the system computed would be a claim it cannot support. A guardianship ends when a party to it says so (D-073), and nobody else — not a job, not a birthday. Channel 1 does not expire by itself either, for the same kind of reason: it is a fact about the world.
    • Channel 3 is per-record and therefore high-volume and short-lived. Channel 1 is low-volume and long-lived. Mixing them makes both hard to reason about.
    • Channel 3 may target a person or an audience (D-042) — "everyone", "all members", "the leaders of this unit". Channels 1 and 2 never do: a role is held by a named person, and a guardianship is between two named people.

    That said, role_assignment and access_grant share a resolver and a scope model, so the check logic is written once. See §6.

    A fifth thing that looks like a channel but is not: the membership gate. Whether a channel applies at all can depend on whether the person is currently a paid-up member (D-039). That is not a source of authority — it is a condition on channels 1 and 3, resolved inside the same function. Channels 2 and 4 are exempt. See §6.1.

    2. Scope authority — no downward inheritance¶

    This section states the rule that most shapes the permission system.

    Authority over a unit originates at that unit. It cannot be claimed from outside it.

    A parent unit — including the organization itself — cannot decide permissions inside a sub-unit. The root does not reach down into a pack; a pack does not reach down into the groups under it. If a sub-unit wants a higher-level role to have authority over it, the sub-unit must state so explicitly. Nothing is inherited, nothing is assumed, and there is no configuration flag that enables inheritance.

    The only exception is system_admin, which is organization-wide by definition and by design not scoped to any unit.

    What the rule does not say, and this is the part that changed. It does not say the root cannot act on the root's own data. Memberships, the person registry and the fees belong to the root unit, because that is where they live (D-035). An organization-scoped registrar processing a membership is working at their own scope, not reaching downward, and no exposure is involved. What a sub-unit owns is its own records — its events, its rosters, its local leadership — and there the rule is absolute.

    This is the inverse of the usual hierarchical RBAC model, and it is a deliberate organizational stance rather than a technical preference. The reasoning below is worth preserving, because the "simplification" of adding inheritance back will always look attractive and would silently reverse the policy.

    2.1 Why this rule exists¶

    It is a safeguarding property. In a youth organization, the authority that matters most is authority over children's records. The rule ensures that a person's access to a sub-unit's participants is granted by that sub-unit — the people who know them, who recruited the leader, and who can be held responsible for the grant. Someone at the root cannot grant themselves access to a pack's children unilaterally. That is precisely the property a safeguarding review wants to be able to confirm.

    It makes every grant attributable to a locus of accountability. With inheritance, "who gave this person access to this pack?" has the answer "someone at the root, three years ago, and possibly by accident." Without it, the answer is always a specific unit and a specific person, because the grant had to be written at that unit.

    It prevents silent blast-radius growth. With downward inheritance, adding a subordinate unit to the hierarchy silently expands the permissions of every role above it. A restructure — which in this organization happens every few years — would quietly grant people access to sub-units they have never heard of. Under this rule, restructuring changes nothing about who can do what.

    It matches how the organization actually delegates. Real delegation in volunteer organizations runs up: a pack decides to let a more senior treasurer handle its finances, and says so. It does not run down from a policy nobody at the pack ever agreed to.

    It survives restructuring completely, thanks to D-036. Because a unit never changes parent, no grant's meaning can shift under it. A reparent would move a unit in or out of every ancestor's reach, silently re-scoping rows nobody edited — and that is now impossible by construction, not merely discouraged.

    2.2 What this means concretely¶

    Question With inheritance (rejected) Under this rule
    The root's treasurer acts on a pack's finances Automatic Only if that pack exposed its scope — but the pack's finances are not the pack's: fees live at the root (D-035)
    A new pack added under the root Root roles automatically gain access Nothing changes. Access is unchanged until the pack says otherwise.
    The root's registrar edits a person's record Automatic Yes, and legitimately — the person registry is the root's own data
    The root's registrar edits a pack's event Automatic Only if that pack exposed its scope to the registrar role
    system_admin — Full authority everywhere. The sole exception.

    The rows that were uncomfortable are now two different rows, and that is the whole effect of D-035. Memberships, people, and fees belong to the root, so an organization-scoped registrar or treasurer acting on them is not reaching into anything — that is their own data at their own scope. Events, rosters and local leadership belong to the sub-unit, and there the rule bites exactly as before: the sub-unit must expose its scope, or the work goes through system_admin. §2.4 discusses what remains.

    2.3 scope_exposure — how up-delegation is recorded¶

    Authority crosses a unit boundary downward through exactly one mechanism: an explicit row created at the subordinate unit's level, granting a specific role assignment coverage over that unit.

    A row names the unit — org_unit_id, the unit granting coverage over itself — the role_assignment_id whose authority is to be honoured there, the person who wrote it (exposed_by, who must be authorized at that unit) and when (exposed_at), an exposure_reason, and the period it holds for.

    The full field list is in ../data-model/05-authorization.md. The three properties that matter:

    1. The row is keyed on the unit being covered, and it is written at that unit. The unit is the grantor. A parent cannot create it — see §2.5.
    2. It points at a specific role assignment, not at a role. Exposing a unit's scope to "the treasurer role" would let any future treasurer inherit access without the unit knowing. Pointing at the assignment means the unit approves a person in a role, which is what a unit can actually reason about.
    3. It is temporal and revocable, like every other grant. A unit can withdraw coverage without touching the parent's role assignment.

    There is no bulk tool (D-045). An earlier version of this design included one — a person authorized at a parent unit would select sub-units and the tool would write an exposure row for each, marked bulk:. It was removed, for two reasons. It wrote rows a sub-unit had not authored, which is a real weakening of the rule the section is about. And on inspection, the case it was actually built for turned out to be a different problem: not "let the treasurer serve forty sub-units" but "tell everyone about this event" — which is what audiences (D-042) now express directly, without touching the exposure mechanism at all (§6.2).

    What remains is the honest case. A sub-unit that wants the root's treasurer to keep its books writes one exposure row naming that assignment: a deliberate, dated, revocable act, which is exactly what the rule exists to produce. Forty of them is forty deliberate acts, and that is not drudgery — it is the auditability.

    2.4 The organization-wide role problem, and why it mostly dissolved¶

    The rule used to create a real tension. D-035 largely removes it, and it is worth seeing why rather than assuming the tension is still there:

    If the organization cannot reach down into units, how does a national registrar process memberships for a unit that has not exposed its scope?

    Because the registrar is not reaching into a unit. Memberships are held at the root unit, which is the organization (D-035). The registrar processing a membership is working on the organization's own records, at the scope they hold. No exposure is needed and none should be.

    The same is true of the treasurer and the fee data, and of anyone maintaining the person registry. The problem existed only while "the organization" and "the units holding the data" were assumed to be different entities.

    What genuinely remains is narrower: the root does not own a pack's events, rosters or local leadership. So the question becomes:

    May the root read what happens in its sub-units, or must each sub-unit expose its scope?

    Answered: the sub-unit exposes its scope, and there is no other route (D-049). Every crossing is a written, attributed act by the unit that owns the data.

    No reporting channel outside the permission model is built, and this was a deliberate refusal. A push-upward aggregate channel was the answer most likely to preserve the rule while still answering "how many members in 2019?" — but it turns out not to be needed, because the root already owns the memberships. The question that motivated it is answered from the root's own table.

    The split is worth stating plainly, because it is what makes the strict answer affordable:

    The root's own records — no exposure needed A unit's own records — exposure required
    How many members in 2019 Who was in the 4th Pack in 1998
    Who is currently a member Which events a pack ran
    What fees are outstanding Who leads a pack
    Was this person a member on this date A pack's roster and attendance

    Memberships and fees are held at the root unit, which is the organization (D-035). A unit's internal life is the unit's.

    What must not happen is a background process that quietly treats root-scoped roles as universal because "otherwise the office can't do its job." That converts a deliberate policy into an inconsistency, and the inconsistency is where access bugs live. The specific shape this takes in this system is worth naming: registrar holds unit_participation.view at organization scope, and that does not let it read a single pack roster. Scope is exact — the role reads the root participation rows, which are the membership rows (D-041). It looks like an oversight and is not (appendix/permission-catalog.md §11).

    Note for the real organization: if it turns out that the majority of sub-units are writing exposures to the same organization-wide roles, that is a signal the rule does not match how the organization works — and it should be revisited as a policy decision, recorded in ../decisions/decision-log.md, rather than worked around in code. The signal is legible precisely because there is no side channel that would hide it: every crossing is a row someone wrote.

    2.5 Enforcing the direction of authority¶

    The rule is only as good as its enforcement. Three layers:

    Layer 1 — the permission that gates exposure. Creating a scope_exposure requires scope.expose, held at the unit being exposed.

    Note the scope: the unit being covered, not the actor's own unit. Someone holding scope.expose at a parent unit cannot satisfy this for a sub-unit, because they hold nothing at the sub-unit. That one detail of the wording is what makes the rule structural rather than advisory.

    Layer 2 — a check that rejects exposures created from above, defense in depth against a bug or a write that bypasses the ordinary path. It runs before a row is written, once per row, on both a new row and a change to an existing one, and it does this in this order:

    1. Apply the rule, with no exceptions: the person writing the row must hold scope.expose at the unit being covered. Holding it at an ancestor, or at an unrelated unit, does not qualify.
    2. Ask the same question layer 1 asks, rather than a weaker copy of it — that is the only reason this layer exists. See the two notes below.
    3. There is no bulk branch. The tool that wrote rows on a unit's behalf was removed (D-045) once audiences (D-042) covered the case it existed for.
    4. If the answer is no, refuse the row and name both the person and the unit in the refusal — a rejection that does not say who and where is not diagnosable. Otherwise the row stands.

    Two things about this check are deliberate:

    • It is now a single unconditional predicate. It used to have a second branch permitting a bulk: prefixed row written by an ancestor — the design's one sanctioned exception. That branch is gone (D-045), and its removal is what makes this function something a reviewer can hold in their head. Every exposure row is written by the unit it covers, with no marker to explain away.
    • It was layer 2, and layer 2 is now missing. Until D-072 this check ran a second time inside the database, so that a migration, a data fix, or a future code path that bypassed the write path still could not invert the direction of authority. The write-path permission check is now the only thing standing between a caller and an upside-down exposure — it cannot be a constraint (the answer comes from the resolver), and there is no longer a second copy of it below the application. Two consequences, both load-bearing: it must run inside the same transaction as the row, so that "checked, then written" is not two moments in time; and layer 3 below stops being a backstop and becomes the only detector.

    Layer 3 — the nightly integrity check in ../data-model/05-authorization.md, which reports (a) any exposure whose granting assignment sits at an ancestor of the covered unit, and (b) any exposure whose actor does not hold scope.expose at the covered unit. Layer 3 is now the only detection, not the second line of it — it catches what the write-path check missed, inverted, or was bypassed by, and nothing else will. That makes it the one invariant in the authorization model worth alerting on rather than reporting: an inverted exposure is authority flowing in the direction the founding principle forbids, and it is invisible in the UI, which will show the exposure as a legitimate row either way.

    2.6 system_admin is the only exception¶

    system_admin is deliberately outside this rule, and deliberately so in a way that is visible rather than implicit:

    • It is organization-scoped and unscoped simultaneously — it satisfies every check at every scope without needing an exposure row.
    • It contains every permission, so concerns about individual unit permissions are largely moot for a system administrator.
    • Sub-permissions (system.permission.edit, report.*, and similar) also resolve under system_admin wherever the check is made.

    Two properties keep this exception from being the hole in the design:

    1. Its holders are alarmed on. The integrity check that lists current system_admin holders should notify on every change — a new holder is either a planned act or an incident, and either way someone should know within the hour.
    2. It is not delegatable. role.is_delegatable = false for system_admin, so it cannot be lent, which prevents it becoming the routine workaround.

    The exception is safe only because it is narrow, alarmed, and undelegatable. If system_admin becomes the mechanism by which routine organization-wide work is done, the rule in §2.1 has been defeated in practice even though it holds in the schema. Watch the access log for its use — see §9.

    2.7 What this replaces¶

    Two things previously stated in this document are reversed by this section:

    Previously Now
    "Authority inherits downward through the unit hierarchy" It does not. scope_exposure only, and only upward-delegated.
    The resolver expanded unit scope via unit_ancestors() The resolver matches scope exactly, plus explicit exposures. No ancestor walk.

    unit_ancestors() remains in the schema and is still used for reporting and for the exposure-direction check (§2.5) — but never for permission resolution. Anywhere it appears in a permission path is a bug.

    Both reversals are recorded in ../decisions/decision-log.md, with the reasoning, so they are not re-litigated.

    3. Permissions — the atomic unit¶

    A permission is a coded capability. It is the only thing the check function ever looks at.

    A permission row carries its code, the resource and action it decomposes into, a description, and is_system — set for the permissions the platform ships with, which therefore cannot be deleted.

    Codes are resource.action and are stable identifiers, never display strings. The UI shows a human label; the code never changes. Renaming a permission code is a breaking change and must be avoided.

    Naming convention¶

    Pattern Meaning Example
    <resource>.view Read person.view
    <resource>.create Create event.create
    <resource>.<field>.edit Edit a field subset person.contact.edit
    <resource>.delete Delete (soft, and audited) document.delete

    There is no row here for acting on a supervised person, because there is no dependent.* namespace to name (D-074). A guardian's act is checked under the ordinary code for the thing being touched — person.view, person.address.edit — and reaches the guardianship channel by the shape of the request, not by its name (§6, and D-079).

    Every capability is narrow and explicit. A check for X.y needs an exact grant of X.y, or system.admin. There is no <resource>.admin wildcard and no prefix match in the resolver (D-068, reversing the earlier convention) — which is what makes the next section load-bearing rather than decorative: if somebody can do everything about events, a role says so, in thirteen rows, and you can read them.

    Do not confuse this with the admin grant level in §5, which is real and untouched: the level says how much authority a grant carries, the deleted code said which capabilities it implied.

    4. Roles — bundles of permissions¶

    A role is a named set of permissions, assigned to a person for a period, within a scope.

    A role row is a code, a name, a description, is_system, and scope_required — whether an assignment of this role has to name a scope. role_permission is a join entity and nothing more: this role carries this permission.

    An assignment joins a person to a role for a period — valid_from, valid_to — and records who created it (granted_by, granted_at) and why (grant_reason). delegation_of is absent on an ordinary assignment and set on one that was lent, naming what it came from (§9).

    Scope¶

    scope_type/scope_id is what makes "treasurer of Pack 12" and "treasurer of the organization" the same row shape. It also means adding a new scope kind later (an event series, a committee) is a data change, not a schema change.

    Authority does not inherit downward. See §2. A role at a parent unit does not cover the sub-units beneath it unless each of those has explicitly exposed its scope to it — with the single important nuance that the membership registry and the fees belong to the root unit, so an organization-scoped role over them is not reaching downward at all.

    Seed roles (a starting shape, not a specification)¶

    Roles are data. The organization creates them, composes them from permissions, assigns them, edits what they grant and deletes the unused ones (D-052, D-053). This means two things about the table below, and both are corrections to how this section read before:

    1. It is a worked example of composing permissions into permission groups, included so the model can be reviewed against something concrete. It is not a requirement list.
    2. The Req. and Waives gate columns are configuration, not specification. They are attributes of roles, and an organization sets them in the admin UI. The values shown are reasoned defaults; only system_admin carrying system.gate_exempt is not a preference, because the 1 January lockout it prevents is a property of the system.

    A person may hold several roles at once — a leader can hold a leadership role and a role carrying administrative permissions; a counselor may hold a role carrying event creation and person.view. A role is a bundle, and bundles compose.

    The Req. column is role.requires_membership — whether the role stops working when the holder's fee lapses (D-039). It remains the blunt form of the gate's waiver; system.gate_exempt is the precise form.

    Role Scope Req. Waives gate Key permissions
    system_admin organization — yes all, plus system.*. Holds system.gate_exempt: the 1 January sweep lapses every membership including this one, and the recovery path must work on the day it is needed.
    registrar organization yes no membership.request.*, person.*, unit_participation.view (root rows — see the note below the table)
    treasurer organization or unit yes no fee.*, payment.*, report.finance.view
    unit_leader org_unit no no event.* for own unit, unit_participation.view, unit_report.view
    assistant_leader org_unit no no subset of unit_leader
    unit_admin org_unit yes no administrative management of a sub-unit and its children
    audience_admin organization or unit yes no audience.view, audience.manage — who maintains the named groups

    Req. and Waives gate are not alternatives. Req. is role.requires_membership — "is this role membership-dependent?". Waives gate is whether the role carries the person-level system.gate_exempt permission — "does holding this role make me exempt everywhere?" (D-051). Which roles carry it beyond system_admin is open for your review — the mechanism is settled, the list is data.

    Why the two leader roles are marked no. They are held by adult volunteers, and the organization's answer was "everything except guardianship and adult volunteers" — a volunteer whose own fee is unpaid still runs the Thursday meeting, and losing the roster the night the fee lapses is the failure mode this flag exists to prevent. Note the asymmetry with position.requires_membership, which stays true for those same positions and puts the volunteer on a chase-up report. The report is how the organization notices; the resolver is not how it punishes. See data-model/02 §7.1.

    Why registrar and treasurer are marked yes. Both are staff-like roles working on the organization's own records at the root. A lapsed member should not be processing other people's memberships or fees, and there is no operational reason for the exemption.

    registrar reading unit_participation.view at organization scope does not let it read a pack roster (D-049). Scope is an exact match, so the role reads the root participation rows — the ones that mirror membership. A sub-unit roster is a different scope and requires that sub-unit to expose it. This looks like a mistake and is not; widening the role to "fix" it would quietly reverse D-049.

    Organization-scoped roles do not reach into sub-units. A registrar assigned at organization scope has authority over organization-level records — which now includes the membership registry and the fees, because those live at the root (D-035). Authority over a sub-unit's own records — its events, its local rosters — requires that sub-unit exposing its scope to the assignment (§2.3). There is no other route, and system_admin is not one: it remains the stated emergency exception, alarmed and logged (§2.6), not a way to read a roster on a Tuesday. See §2.4.

    The role assignment period matters¶

    valid_from/valid_to on role_assignment means authority ends on a date even if nobody remembers to revoke it. Every assignment of a role with real authority should have an end date. Leavers are the classic audit failure: the volunteer who stepped down in 2019 still has an active account with treasurer access in 2026. A default term (e.g. one organizational year, renewed explicitly) turns that from an invisible risk into a visible expiry report.

    Recommendation: require valid_to on all roles flagged scope_required = true, and produce a periodic report of assignments expiring in the next 60 days. Tracked in ../open-questions.md.

    5. Access grants — the per-item layer¶

    This is the "per-item permission" you asked for, and you specified it means a capability on that item: "person X may act on item Y". Not a statement of attendance, and not a separate eligibility check — those are other tables.

    A grant names its grantee, the item it is about, and how much. The grantee is a person_id or an audience_id. The item is a scope_type — event, document, org_unit, membership, and whatever is invented next — and a scope_id naming the concrete row. The level is one of the five below, the period is valid_from/valid_to, and the provenance is is_delegated with delegated_from naming the person whose authority a delegation came from, plus granted_by, granted_at and grant_reason.

    person_id or audience_id, never both and never neither. The audience form is what makes "expose this event to everyone" expressible without writing a row per person (D-042) — see §6.2 for how it resolves.

    Levels¶

    Ordered, cumulative — a higher level always subsumes the lower ones:

    Level Numeric Means
    revoked 0 Explicitly denied. Overrides any other grant — see §7.
    view 10 Read the item
    contribute 20 Read and add to it (sign up, comment, submit)
    manage 30 Full control short of permissions administration
    admin 40 Everything, including granting others access to this item

    revoked at 0 is important: without an explicit deny level there is no way to say "this person must not see this event" when a broader grant would otherwise allow it.

    Why scope_type/scope_id and not one column per item type¶

    scope_id has no foreign key — it is a polymorphic reference. This is a deliberate, accepted trade-off:

    • Cost: no referential integrity. A grant can point at a deleted event.
    • Benefit: the permission system does not need a schema change every time a new grantable thing is invented, which — since you have deferred events, documents, and everything else — will happen repeatedly.

    Mitigations, all of which are required:

    1. A scope_type registry table validates scope_type values.
    2. Deleting an item of a granted type must close its grants (an application-level obligation, tested).
    3. A nightly consistency job reports orphaned grants.
    4. scope_id is BIGINT and ids are never reused.

    If you would rather have strict referential integrity, the alternative is a grantable_item supertable that every grantable entity references. That is a heavier design; I have chosen polymorphism for flexibility, and flagged it here so you can overrule it. See ../open-questions.md.

    6. The resolver¶

    One function answers every authorization question. Written once, used everywhere — an authorization rule that exists in two places will diverge.

    The call is can(person, permission_code, scope_type, scope_id, at). The scope pair is optional and at defaults to now. Where the thing being touched has an owner, the caller passes owner_person_id too, because two of the steps below need to know whose record this is. The answer is allowed or denied; the function has no other output, and no part of it is stored.

    These steps run in this order, and the order is a rule (D-032). Each one either returns an answer or falls through, so no later step can contradict an earlier one — and renumbering them silently changes who can do what.

    1. Deny — absolute, at any scope. If any applicable grant for this person carries level revoked and covers this permission — at item scope, unit scope or organization scope, to this person or to an audience they are in — the answer is no. No scope outranks a deny: a per-event grant can never restore access that an organization-wide suspension removed (§7).

    2. Ownership. If the target resource's owner is this person → true for *.view, *.edit and the self-service actions. Exempt from the membership gate (§6.1): a lapsed person must still be able to reach the screen they renew on.

    3. Guardianship — entered by the shape of the request, and it answers with allows only. Compare the actor with the owner of the thing being touched. The same person, and this channel is not in play at all. Different people, and the question is whether the actor holds a live guardianship over that subject as of at whose right level is at least what the checked permission needs — view for a read, write for a change, and none for nothing (D-074). A row at a sufficient level → true. Every other outcome — no row, none, a level too low — is not a refusal: the channel says nothing, and the resolver continues to step 3, where the actor's own grants and roles are tried exactly as they always were (D-079). A parent who is also the event registrar holds view over their own child and must not be refused the roster by their own guardianship.

    4. Nothing escapes by naming. The act that routes here is chosen by whose record it touches, so a permission written next year for a field a guardian must edit is covered the day the field exists. Under the rule this replaces, dispatch read the code's prefix — and an ordinary person.address.edit slipped past it.

    5. An ended guardianship never opens this channel again. What a former guardian may still do is what any other member may do, through their own assignment.
    6. The row is the whole test. The guard must not be rewritten as "the subject is a minor": the resolver contains no age branch, and D-070 and D-077 keep it that way.

    A genuine prohibition is still available and still absolute — it is step 0, which runs before anything and does not care about scope. This step is exempt from the membership gate (§6.1): guardianship is a legal relationship, not a membership. See ../data-model/04-relationships.md.

    1. Allow — the most specific scope determines the level. Evaluate from the most local scope outward. The first tier that yields a determination decides this permission's effective level for this check:

    2. a. Item grant — an access_grant on this exact (scope_type, scope_id).

    3. b. Unit grant — an access_grant on this org_unit, matched exactly. An exposure never widens a direct grant: it names one person's role assignment, and a grant carries no assignment for it to attach to (D-078).
    4. c. Org grant — an access_grant with scope_type = 'organization'.
    5. d. Role — a role_assignment at the most specific applicable scope: the exact unit, a unit that has written an exposure row naming that assignment, or the organization. This tier is the only consumer of scope_exposure, and the membership gate (§6.1) applies to tier d alone.

    If the deciding tier's level satisfies the permission's required level (per permission_level) → true. Otherwise fall through to the next tier.

    1. Otherwise → false.

    Points worth stressing:

    • Default deny. Step 4. Any new resource starts with nobody able to touch it.
    • Deny is absolute and is the only thing that outranks locality. A safeguarding suspension is not overridable by a more local grant — that is the entire reason revoked sits outside the tier ordering. See §7.
    • Locality decides between allows. The most specific scope sets the level. This is what "local permissions override global" means: a per-event manage grant gives you manage on that event even if your only unit-level grant is view, and the unit-level view still applies to the unit's other events.
    • No ancestor walk, and no widening of grants. Step 3b matches the checked unit and nothing else; an exposure enters at step 3d only, and only for the single assignment it names (D-078). unit_ancestors() must not appear anywhere in this path. See §2.
    • A grant may name an audience instead of a person. "Everyone", "all members", "the leaders of this unit" — the grantee is resolved by a rule, the level and scope are resolved exactly as for a personal grant. See §6.2.
    • Effective permissions are never stored. They are computed per request. A stored permission snapshot will drift from the grants that produced it — the only question is how long before anyone notices.
    • Time is a parameter. at defaults to now, but the resolver accepts a historical time. This enables "could this person have done this on that date?" — needed for auditing past actions, and free if built in from the start.

    6.1 The membership gate¶

    A role grants nothing while its holder's membership has lapsed (D-039). This is a condition on tier d, not a source of authority — it is what makes the resolver's answer time-sensitive rather than merely scope-sensitive.

    Concretely — inside tier d, each candidate role assignment counts only if one of three things holds: the role does not require membership, which is the carve-out for this role; the person is a current member as of the date being checked, which is the gate; or the person holds system.gate_exempt, which is the carve-out for this person. One of the three is enough, and none of them is a source of authority — they say only whether the role's authority still applies.

    Channel Gated? Why
    Ownership (step 1) No Otherwise a lapse locks a person out of the screen they renew on
    Guardianship (step 2) No A legal relationship, not a membership
    Item / unit / org grants (tiers a–c) No An explicit act by someone who chose to give this person access. Not inherited, not implied.
    Roles (tier d) Yes, unless the role or the person opts out The role was granted because the person was a member; the justification has gone

    Two ways to opt out, and they answer different questions (D-051):

    role.requires_membership = false system.gate_exempt (a permission)
    Question Is this role membership-dependent? Is this person exempt?
    Granularity the role the person
    Effect the role grants regardless of membership every gated role that person holds is honoured
    Example a volunteer-facing role that would break the evening if it stopped working the system administrator, who must not be locked out by the sweep they are expected to run

    system.gate_exempt must not be evaluated through can(). Doing so is circular — the resolver would have to apply the gate in order to find out whether the gate applies — and a lapsed holder would be denied the permission whose only purpose is to say that a lapse does not matter. It is a plain read of role_assignment, and it is the one place the resolver touches the role tables without consulting itself. Because it is a system-namespace permission, it can never arrive through an access_grant: an item-level grant cannot lift the gate, structurally.

    Why the gate reaches tier d and stops there. The distinction is how the authority arose. A role is granted to a member qua member — a leader's authority is a consequence of belonging. An access_grant is a deliberate, per-item act: someone decided that this person should see this event, and a lapsed fee does not undo that decision. Gating grants too would make the per-item layer unusable for exactly the people it exists for — a contractor, a photographer, a parent helper who was never a member at all.

    The cost, stated plainly. This is a real coupling:

    • A lapse silently removes access. The intent, but "why can't I see this?" now has a date for an answer rather than a person's decision.
    • Every membership transition must invalidate the cache, and the yearly sweep does it for everyone at once. Build the cache for this from the start (§8).
    • role.requires_membership is now enforced, not merely reported. Its meaning changed; see data-model/05 §3.

    6.2 Audience grants¶

    A grant names a person or an audience, never both (D-042):

    Kind How it resolves Example
    Built-in A rule the resolver evaluates against current state everyone, all_members, unit:<id>:participants, unit:<id>:leaders
    Named group A stored membership list someone administers "the summer camp kitchen crew"

    Built-in audiences are rules, not lists, and that is the whole point: a new pack member automatically receives what the pack was granted, with no rows to maintain and no stale-group problem. Named groups are the escape hatch, and they carry that problem knowingly — nobody ever removes anyone from a group.

    Audiences are evaluated inside tiers a–c, through the same grant_satisfies() predicate personal grants use. They do not add a channel and they do not change the level or the scope resolution; they change only how "is this grant addressed to me?" is answered.

    Permission → required level mapping¶

    The mapping lives in permission_level, which pairs a permission with the level it needs, held per scope_type.

    Example: event.view requires level view; event.edit requires contribute; event.manage requires manage; event.grant_access requires admin. This table is what lets one access_grant level serve many different permission codes without a hardcoded mapping in code.

    7. Precedence¶

    Two separate questions, answered by two separate rules. Conflating them is the mistake this section exists to prevent.

    7.1 Who decides — deny is absolute¶

    1. Explicit deny  (level = 'revoked', at ANY scope)   ← WINS OVER EVERYTHING
2. Ownership     (it's your own row — gate-exempt)
3. Guardianship  (the act is about someone else — allows only, gate-exempt)
4. Allows        (see §7.2; the membership gate applies to the role tier only, §6.1)
5. Deny          (nothing matched)

    A deny is checked before locality and is never subject to it. A revoked grant at organization scope blocks access to a unit's event even if a per-event grant explicitly allows it. There is no scope local enough to override a suspension.

    A deny is also never subject to the membership gate — in either direction. A revoked grant blocks someone who is not a member, which is obvious. What is worth stating: a revoked grant to an audience blocks the members of that audience whether or not they are current members, because the deny is evaluated against who the audience rule matches, not against the gate. Suspensions are absolute; the gate is a condition on a grant of authority, and there is no authority here to condition.

    This answers the question directly: when does a local allow lose? Only when any deny applies at any level. A safeguarding suspension, a court-ordered restriction, or an emergency removal must not be defeatable by a grant someone created earlier and forgot about — which is exactly what a "most specific always wins" rule would permit.

    7.2 How the level is decided — locality¶

    Among competing allows, the most specific scope determines the effective level:

    1. Item grant    (access_grant on this exact item)              ← most specific
2. Unit grant    (access_grant on this exact unit — matched exactly.
                  An exposure never widens a grant)
3. Org grant     (access_grant, scope_type = 'organization')
4. Role          (role_assignment at the most specific applicable
                  scope, or at a unit that has exposed its scope to
                  that assignment)                              ← least specific

    The first tier that yields a determination decides the level; if that level is insufficient for the permission being checked, the next tier still applies. So a per-event view grant does not downgrade you below a unit-level manage for other purposes — it decides the level for that event, and the broader grants continue to govern everything else.

    Consequences worth being explicit about:

    • A unit leader whose role grants event.manage on their unit still gets manage on a new event — the item tier is empty, so the unit tier decides.
    • A specific view grant on one document works even for someone with no organizational role at all — the case for an external contractor or a parent photographer.
    • A per-event manage grant gives full control of that event even to someone whose unit role is only view. This is the user's requirement — local overrides global — and it applies to allows. It does not apply to a deny: a revoked row anywhere beats this manage grant (§7.1, D-032).
    • Locality orders grants; it does not create authority. An item grant on a unit you have no relationship to is still a valid grant — but creating it required access_grant.create at that item with admin level (§5), which is where the authority to make it came from. Contrast §2: the right to hand out authority at a unit lives at that unit, but the right to delegate a specific grant comes from the level held on the item.

    8. Caching and invalidation¶

    Permission checks happen on every request. Compute cheaply, cache carefully.

    Cache key must include everything the answer depends on:

    (person_id, permission_code, scope_type, scope_id, at_bucket)

    where at_bucket truncates the timestamp to a granularity shorter than the shortest grant duration you allow. If grants can start/end at an arbitrary second, a time-bucketed cache is unsafe — use now() and a short TTL instead.

    Invalidate on:

    • any write to role_assignment, access_grant, guardianship_permission, role_permission, audience, audience_member
    • every membership transition, including the yearly expiry sweep — the gate in §6.1 means a lapse changes the resolver's answer for a whole person, so invalidate by person_id, not by scope. The sweep touches everyone at once, so this must be a bulk operation the cache is designed for, not one delete per person in a loop.
    • a merge — this is the one that gets missed. Merging two people unions their permissions, so the survivor's cache must be cleared. See 03-identity-and-legacy.md.
    • a unit's parent changing — retired, because this can no longer happen (D-036). It previously affected reporting and the exposure-direction check but not permission resolution (§2), so its removal costs nothing.
    • a write to scope_exposure (a sub-unit exposing or withdrawing coverage over itself)

    A note on the gate and TTL. Because access now changes on a date rather than on someone's action, the cache TTL is a policy choice about how long a lapsed member keeps access. The sweep invalidates immediately; the TTL governs the window for transitions that are not the sweep. Keep the TTL short and measure.

    Alternative worth considering: materialize effective_permission as a table refreshed on write rather than cached per-request. Faster reads, but it is a denormalization and it must be maintained transactionally — if a refresh fails silently you have an access bug that nothing detects. If you take this path, add a verification job that recomputes and compares. Recommendation: start with cache-only, add materialization only if measurement demands it.

    9. Delegation¶

    A leader going on leave, a treasurer handing over during an audit — the common need to lend authority temporarily.

    A delegation is an ordinary access_grant with its provenance set: is_delegated, and delegated_from naming the person whose authority is being lent, a grant_reason saying what for, and a valid_to.

    Rules:

    • A delegation always has an end date. Enforce with a constraint.
    • Delegation cannot exceed the delegator's own authority — validate at grant time that every permission being delegated is one the delegator currently holds, and re-check at use time, because the delegator may lose it mid-delegation.
    • The provenance is recorded, so an audit can answer "who really had this authority on that date?" — it lists both the holder and the delegator.
    • Delegation of system_admin should be prohibited outright.

    10. Anti-patterns this design rejects¶

    Stated explicitly, because each is a common and expensive mistake:

    Rejected Why Instead
    person.is_admin boolean Cannot scope, cannot date, cannot audit, cannot delegate role_assignment
    Role on person (person.role = 'leader') One role per person, no scope, no history role_assignment, many per person
    Checking membership type in code (if type == 'adult') Rules change; the check is invisible and untestable A permission, granted to a role. There is one membership type now, so this is doubly wrong
    Deriving access from "is a member" without a role Being a member is not a capability; it is a fact The membership gate (§6.1) conditions roles — it never grants anything
    A parent-unit actor writing a scope_exposure covering a sub-unit A sub-unit's records are not the root's records. A sanctioned bulk shortcut did exist for this and was removed (D-045) The sub-unit exposes its own scope, or an audience grant covers the "tell everyone" case (§6.2)
    Role → permission mapping hardcoded in the app Cannot be reviewed by the organization, cannot change without deployment role_permission table
    Deriving access from guardianship without a right Authorization by identity is unauditable and unsafe The explicit right_level on the guardianship row
    Deleting a grant to revoke it Loses the audit trail of what was revoked when valid_to + revoked_at
    Two authorization implementations They diverge; the weaker one becomes the exploit One resolver, §6
    Unit leader sees all member data by default Fails safeguarding review Scoped role permissions, resolved per operation
    Downward inheritance of authority through the unit tree A parent unit decides permissions over nested units; restructuring silently changes who can do what scope_exposure rows written at the covered unit — §2
    "Most specific scope always wins", including against a deny Makes a safeguarding suspension defeatable by a stale local grant Deny is absolute; locality orders allows only — §7
    Treating an organization-scoped role as universal "so the registrar can work" Converts a deliberate policy into an inconsistency; the inconsistency is where access bugs live Exposure rows, or system_admin — §2.4

    11. Open issues¶

    • The complete permission catalog and the real role definitions — the starting set in §4 is a proposal awaiting your input. In particular the Req. column: which roles are marked requires_membership = false decides who keeps working when their fee lapses, and it needs a deliberate answer per role rather than a default (§4, §6.1).
    • Whether role assignments must have end dates (§4). This matters more now — an open-ended role held by a lapsed member is a "dormant authority" case (§6.1).
    • May the root read what happens in its sub-units, or must each sub-unit expose its scope? The organization-wide-role problem largely dissolved (D-035), because the root owns the registry and the fees; what remains is this narrower question (§2.4).
    • Which built-in audiences are needed, and whether the parameterised ones are seeded or created on demand (§6.2).
    • The cache TTL under the membership gate (§8) — a policy choice about how long a lapsed member keeps access.
    • Whether the membership gate should also apply to item grants, or only to roles (§6.1). The design says roles only, on the grounds that a grant is an explicit act — but a unit that hands an event to an unpaid helper may not see it that way.

    Resolved and no longer open, kept visible so the reasoning survives: whether any permission is conditioned on membership being current (yes, with three carve-outs — D-039, §6.1); polymorphic scope_id (confirmed — §5, F-2); and the bulk exposure tool (removed — D-045, §2.5).
