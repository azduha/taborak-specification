    Authorization Model¶

    The permission system end to end. This is the document to read if you read only one.

    Round 10 rewrote this page. D-097 deleted the unit hierarchy, D-099 the level ladder, D-100 the membership gate and positions, D-101 the audience's rule/list/inclusion arms, and D-102 event ownership. What is left is smaller than what was there, and every claim that died with them is marked where it used to be read, because why it existed is the part that stops coming back.

    The whole model, stated once.

    Scopes. There are two. The unit — the organization, of which there is exactly one, and which is a scope and never a row — and one item: an event, a group, a role, later a document. A grant at the unit reaches every item of every type; a grant on an item reaches that item and nothing else. There is no tier between them and nothing to test.

    A grant is: a grantee (a person or an audience) + a scope + one permission code + an effect of allow or deny + a period.

    The answer. Allowed when some live allow applies and no live deny applies. There is no level, nothing is inherited downward, and no nearer grant beats a further one — because once the answer is boolean, there is nothing left to order.

    Roles and groups are not sources of authority. They are named sets of people that a grant aims at.

    1. The three channels of authority¶

    Every question of the form "may person P do X?" is answered by consulting three mechanisms. They are separate because they have different lifetimes, different granters, and different revocation semantics.

    # Channel Question it answers Where the answer comes from Reaches
    1 Grants What has this person, or this set of people, been given? access_grant, aimed at a person or an audience The unit, or one item
    2 Guardianship What may this person do regarding this child? guardianship.right_level compared with permission.right_kind One dependent
    3 Resource ownership Is this their own data? (derived) The row's owner

    Channel 3 is not a table. It is the rule that a person may always act on their own profile, their own requests, their own memberships. It is resolved by comparing person_id before the other channels are consulted.

    What a channel reaches once one of them says yes. Each of the three answers whether, never how much of a person: there is one read rule and one write rule over a person's record, and no narrower tier standing beside them, so a grant that reaches the record reaches all of it. What that consists of, and why there is no sensitive corner left inside it to withhold, is 08-person-data.md — worth reading before proposing a fourth channel that reaches "only" one kind of fact.

    Why the channels stay apart rather than one unified grants table. They differ in ways that matter:

    • Channel 2 has a legal character — guardianship is a fact about the world, often court-ordered, and the organization records what it was told rather than deciding it. It does not expire by itself. An earlier version of this bullet said it "expires when a child reaches majority", which is exactly the claim D-069 withdrew, together with the review queue that asserted it more quietly: a parent's authority over health and school records does not end at 18 in many jurisdictions, and in some it ends at a different age, so any date the system computed would be a claim it cannot support. A guardianship ends when a party to it says so, and nobody else — not a job, not a birthday.
    • Channel 1 is the only channel that can name a set rather than a person (D-042). A guardianship is between two named people and an owner is one person. That is a boundary, not an oversight — it is why an office can be aimed at but never held by a rule (§4, F-12).
    • A deny lives in channel 1 and defeats all three (§7.1). Nowhere else can one be written, and nowhere else can one be argued with.

    Roles and groups are not channels¶

    This is the section that used to list four channels, the fourth being role assignment, and a fifth thing — the membership gate — being described as "a condition on channels 1 and 3". Both are gone, for the same reason and a different one.

    ~~Role assignment — what may this person do in the organization? A role_assignment joined a person to a role for a period within a scope, and the role carried the permissions.~~ Withdrawn by D-100. role_assignment is now role_holder, role_permission is deleted, and a role confers nothing by itself. The codes an office carries are the live grants whose audience names ROLE(x). A role is therefore not a source of authority; it is a set that grants point at, in the same sentence as a group, an event's attendees, and "everyone". Authority still has exactly one source besides ownership and guardianship, which is D-005 said one level down: being something is not being able to do something.

    ~~The membership gate — whether a channel applies at all can depend on whether the person is currently a paid-up member (D-039); channels 2 and 4 were exempt.~~ Withdrawn by D-100. The test did not disappear, it moved into the expression: MEMBERS is a selector evaluated live on the checked date, and writing it or leaving it out is now the writer's act rather than the resolver's (§6.1). It is not a channel because it never granted anything, and it is no longer a clause in the resolver either.

    What did survive as a channel is worth naming, because both were nearly caught in the sweep. Ownership and guardianship are unaffected by D-097 through D-102 — neither was ever scoped to a unit, gated on membership, or graded by a level.

    2. Scopes — the unit and the item¶

    There are exactly two scopes.

    The unit is the organization. There is one. It is not a row, not a table, and not the top of a structure: it is the non-item scope that grant_scope_type already carries, whose code is unit, whose is_item_scoped is false, and at which a grant simply has no item to name.

    An item is exactly one thing: one event, one group, one role, and later one document. A grant on it names it.

    Reach is one-directional and there is nothing to test. A grant at the unit reaches every item of every type. A grant on one item reaches that item and nothing else. No item reaches another item, and no item reaches the unit.

    That is the complete theory of scope. There is no nesting, no path, no depth, no tier between the unit and the item, and no relation on the item — no owner, no parent, no covering body — that a check consults.

    Which codes may be granted where is data, not prose. permission.applies_to is unit or unit_and_item (D-099): event.create and membership.manage are nonsense on an event, and the column says so instead of hoping a reader agrees. It is checked when the grant is saved, not at check time — a grant of event.create written on one event is refused with the reason in the error, because an event cannot be created twice and a creation permission attached to a thing that already exists answers no question. The system namespace is unit-only for the same reason, which is who enforces now for the old rule that system.admin can never arrive through an item grant (D-081).

    2.1 The founding rule, and what is left of it¶

    This section states a rule that shaped this page for eight rounds and no longer holds. It is kept because the reasoning is the expensive part, and because someone will propose the simplification that reverses it again.

    ~~Authority over a unit originates at that unit. It cannot be claimed from outside it. A parent unit — including the organization itself — cannot decide permissions inside a sub-unit. The root does not reach down into a pack; a pack does not reach down into the groups under it. If a sub-unit wants a higher-level role to have authority over it, the sub-unit must state so explicitly. Nothing is inherited, nothing is assumed, and there is no configuration flag that enables inheritance.~~

    Withdrawn by D-097, which did not weaken the rule so much as deprive it of its subject (D-031). The rule existed to stop the center deciding permissions inside a body it does not run. With one unit there is no "outside it" to be stopped from. Every permission is held at the unit or on an item, and the unit's people are the only people there are.

    It is worth keeping why it looked right, because the argument was good:

    • It was a safeguarding property. Access to a body's children had to be granted by that body — by the people who know them, who recruited the leader, and who can be asked. This property is gone and nothing in the remaining model reproduces it. A permission held at the unit reaches every member, and person.view was already the widest read permission in the system (D-083, D-086). What is left of the safeguarding story is a permission held by a named adult, an audit trail whose payloads expire after two years (D-088), and a report of who holds the write — minus the part where the named adult was somebody the body chose. Saying this once, plainly, is the honest version of a deletion.
    • It made every grant attributable to a locus of accountability. "Who gave this person access to this pack?" used to have the answer "a specific person at a specific pack, on a specific date". Now it has the answer "a specific person at the unit, on a specific date" — still a name, no longer a nearby name.
    • It prevented silent blast-radius growth. Restructuring used to be the risk; with one unit there is nothing to restructure, so the property survives for the dull reason that its threat has no subject.
    • It matched how the organization actually delegates — up, not down. That observation was about bodies, and there are none. What replaces the need it served is the audience: "tell everyone about this event" was always the real requirement behind the delegation machinery (D-045), and it is one grant aimed at EVERYONE or MEMBERS (§6.2).

    What died with it — all of it by D-097, listed so a reader can tell whether a page they are holding is stale: org_unit and its types, names, relationships and participations; scope_exposure and the scope.expose and scope.exposure.review permissions; the direction-of-authority check and the nightly alert that was its only detector; unit_ancestors() and unit_descendants() and every ancestor walk. Who enforces the direction of authority now: nobody, because there is no direction. Not "the application instead of the database" — the question cannot be asked (D-081).

    2.2 The questions people actually ask, before and now¶

    Question What this page used to answer What it answers now
    May the unit's treasurer act on a group's finances? Only if that body exposed its scope A unit grant reaches every group. Nothing to ask, nothing to write
    May registrar read a person record? Yes, because the registry lives at the root Yes, because it is a unit grant. Same answer, one fewer step of reasoning
    May a helper see one event? A per-item grant at view A grant of event.view on that event. The same row, minus the level
    May the center read what happens inside a body? No — exposure was the only crossing (D-049) There are no bodies. The question is now "may this person see these people?", which is a person.view grant with a name on it
    Who owns an event? Exactly one unit (D-058) Nobody. event.org_unit_id is deleted; the people connected to an event are named by selectors that bind to it (§6.2) — D-102
    Who was in the Thursday group in 2011? A point-in-time read over a unit roster A point-in-time read over person_group_member — dated rows, same answer (D-098)

    2.3 system.admin¶

    system.admin is the one wildcard permission: a check for X.y is satisfied by an exact grant of X.y or by system.admin and nothing else (D-068). It used to be described as "the sole exception to the no-downward-inheritance rule", and that framing went with the rule — there is no boundary for it to cross.

    Two properties keep a wildcard from being the hole in the design, and both stand on their own:

    1. Its holders are alarmed on. The report listing current holders must notify on every change — a new holder is either a planned act or an incident, and either way someone should know within the hour.
    2. It is not delegatable (§9), and it is applies_to = unit, so it cannot arrive on an item.

    If system.admin becomes the mechanism by which ordinary organization-wide work is done, that is a signal about the catalog rather than about a boundary rule: the work is telling you it needs a permission that does not exist. Record it in ../decisions/decision-log.md instead of working around it.

    3. Permissions — the atomic unit¶

    A permission is a coded capability. It is the only thing the check function ever looks at.

    A permission row carries its code, the resource and action it decomposes into, a description, is_system — set for the codes the platform ships with, which therefore cannot be deleted — and the two columns D-099 added: applies_to (unit or unit_and_item, above) and right_kind (read or write). right_kind exists for exactly one customer: guardianship (§1, channel 2) grades a guardian at none / view / write, and with the ladder gone that channel had nothing left to compare a permission against. none covers nothing, view covers read codes, write covers both. It is a column whose only job is one other mechanism, and saying so is cheaper than discovering it later (D-074).

    Codes are resource.action and are stable identifiers, never display strings. The UI shows a human label; the code never changes. Renaming a permission code is a breaking change and must be avoided.

    Naming convention¶

    Pattern Meaning Example
    <resource>.view Read person.view
    <resource>.create Create (always applies_to = unit) event.create
    <resource>.<field>.edit Edit a field subset person.contact.edit
    <resource>.delete Delete (soft, and audited) document.delete
    <resource>.member.manage Write one set's membership person_group.member.manage

    There is no row here for acting on a supervised person, because there is no dependent.* namespace to name (D-074). A guardian's act is checked under the ordinary code for the thing being touched — person.view, person.contact.edit — and reaches the guardianship channel by the shape of the request, not by its name (D-079).

    Every capability is narrow and explicit. A check for X.y needs an exact grant of X.y, or system.admin. There is no <resource>.admin wildcard and no prefix match in the resolver (D-068) — which is what makes the next section load-bearing rather than decorative: if somebody can do everything about events, that is a dozen grant rows naming a role, and you can read them.

    3.1 The level ladder, withdrawn¶

    ~~Each grant carried an access_level — revoked 0, view 10, contribute 20, manage 30, admin 40 — ordered and cumulative, a higher level always subsuming the lower ones, and permission_level said which level satisfied which permission at which scope.~~ Deleted by D-099.

    It was Why it goes
    access_level A level is a bundle of permissions identified by one word. D-068 already refused that idea once, when it deleted <resource>.admin and said roles are the bundle. This finishes the same thought: roles and audiences are the bundle, and nothing else is
    permission_level Its two jobs moved to applies_to and right_kind, both on permission itself
    The rank integer (D-012) There is no ordering left to compare. The famous bug it guarded — 'admin' < 'contribute' comparing alphabetically and inverting the model — cannot occur in a system with no levels, which is a better fix than the guard
    revoked as a level Survives as an effect on a grant row, checked first, unchanged in substance (§7.1)

    The cost, in rows: an office that used to be one grant at manage is now one row per code. The catalog holds 66 codes, and a broad office is a couple of dozen rows. That is the price of the question this makes answerable — "what exactly does this person have?", answered by reading rows — and the price of the one that is no longer askable, which is "what does manage give me on this event?": there is no such word, so there is no such lookup.

    4. Roles are offices; groups are company¶

    Two tables of the same shape, and the difference between them decides who may edit the membership.

    Role (role, role_holder) Group (person_group, person_group_member)
    What it is An office: leader, finance, IT. Small, visible holders, expected to be seen Company: the Thursday group, the kitchen crew, this year's camp staff. People who do something together
    Holder row role_holder(role, person, valid_from, valid_to, granted_by, note) person_group_member(group, person, valid_from, valid_to, added_by, note)
    Held where At the unit, always. D-097 left one scope, so there is nothing else to hold it at The group is its own item, and a grant can be written on it
    Permission that writes its membership role.assign, role.unassign — 🔒🔒, the most sensitive family in the catalog person_group.member.manage — ⚠
    In an expression ROLE(x), or bare x GROUP(x), or bare x
    Authority by being in it None None

    Why two tables when they are technically the same shape — and they are the same shape, which is exactly what D-080 teaches a reader to distrust. The answer is the permission consequence: one table would merge those two codes into one. A single membership table with a kind column gives you a single permission that both adds a child to the Thursday group and puts the holder into SYSTEM_ADMINS. That is the merge to refuse. Same columns, different codes, and ROLE(x) / GROUP(x) distinct in the grant text so the distinction is visible to whoever reads the grant.

    A role confers nothing. role_permission is deleted, and the codes an office carries are the live grants whose audience names ROLE(x). That is what makes "what may a treasurer do?" a question with an answer: it is a query over visible grant rows, not a walk through a bundle table nobody reads. What "the treasurer's permissions" consists of now:

    role         code = treasurer                (a name, a description, is_system = false)
role_holder  treasurer ← Ana, 2026-01-01 → open
access_grant audience = MEMBERS * ROLE(treasurers)   scope = unit
             permission = fee.view            effect = allow
access_grant audience = MEMBERS * ROLE(treasurers)   scope = unit
             permission = payment.record      effect = allow
access_grant audience = MEMBERS * ROLE(treasurers)   scope = unit
             permission = report.finance.view effect = allow
  … one row per code, and the list is the office

    Neither a role nor a group authorizes anything by existing, which is D-005 and D-019 restated one level down: participation is not authority, and storing the participation in a tidier table does not quietly promote it. A group additionally has no parent and no children, no type vocabulary, no code, no path, no nesting rules, no rename history, no split or merger edge, no leadership of its own, no roles, and no fee (D-098).

    Both are items. person_group and role are registered in grant_scope_type, so "these two may work this group's roster" and "these two may manage the counselors roster" are grants on one item — reachable only by someone who already holds the corresponding code at the unit (§2).

    Roles are runtime data, and the seed set is not a specification (D-052, D-053): the organization creates roles, composes them from grants, and deletes the unused ones. The real definitions live in appendix/permission-catalog.md §11. One role ships, and ships is_system so an editor refuses to delete it: counselors. A permanent, organization-wide population is the opposite of the organization's own definition of a group ("a temporary set of members that do events together"), and the table decides the sensitivity that protects the set every safeguarding convention hangs from. It is still only a set — being in it confers nothing until a grant names it (§6.2).

    Positions are deleted¶

    ~~position, position_assignment, is_leadership, max_holders, and the appointment workflows that created a role assignment alongside a position one.~~ Deleted by D-100, along with D-033's occupancy rules. The reason is structural: with no units there is no body to be a leader of, which is the only thing a position recorded and a role does not. An elected leader is now a holder of a role, and the audience rule that used to read unit_leaders — which round 6 had to correct because it was silently matching nobody — is ROLE(leaders), and means what its name says.

    What is genuinely lost is the elected-versus-appointed distinction as stored data. It survives only as roles the organization invents (elected_council next to appointed_it) or not at all. Where the workflows that used to end at a position end now is 04-organization-structure.md §6.

    A role is held by exactly one person per row, and that is now settled rather than open. F-12 asked whether a role could be held by a rule the way a group is, and refused it: an office record that cannot answer "who was treasurer in 2023" is not an office record, and a derived holder list cannot answer that. F-12 is closed, and the answer is no — and the grantee pair F-12 contemplated is no longer even tempting, because grants aim at roles instead of roles being filled by grants.

    The holder period matters¶

    valid_from / valid_to on role_holder means an office ends on a date even if nobody remembers to revoke it. Leavers are the classic audit failure: the volunteer who stepped down in 2019 still has an active account with treasurer access in 2026.

    Nothing forces a holder row to end, and the control is the report (D-071, whose posture transfers from assignments to holder rows unchanged). The old reason to soften that — that the membership gate would quietly expire dormant authority anyway — went with the gate, so the report is now carrying more weight than it did (§6.1, §11).

    5. The grant — one permission, one effect¶

    A grant is the whole of channel 1, and its shape is the whole of this section:

    grantee + scope + one permission_id + effect + period.

    Grantee: a person or an audience, never both and never neither. Scope: the unit, or exactly one item. Effect: allow or deny. Period: valid_from, valid_to, live on the checked date.

    Plus provenance: granted_by, granted_at, grant_reason, and for a lent authority is_delegated with delegated_from naming who it came from (§9).

    One code per grant, not a set. That is what makes the answer readable: "what exactly does this person have?" is a list of rows, and "who else can do this?" is the same list read the other way.

    Allow and deny are not symmetrical¶

    Allowed when some live allow applies and no live deny applies. Everything else in this document is elaboration.

    A deny is legal at either scope. A deny at the unit is precisely the case an expression cannot serve: "exclude her from what the unit grants everyone."

    - in an expression is not a deny, and the two are not interchangeable (D-099). Subtracting a set narrows one grant's reach. A deny defeats every grant that applies to that person, including ones written elsewhere, later, or by somebody else. The first cannot express "she must not have this, whatever else she is given", which is D-013's whole reason for existing: a court-ordered restriction must not be beatable by a broader grant someone forgot about.

    One report comes with this, and it is the whole hygiene of the thing. A live deny whose grantee, scope and code match no live allow is dead weight, and it should be reported — a deny that denies nothing is how a stale restriction survives its reason. Nothing enforces it; the report is the mechanism.

    Why scope_type/scope_id and not one column per item type¶

    scope_id has no foreign key — it is a polymorphic reference, and this is a deliberate, accepted trade-off (D-011):

    • Cost: no referential integrity. A grant can point at a deleted event.
    • Benefit: the permission system does not need a schema change every time a new grantable thing is invented, which — since documents and everything else are still deferred — will happen repeatedly.

    Mitigations, all of which are required: the grant_scope_type registry validates scope types; deleting an item of a granted type must close its grants (an application-level obligation, tested); a nightly job reports orphaned grants; ids are never reused. The heavier alternative — a grantable_item supertable every grantable entity references — was declined for flexibility, and is ../open-questions.md if anyone wants to overrule it (F-2).

    6. The resolver¶

    One function answers every authorization question. Written once, used everywhere — an authorization rule that exists in two places will diverge.

    The call names a person, a permission code, a scope, and a date. The scope is the unit or exactly one item: asking at the unit is how "may this person create an event?" is asked, and asking at an item is how "may this person edit event 1204?" is asked. at defaults to now. Where the thing being touched has an owner, the caller passes the owner too, because one step below needs to know whose record this is. The answer is allowed or denied; the function has no other output, and no part of it is stored. (Field names and the exact signature are in data-model/05 §10.)

    The steps run in this order:

    1. Deny — absolute, at any scope. If any live deny applies — its scope is the unit or the exact item being checked, its code is the code being checked, its grantee is this person or an audience whose expression contains them on the checked date — the answer is no. No scope outranks a deny, and neither does a channel: a per-event allow cannot restore what a unit-scope suspension removed (§7.1).
    2. Ownership. If the target resource's owner is this person → yes for the read, edit and self-service actions. Never conditioned on anything (§6.1): a lapsed person must still be able to reach the screen they renew on.

    3. Guardianship — entered by the shape of the request, and it answers with allows only. Compare the actor with the owner of the thing being touched. Same person, and this channel is not in play at all. Different people, and the question is whether the actor holds a live guardianship over that subject as of at whose right level covers the checked permission's right_kind — view covers read, write covers both, none covers nothing (D-074). Covered → yes. Every other outcome — no row, none, a level too low — is not a refusal: the channel says nothing and the resolver continues, where the person's own grants are tried exactly as they always were (D-079). A parent who is also the event registrar holds view over their own child and must not be refused the roster by their own guardianship.

    4. Nothing escapes by naming. The act that routes here is chosen by whose record it touches, so a permission written next year for a field a guardian must edit is covered the day the field exists. Under the rule this replaces, dispatch read the code's prefix — and an ordinary person.contact.edit slipped past it.

    5. An ended guardianship never opens this channel again. What a former guardian may still do is what any other person may do, through their own grants.

    6. The row is the whole test. The guard must not be rewritten as "the subject is a minor": the resolver contains no age branch, and D-070 and D-077 keep it that way.

    7. Allow — does any live grant apply? Two shapes, both boolean, neither ranked against the other:

    8. a unit grant of this code, to this person directly or to an audience whose expression contains them on at. It reaches this item because a unit grant reaches every item (§2) — there is no tier to walk and no relation on the item to test.
    9. an item grant of this code on exactly this item, to a person or an audience.

    Either → yes. 4. Otherwise → no.

    The order used to be a rule, and half of it is gone. ~~Among allows, the most specific scope determined the effective level, and the first tier that yielded a determination won~~ (D-032's second half, deleted by D-099). With a boolean answer, two allows that both apply produce one answer, so there is nothing to order and renumbering steps 1–3 changes nothing about who can do what. What is still ordered, and must stay so, is step 0: the deny is asked first and can defeat every allow below it. Steps 1–3 keep their order for two reasons that are not precedence — cheap checks before expensive ones, and step 2's "says nothing" fall-through — and neither order is load-bearing for the answer.

    Points worth stressing:

    • Default deny. Step 4. Any new resource starts with nobody able to touch it.
    • Fail closed, in every direction. An operand that does not resolve is the empty set; an expression that cannot be evaluated reaches nobody; a scope type nobody registered grants nothing. This is the same posture as step 4 and step 0, and it is the direction every uncertainty in this model resolves in.
    • A grant may name an audience instead of a person. The grantee is resolved by the expression in §6.2; the scope and the code are resolved exactly as for a personal grant.
    • Effective permissions are never stored. They are computed per request. A stored permission snapshot will drift from the grants that produced it — the only question is how long before anyone notices. There is now a second reason to keep that question open: the snapshot is also the only artefact that could ever answer who could read this in 2023, since set membership is recomputed from state that keeps moving and the audit payload that recorded the change expires (§6.2, F-7).
    • Time is a parameter. at defaults to now, but the resolver accepts a historical time. This enables "could this person have done this on that date?" — needed for auditing past actions, and free if built in from the start. It answers better than it used to: the dated rows behind MEMBERS, GROUP(x), ROLE(x) and the attendance selectors are all periods (§4, §6.2), so the recomputation has something honest to read.

    6.1 MEMBERS — the membership test, moved into the expression¶

    Since round 2 this was the membership gate, a clause in the resolver: a role granted nothing while its holder's membership had lapsed (D-039), which made the resolver's answer time-sensitive rather than merely scope-sensitive, and needed two exemptions — role.requires_membership for a whole role, and the system.gate_exempt permission for one named person (D-051) — the second of which had to be carefully kept out of can() and came with a documented lockout hazard.

    All of it is deleted by D-100: the resolver's membership clause, role.requires_membership, system.gate_exempt, person_holds_gate_exempt. The test is now a selector term, and MEMBERS is evaluated live on the checked date like any other operand:

    Old mechanism New expression
    The test applied to a role with requires_membership = true MEMBERS * ROLE(x)
    The test waived for a whole role ROLE(x) — write it without MEMBERS
    The test waived for one named person (system.gate_exempt) MEMBERS * ROLE(x) + PERSON(y)
    The test never applied: ownership, guardianship, and every explicit grant Not written at all — a channel has no expression

    The third row is also writable without a selector, since a grant may be aimed at a person directly (§5): "and also him" is a second grant, which is how the exemption looks when the writer would rather not touch the expression everyone else reads.

    The posture reverses, and the new failure mode must be named rather than buried. The gate was default-on, so forgetting it was impossible. It is now opt-in per grant: a grant written as ROLE(treasurers) with no MEMBERS authorizes a lapsed member, silently, forever. Nothing errors, nothing reports itself when it happens, and nothing about the grant looks wrong — a lapsed treasurer is still a treasurer. Three controls, none of which is the resolver:

    1. A standing report of live grants naming a ROLE(...) operand whose expression does not contain MEMBERS. An office held by a non-member is the case worth a human eye, and the report is narrow enough to actually be read.
    2. Seeded grants and any grant template carry MEMBERS, so the path of least repetition is the safe path.
    3. Import completeness. MEMBERS * COUNSELORS is only as correct as the counselor roster: the test used to fall out of membership alone, so an import that misses 300 counselors silently un-gates them. It fails closed — nothing breaks, which is exactly why nobody notices. The import reconciliation report of D-090 must count people marked as counselors in the corpus against role_holder rows written.

    The case the old flag existed for still decides the default. The organization's answer on who the gate should spare was "everything except guardianship and adult volunteers": a volunteer whose own fee is unpaid still runs the Thursday meeting, and losing the roster on the night the fee lapses is the failure the marking existed to prevent. None of that judgment moved. What changed is that honouring it no longer needs a flag on a role or a permission on a person — it needs an expression written without MEMBERS — so leaving MEMBERS out is now the normal shape of a grant to a contractor, a photographer or a parent helper rather than an exemption somebody must be granted. The line the old design drew still holds, and it holds harder now that the report is the only mechanism left: the report is how the organization notices; the resolver is not how it punishes.

    Why the test moved rather than vanished. A resolver clause applies to everything or nothing, and so had to carry its own exemptions; an expression says which grants are gated, in the text of the grant, where the person writing it can see it and a report can read it. The old design could not answer "is this particular permission meant to require membership?", because it never recorded the answer anywhere. The cost is that recording it is now optional, and the three controls above are what stands where the guarantee used to be. Nothing enforces MEMBERS being present: a convention, not a guard (D-081).

    The old table of who was gated is kept, because the answers did not change — only who gives them:

    Channel Gated? Why, and how it is written now
    Ownership (step 1) No Otherwise a lapse locks a person out of the screen they renew on. Not expressible in an expression at all
    Guardianship (step 2) No A legal relationship, not a membership
    Grants (step 3) Only if the expression says so MEMBERS * …. A grant to a contractor, a photographer or a parent helper who was never a member is written without MEMBERS — and that is now the default rather than an exemption
    A deny (step 0) Never, in either direction §7.1

    6.2 Audiences — an expression over named sets¶

    A grant names a person or an audience, never both (D-042). An audience is now an expression over named sets of people, stored as a tree of binary nodes and aimed at by a grant (D-101). Fields, storage rules and the write-path checks are in data-model/05 §8; what belongs here is the language, and why it is shaped like this.

    Author in sets. Evaluate per person.

    A check asks about one person, so the resolver compiles the expression down to a predicate and never materializes a set. An implementation that evaluates MEMBERS as a list of ids and then tests for containment pays three thousand rows per request. That is the one way to get this wrong, which is why it is a requirement rather than an implementation note.

    The two notations the organization was offered — set algebra, and a per-person boolean formula — describe the same function: p ∈ (A ∩ B) ∪ C is by definition (p ∈ A ∧ p ∈ B) ∨ p ∈ C. The choice therefore never decided what the machine computes; it decided what gets stored and what a writer is allowed to say. The set form won because it is the closed one, and closure was the whole case. D-060 refused a rule engine, and a per-person boolean language is the door to one: once &, | and ! exist, the next operand anybody wants is AGE >= 15 or FEE_PAID, and there is a parser standing there to accept it. A set language closes that by rule instead of by discipline: an operand must be a named, materializable set of people. An attribute is not a set of people. Guardianship is (GUARDIANS(x) is a set), age is not, and the line falls exactly where D-060 already drew it.

    Grammar¶

    expr     := term ( '+' term )*
term     := operand ( ( '*' | '-' ) operand )*
operand  := EVERYONE | MEMBERS | LIST
          | ROLE '(' name ')' | GROUP '(' name ')' | name
          | GUARDIANS | GUARDIANS '(' person ')'
          | ATTENDED | PLACED | CONFIRMED | ON_WAITLIST | ORGANIZERS
          | ATTENDED '(' event ')' | PLACED '(' event ')' | CONFIRMED '(' event ')'
          | ON_WAITLIST '(' event ')' | ORGANIZERS '(' event ')'
          | PRESENT '(' event ')'        ← refused, see below
          | '(' expr ')'

    Operators: + union, - difference, * intersection.

    Precedence: * and - bind tighter than +; * and - bind equally, left to right; brackets override.

    The canonical stored rendering prints every bracket, always. The alternative is a grant whose meaning is recoverable only by someone who memorized that table, and the reason this is a requirement rather than a courtesy is the worked example below: the same string means different things under different precedence, and a stored grant is read by people years later who did not write it. Because canonicalization happens on save, changing the precedence rule later changes nothing already stored — a nice property, and the second reason the rendering is a requirement.

    One arm above is not in D-101's printed grammar and is required by its prose twice over: a bare name, resolving against roles and groups together — without it, MEMBERS * COUNSELORS, which D-100 writes as an import control, does not parse. Status: assumption — that a bare name is legal; recorded in ../open-questions.md.

    Names are one namespace, and that is a new dependency. A bare name resolves against role and person_group together, since ROLE() and GROUP() no longer have to disambiguate. So names are unique across both tables — and no single constraint spans two tables, so the write path carries the rule and an integrity check asserts that it does (D-081) — with case, whitespace and separators folded on write. Every selector_type.code is a reserved name, so a group called MEMBERS is refused at the save rather than silently rewriting every expression in the system. What is stored is always kind + id, so a rename never rewrites a grant.

    The selectors¶

    Selector The set, on the checked date Argument Notes
    EVERYONE Any person, including non-members and the public none Legal — and the reason the editor must show its reach (§The cost)
    MEMBERS Anyone with a current membership none Evaluated live. §6.1
    GUARDIANS / GUARDIANS(p) Guardians of the person this item is about / of p optional person
    LIST This audience's own dated list (audience_member) none The manual arm, re-roled as a leaf
    ROLE(x) / x Holders of role x (role_holder) a role name
    GROUP(x) / x Members of group x (person_group_member) a group name
    ATTENDED / ATTENDED(e) Said yes to this event / to e optional event response = attending
    PLACED / PLACED(e) Said yes and is not waitlisted — the capacity set optional event The set the events module counts
    CONFIRMED / CONFIRMED(e) Holds a paid place: PLACED and is_confirmed optional event
    ON_WAITLIST / ON_WAITLIST(e) Attending and waitlisted optional event
    ORGANIZERS / ORGANIZERS(e) Runs this event optional event Appointed on the event, and still grants nothing by itself (§Event sets)
    PRESENT(e) — refused §Event sets

    Bound operands — the argument-less forms — are the important half, and they exist to kill a specific flaw. ATTENDED(<event 1204>) written on event 1204 is one grant per event with the event's own id typed into it, so "every participant may see the other participants" is a thing you do 400 times and forget on the 401st. Bound, it is one expression written the same way every time. Two rules make that safe, and both are enforced when the grant is saved, refused with the operand named in the message:

    • A bound operand is legal only on an item-scope grant. At the unit there is nothing to bind it to.
    • A bound operand is legal only on a matching item type. PLACED on a group grant is refused, because that item is not an event. This is selector_type.argument_type checked against grant_scope_type.

    selector_type is a seeded registry, not an enum — code, takes_argument, binds_to_item, argument_type. Which operands take an argument, which may bind to the item, and what type their argument is used to be three hard-coded lists. Now the parser reads its reserved words from this table, the editor builds its dropdown from it, and the write path checks arity against it. The language became data.

    The example, worked¶

    The organization wrote:

    MEMBERS - GROUP(schuzka2026-1) * COUNSELORS

    meaning "all counselors that are not part of the group schuzka2026-1". Under the fixed precedence — - and * equal, left to right — it parses as:

    (MEMBERS - GROUP(schuzka2026-1)) * COUNSELORS

    which is what its author meant, and that is the string that gets stored, brackets and all: the canonical rendering of what was typed.

    The hazard is real even though this string lands safely, and that is exactly why the rendering is a requirement. Read with * taking the tighter binding — the other natural choice, and the one a writer who thinks of - as "everything except" will assume — the identical text means:

    MEMBERS - (GROUP(schuzka2026-1) * COUNSELORS)

    "everyone except the counselors in that group": a different set of people, and a far wider grant — nearly the whole membership instead of a handful of counselors. D-101 records the corrected bracketed form as (MEMBERS - GROUP(schuzka2026-1)) * COUNSELORS, and that is what the editor writes to storage whichever way the typed string parses, so no stored expression is ever ambiguous and none is ever read bare. What canonicalization does not do is tell the writer, after the fact, that the expression they typed meant the wide thing. That is the editor's preview, and it is the same requirement as §The cost.

    One honest wrinkle, reported rather than smoothed over: D-101 states the precedence as equal-and-left-to-right and, in the same entry, states that the bare form "now means everyone except the counselors in that group", which is the *-binds-tighter reading. The rule above is the one both D-101's grammar and round 10's vocabulary fix, and the corrected bracketed form is reproduced as D-101 gives it. If * was ever meant to bind tighter, that is a decision to record — and because everything is stored fully bracketed, nothing already stored would change.

    Event sets, and the two sets that are refused¶

    Nothing owns an event. event.org_unit_id is deleted, and with it can_event() — the unit-authority test that almost every event permission in this system used to be reached through (D-102). An event is an item like any other: event.create is held at the unit and creates one, and a unit grant reaches every event because there is no tier between the unit and the item and no owner to test.

    ORGANIZERS is what replaces unit authority over an event, and it is not a contradiction of D-062: an event_organizer row grants nothing still stands, because an operand is a set, not a grant. What changed is that the appointment is now aimed at by grants rather than special-cased inside the resolver. The rule that used to be hard-coded prose — "answers are read by that event's organizers" (D-061) — is now said where permissions are said: a seeded grant of event.answer.view on an event, aimed at ORGANIZERS.

    Presence is refused, and saying so here is the point. PRESENT(<event>) over was_present is not a selector, and the reason is the failure direction: was_present is NULL until someone takes a register, so an operand built on it would revoke a child's access to their own camp photos because a volunteer forgot to tick a box. That column is a safeguarding signal, three-valued on purpose (D-067), and keeping it out of this language is what keeps it meaning "a person recorded this" rather than "this is who may see things." Wherever presence tempts a reader, that is the answer.

    WHITELISTED does not exist yet — there is no stored state to name. The nearest facts are source = staff (an organizer entered them) and promotion off the waitlist. Either it means one of those or it is a new column on event_registration, which is an events decision and not a selector decision, so it stays an open question in ../open-questions.md rather than being implied by a word in a grammar.

    The rules that make the tree safe¶

    The expression is stored as a tree: one row per node, a node being operator + left_id + right_id, a leaf being selector_kind + selector_arg_id, with audience_id denormalized onto every row so the read path is one indexed fetch and never walks a parent chain. selector_depth_limit on the audience row bounds how tall an expression may get.

    • Exactly one shape per row. A node has an operator and children and no selector; a leaf has a selector and neither. Expressible, so it stays a constraint.
    • One parent, never a DAG. A side table letting two expressions share a node is refused: editing the shared node would silently widen every grant using it. Structure, plus a CI check that no such table exists.
    • Cycles and over-depth are refused when the row is saved — walk the parent chain, refuse a revisit, refuse past the limit. Structurally hard to build, refused rather than trusted, for the reason D-091 gives for its own guard: a hand-fixed row, a restore from before the guard existed.
    • selector_arg_id has no foreign key — it points at role, person_group, person or event depending on kind. It is D-011's already-accepted trade-off, with its compensating orphan check.
    • An operand that does not resolve is the empty set, and an expression that cannot be evaluated reaches nobody. Fail closed, in every direction.

    ~~A group is filled three ways: a rule arm, a manual arm, and an inclusion arm naming other groups, and its membership is the union of whichever it has.~~ Superseded by D-101, which amends D-091 rather than reversing it. The rule arm became the selectors, the manual arm became the LIST leaf, and the inclusion arm is gone: audience_include, include_depth_limit, the transitive walk and the write-time cycle check over a graph all went, because a tree cannot cycle. The outcome D-091 wanted survives and is better served — GROUP(kitchen) + ROLE(troop_staff) composes as much as an edge ever did, and does it in one grant, where the old audience had no intersection and no difference at all, which is why the counselor example above was not writable in any notation.

    audience_member stays, re-roled as the LIST leaf: the audience's own dated list of people. Merging it into GROUP(x) was weighed and refused twice over — naming three people in one grant would create a row in the group browser that is not a group anybody talks about, and a group's members are the organization's record of who is in it, true when no grant points at it, while a selector's additions are meaningless outside the selector they complete (D-098). The duplication D-080 would flag is therefore kept on purpose, and the mitigation is the one D-091 already required: the editor shows which mechanism each person arrived through.

    What is genuinely lost with the inclusion edge is one-edit widening: "add the kitchen crew to everything the troop-staff group is used for" is no longer a single edit. The answer is that named sets are the composition mechanism now — name the composite once, aim grants at it — which is D-091's outcome reached without a graph nobody could see the shape of.

    The cost lands on the editor, not the resolver — and it moved in round 10. A grant's reach was never visible in the row that grants it, and D-091 made seeing it a requirement on the screen rather than a nicety: the person saving the grant must see the people, not the audience's name, because nothing in the grammar stops an expression from containing EVERYONE. After D-102 the preview can no longer be computed from the grant alone: PLACED means different people on different events, so the preview is computed for an item and says so on screen. That is a real increase in what the editor has to do, and it is the only thing standing between a writer and a grant that reaches 3,000 people.

    "Who could read this in 2023?" has one fewer answer than it had. Set membership is recomputed, not recorded, and composition widens how much of the permission answer depends on state that has itself moved on. The audit trail used to hold the change that made it true, and D-088 empties those payloads after 24 months. So after two years the question is answered by re-running expressions against dated rows — which is honest for ROLE, GROUP, LIST and MEMBERS, whose rows are all periods, and unanswerable for bound event operands, since event_registration records the current fact. F-7 is the route back — a materialized effective_permission snapshot kept as history rather than as a cache — which until this round looked like a performance question.

    7. Precedence¶

    Two separate questions used to live here. One of them is gone, and the section keeps its shape so that a reader arriving from the decision log can see which half died.

    7.1 Who decides — deny is absolute¶

    1. Deny          (a live deny applies at ANY scope)   ← WINS OVER EVERYTHING
2. Ownership     (it's your own row)
3. Guardianship  (the act is about someone else — allows only)
4. Any live allow (unit grant or item grant — §6 step 3)
5. Deny          (nothing matched)

    A deny is checked before anything else and is never subject to any ordering. A deny at the unit blocks access to one event even if a grant on that event explicitly allows it. There is no scope local enough to override a suspension (D-013).

    A deny is also never subject to the membership test, in either direction. A deny blocks someone who is not a member, which is obvious. What is worth stating: a deny to an audience blocks the people in that audience whether or not they are current members, because the deny is evaluated against who the expression matches, not against whether they are paid up. Suspensions are absolute; MEMBERS is a condition on a grant of authority, and there is no authority here to condition.

    A deny is legal at the unit as well as on an item, and "exclude her from what the unit grants everyone" is the case only a deny can serve (§5).

    7.2 There is nothing left to order¶

    ~~Among competing allows, the most specific scope determined the effective level: item grant → unit grant → organization grant → role. The first tier that yielded a determination decided the level, and a per-event manage therefore gave you manage on that event even if your only unit-level grant was view.~~

    Withdrawn by D-099, which deleted half of D-032 — and it is worth being exact about which half, because the surviving half is the reason this section still exists.

    Locality only ever ranked allows against each other. It answered "two grants both apply and they say different amounts" — a question that cannot be asked when a grant says yes-or-no about one permission code. Two allows that both apply produce one answer. Once the answer is boolean, there is nothing left to order, and the tie-break, the tier ladder, and permission_level's "is this level high enough" went with it. D-032's first half — deny is absolute — survives intact and is now its entire content.

    What the old consequences guaranteed, and where it comes from now:

    Used to follow from locality Follows from
    A per-event grant gives you control of that event even with a narrow unit grant A grant on that item applying, full stop (§6 step 3)
    A unit role keeps working for the unit's other events A unit grant reaches every item, and no item grant can retract it — only a deny can (§7.1)
    A specific view grant works for someone with no role at all — a contractor, a parent photographer The same, and with no MEMBERS in the expression it is not even conditioned on membership (§6.1)
    Locality orders grants but does not create authority Still true, and now blunt: creating a grant required access_grant.create on that item. That is where the authority to make it came from

    8. Caching and invalidation¶

    Permission checks happen on every request. Compute cheaply, cache carefully.

    The cache key must include everything the answer depends on:

    (person_id, permission_code, scope_type, scope_id, at_bucket)

    where at_bucket truncates the timestamp to a granularity shorter than the shortest grant duration you allow. If grants can start or end at an arbitrary second, a time-bucketed cache is unsafe — use now() and a short TTL instead.

    Since D-102 the item is not optional in that key. A bound operand means the same expression yields different people on different items — PLACED on event 1204 is not PLACED on event 1205 — so a cached answer computed while checking one item may never be served for another, and any cache keyed per person or per grant alone is now a bug (F-6, amended).

    Invalidate on:

    • any write to access_grant, audience_operand, audience_member, guardianship_permission
    • role_holder — an office gained or lost changes every grant whose expression names ROLE(x). This is the index the question "what may a treasurer do?" runs on (§4), which is also why operand-level caching pays off: a cached operand can be reused across every grant that names it, and role_holder changes are the invalidation
    • person_group_member, for the same reason through GROUP(x)
    • any write to an audience's tree — an edit to an expression changes the answer for every grant aimed at it, so invalidation cannot be scoped to the row that was touched
    • every membership transition, including the yearly expiry sweep — MEMBERS is evaluated on the checked date, so a lapse changes whether a person is in the set for every gated expression in the system. Invalidate by person_id, not by scope, and make it a bulk operation the cache is designed for: the sweep touches everyone at once
    • a merge, and an unmerge — this is the one that gets missed. Merging two people unions their permissions, so the survivor's cache must be cleared; reversing a merge splits them back in two, so both rows must be cleared, and it gets missed for the opposite reason — nobody thinks of an undo as a permission change (D-087). Unmerge is person.merge, not a separate code, so nothing here changes shape; only the list of events that must invalidate does. See 03-identity-and-legacy.md.
    • a write to event_registration — a new registration, a confirmation, a promotion off the waitlist. Every bound attendance operand changes, and this is the invalidation most likely to be left out, because the events module does not look like a permission table
    • ~~a unit's parent changing~~ and ~~a write to scope_exposure~~ — retired together by D-097, the first because reparenting was already impossible (D-036) and the second because the table it named no longer exists

    A note on TTL. Access still changes on a date rather than on someone's action — not because of a gate, but because MEMBERS, GROUP(x), ROLE(x) and every dated row are read as of the checked date. The sweep invalidates immediately; the TTL governs the window for transitions that are not the sweep. Keep the TTL short and measure.

    Alternative worth considering: materialize effective_permission as a table refreshed on write rather than cached per request. Faster reads, but it is a denormalization and it must be maintained transactionally — if a refresh fails silently you have an access bug that nothing detects. If you take this path, add a verification job that recomputes and compares. Recommendation: start with cache-only, add materialization only if measurement demands it (F-7, and §6.2 for the history argument that now points the other way).

    9. Delegation¶

    A leader going on leave, a treasurer handing over during an audit — the common need to lend authority temporarily.

    A delegation is an ordinary grant with its provenance set: is_delegated, delegated_from naming the person whose authority is being lent, grant_reason, and a valid_to. Round 10 makes this easier to get right rather than changing it: because a grant is one permission code, "do not lend more than you hold" is a set-membership test rather than a comparison of levels (D-099).

    Rules:

    • A delegation always has an end date. Enforced on the write path.
    • A delegation cannot exceed the delegator's own authority — validate at grant time that the code being delegated is one the delegator currently resolves to, and re-check at use time, because the delegator may lose it mid-delegation.
    • The provenance is recorded, so an audit can answer "who really had this authority on that date?" — it lists both the holder and the delegator.
    • A deny cannot be lent and cannot be lent around. A delegation is an allow; the delegatee's answer is computed from their own rows plus the lent one, and their own denies still apply.
    • Delegation of system.admin is prohibited outright.

    ~~role.is_delegatable~~ — the role-level switch that made a whole office lendable — went with the rest of the role configuration in D-100, which lists role's surviving columns as code, name, description, is_system, sort_order. Lending is therefore said on the grant, not on the role. Status: assumption — D-100 names the column in neither the deleted nor the kept list; recorded in ../open-questions.md.

    11. Open issues¶

    • Which grants are meant to carry MEMBERS? Nothing enforces it (§6.1). The report is the control, and the report's threshold — every ROLE(...) operand without MEMBERS, or only the offices marked sensitive? — is decided nowhere.
    • Whether the report of dead-weight denies and the report of ungated offices are scheduled jobs or screens somebody opens. Both are entire controls now, and neither has a cadence recorded.
    • The holder period. Nothing forces a role_holder row to end, and the report that used to be backstopped by the gate now stands alone (§4, D-071).
    • Who may compose an expression? audience.manage covers creating and editing a set, and the catalog is silent on the edit that puts EVERYONE into a grant's audience (appendix/permission-catalog.md §12). No new code was invented for this — the gap is the finding.
    • Duplicate expressions. Two expressions with the same meaning are now two different row sets, so "you already granted exactly this set on that item" no longer falls out of storage. The route back is a normalized fingerprint computed on save as a derived value used only for a warning — and if the expansion overflows, skip the warning rather than refuse the write: normalization informing a human, not gating one. Registered in ../open-questions.md, not promised (D-101).
    • WHITELISTED — no stored state to name, so no selector. An events decision before it is a selector decision (§6.2, D-102).
    • Which selectors the seed ships, and whether an organization may add one: the language is one seeded table plus an evaluator, so an operand can be added by any later decision, and which ones ship on day one is not decided (F-5).
    • The cache TTL under live-evaluated selectors (§8).
    • Whether a historical check must be answerable at all for bound event operands, and whether that is what finally justifies a materialized history table (§6.2, F-7).

    Resolved and no longer open, kept visible so the reasoning survives: whether any permission is conditioned on membership being current (no resolver condition — the writer says so with MEMBERS; D-039's clause and D-051's two exemptions are deleted, §6.1); whether the gate should also reach item grants (the question is dissolved: it reaches nothing, and an expression is where the choice lives); whether a role may be held by a rule as a group is (no — F-12 closed by D-100, §4); polymorphic scope_id (confirmed — §5, F-2); the bulk exposure tool, the direction-of-authority check, whether the root may read into its sub-units, and whether a body may expose its scope (all dissolved by D-097 — there are no sub-units, §2.1); whether a group may be a rule and a list, or contain another group (both superseded by D-101: the arms became selectors, the LIST leaf, and +; §6.2); and whether a grant needs a level (no — D-099, §3.1).
