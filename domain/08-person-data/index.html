    Domain — Personal Data¶

    What the system knows about a person, why it is all one kind of thing, and what that choice costs. The tables are in ../data-model/09-attributes.md; the reasoning is D-083, D-084 and D-085.

    1. One class of data, and no privileged corner¶

    Everything about a person apart from their name, their gender and their age is personal data, stored in fields rather than files — and it is not different in any way.

    There is no special shelf inside the person record, no code list whose names begin with something clinical, no access path that exists only for the sensitive part. A drug dosage, a preferred contact language and a shoe size are the same kind of fact: something the organization wrote down about somebody, which will be out of date in three years, which nobody could have enumerated when the schema was designed.

    The consequence is uncomfortable and intended: the permission that reads a person's record now reads everything in it. A volunteer with that permission sees the allergy list and the insurance number and the note about who in the family the child actually lives with. That is what "not different in any way" means, and it is why the catalog marks reading a person as one of the serious permissions in the system rather than an ordinary one.

    The three exceptions, and why they are exceptions¶

    Name, gender and age stay structured, in the identity model (03-identity-and-legacy.md). Not because they are less sensitive — a name is the most persistent piece of data here — but because they answer a different question. They are what a person is called and counted as; everything else is what the organization happens to have noticed. The three are also the only descriptive facts that have been stable across every system this organization has used, which is the practical test for whether something earns a column.

    The boundary between "identity machinery" and "descriptive data" is drawn by a harsher test than sensitivity: a fact keeps its own table when there is a rule about it that an open layer cannot express. "Exactly one primary postal contact", "a school id may repeat but a birth number may not", "one current name" — those rules need a known set of columns, and an open layer's whole value is that it has none.

    2. The vocabulary is the system¶

    If the organization decides at runtime what it collects, then the list of things it collects is the part of the design that matters. The tables are trivial; the vocabulary is not.

    An open vocabulary left open decays the same way every open vocabulary does. Within months there will be allergy, Allergy, alergie, and allergies (2), each with some of the values, and after that the layer still stores everything and answers nothing. Three defences, none clever:

    • Codes are compared in normalized form, so the obvious duplicates are refused rather than filed.
    • Recording a value is open; naming a thing is not. Anyone who can edit a person can write down what the doctor said tonight. Creating a code — deciding that this is now a thing the organization knows about people — takes a permission and a person who reads what they are given.
    • An improvised need becomes a proposal, not a workaround. The failure this is aimed at is specific and familiar: at 23:00 on a Friday, there is no field for "carries an adrenaline auto-injector", so the leader types it into a notes box, and it is never read by anyone again. A proposal is visible, queued and curable; a notes box is neither.

    Curation is a job, and the documents say so rather than assuming it happens. An unwatched proposal queue is the same information loss arriving slowly instead of all at once (../data-model/09-attributes.md §8).

    3. Who needs to know what¶

    Who What they need What the design gives them
    A leader The allergies and instructions for the children they lead, tonight, without asking permission Whatever the organization has granted them, and nothing more — with the cost attached: there is no scope smaller than the whole organization except a single item, so "the children in their unit" has no scope to be granted as such. A person.view at unit scope hands over every member's entire record, allergy lists included; the narrower thing a leader actually wanted is a group roster or an event. The starting grant set gives leadership rosters and events but not a read over the person record
    A guardian Everything about their child, or nothing, depending on what the organization decided The guardian right level — none, view, write — over the whole record (D-074)
    The person themselves Their own data Their own record, which is not a special case in the resolver — "about you" is already a channel
    An event organizer What matters on the day, for the people who registered The registration, and the event form that asks. Where the organization already recorded a fact, AT-1 asks whether the form should prefill from it rather than re-asking every camper every season
    A first aider at a camp The short list: what is this child allergic to, what do they take, what should we do The values are fields, so a list can be produced — which is the argument for fields over documents

    Read the leader row twice: it is the sharpest edge in this module. There is no privileged subset of the record to be kept away from anybody, so nothing in the model can promise that a leader cannot read a child's data. What carries the protection is that the starting grants do not give leadership a read over the record, because leadership is not guardianship — and an office confers nothing at all (D-100), so the only thing that could give a leader that read is a grant, written somewhere, naming them. The organization may hand it over deliberately, in one grant, and see exactly what that exposes. (permission-catalog §11 note 3)

    The last row is the one that justifies the whole shape. A scanned form in a drawer is a legally tidy answer and a useless one at 23:00 in a tent; the fact has to be something a screen can show somebody who is not thinking clearly.

    4. What was refused, and what it cost¶

    One level cannot separate the medical record from the profile. The design therefore cannot say "everything except the diagnoses", which is the shape an arrangement a court might order takes. It expresses such a case as a guardian who either has the record or does not, and somebody will have to explain the difference to a parent. The model fails visibly — one decision, in one place, audited — where a capability list fails quietly, because a list of eighteen ticks nobody read is not a safeguard, it is a receipt. If the case arrives in reality, the route back is one new grant scope over a defined set of data, argued once as a decision, and not a fresh namespace of suggestive permission names. Why: D-074, D-083.

    There is no per-field type wall. A field declared as a date warns when someone types 2019, and lets them save it. A wall is the wrong tool precisely where the true value is imprecise and the imprecise answer beats none: probably allergic to wasp stings, mother thinks it was only once is a fact worth keeping, and a validation error would discard it in favor of an empty field.

    No history on the row, and no long history anywhere. Values are overwritten and the audit trail holds the sequence — the values themselves, not a note that something changed, since a trail that will not say what changed cannot serve as a history. Elsewhere the model keeps history in business tables when "what was it on that date?" is routine; guardianship is the standing example. Here it is not routine, it is what someone asks during an incident. So the history of a child's allergy list lives in the one control these documents elsewhere admit is discipline rather than guarantee (D-081), in two dependencies, not one: somebody has to write the audit row, on every path including the ones nobody thinks of, and somebody has to keep the trail guarded as the copy of the whole person record that it is. On top of that, the trail's values are emptied 24 months after they were written, though the rows remain (D-088): two years of a child's changing facts are recoverable, and what the organization believed in 2019 is not recoverable at all. That is a real risk, accepted in the open; the entry to reverse if the long version is ever genuinely needed is D-085, not a longer trail.

    5. Forgetting¶

    Two operations that look alike and must not be confused.

    • Retiring a thing the organization collects keeps what was collected. The label leaves the form; the values stay readable and reportable, and the label can come back. Deleting a name must not delete nine years of facts, or nobody will ever retire a badly-named thing and the list will only ever grow.
    • Erasing a person deletes what was collected, everywhere, including in the trail's payloads. The organization keeps that it held a record and that it was removed — and not what the record said.

    The audit trail carries these values, in the clear, because it is the only place they have a history. What changed is not what the trail holds but who may read it: an entry about a person is as sensitive as the person data it quotes, so the permission that opens the trail sits with the serious permissions rather than among the administrative ones (permission-catalog §8). There is no list of sensitive field names the trail scrubs, and there must not be one: such a list is only as good as somebody having predicted the name of every field that would matter, and this organization invents those names at runtime. The protection is the guard — one line in one table of one appendix, and the documents say so where they describe it (../data-model/09-attributes.md §6).

    What erasure does is absolute in a way the guard is not. It deletes the values and reaches into the trail to delete them again from copies written years earlier, lawfully, when nobody was asking questions about them.

    A third way values leave the trail, and it is a policy rather than a right. Every two years' worth of audit rows have their contents emptied whether anybody asked or not (D-088). The distinction from erasure is worth holding onto, because the two are easy to conflate and only one of them is a person's entitlement: erasure is requested and verifiable — the check is that nothing survives — while expiry is neither requested nor satisfied by nothing surviving, since the row itself remains and only its values go. So its failure mode is the opposite one: it fails by not happening. That is why it is a scheduled job with a check that alerts on an over-age row still holding content, and not a line in a policy document (data-model/06 §2.2 keeps that apart from redaction, which must never be deferred). It is also why the sentence the trail is a second copy of the person record — the sentence that decides who may read it — is true for two years and untrue afterwards.
