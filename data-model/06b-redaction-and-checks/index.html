    Data Model — Audit and Import: the redaction log, the integrity checks, open issues¶

    redaction_log, the checks that run over both trails, and what is still outstanding. The trail the redaction writes into is §2; where the legacy import pipeline used to be, and what replaced it, is §3.

    Section numbers are shared across the two parts and never renumbered, because other pages cite them by number and their anchors are built from them: 06-audit-and-import.md holds §1–§3 (the two audit trails, the audit_log table, and a tombstone where the import pipeline was), and 06b §4–§6 (the redaction log, the integrity checks, open issues).

    4. Redaction log¶

    Fields

    Field Kind Notes
    id surrogate id
    person_id reference to person, required The row is NOT deleted. The id survives so historical references resolve
    requested_at timestamp, audit time Default: now
    requested_by reference to person, optional
    legal_basis text, optional gdpr_art17, court_order, internal_policy — required for accountability
    performed_at timestamp, audit time, optional
    performed_by reference to person, optional
    redacted_tables list of text Which tables had values overwritten — and which had rows deleted. person_attribute is erased by deletion rather than by overwriting (00-conventions §4), so naming it here is what records that the erasure reached the person's descriptive data, and omitting it is indistinguishable from not having reached it
    retained_reason text, optional Why some data was kept. It has a standing answer to hold now. Membership periods, fee obligations, payments and this anonymised person row are kept with no end date because they are the organization's own history, which is the judgement this field was built to carry and which, until D-088, nothing in these documents had actually made. What the field adds per case is that this erasure made it knowingly, and the reason for anything retained beyond the skeleton. The judgement underneath the standing answer is the designer's own — D-205 retired the review that would have settled it, so what the standing answer carries is its risk (these are the one class of row that outlives every safeguard in 00-conventions §8, and nothing outside the design vouches for them) where a basis used to sit (§6)
    note text, optional

    Rules

    • A redaction cannot record when it was performed without recording who performed it: performed_at without performed_by is illegal.
    • An erasure must also rewrite the expressions that named the person, and say what it changed. Since D-105 a hand-named person is a PERSON(x) leaf of audience_operand, and selector_arg_id carries no foreign key, so nothing refuses the deletion underneath it. The failure is not a dangling row, it is an inverted grant: a leaf that resolves to nobody is the empty set, and the empty set subtracts nothing, so an expression reading EVERYONE - PERSON(x) begins denying everybody the moment x is erased. So the erasure walks audience_operand for leaves naming that person, removes or rewrites them under the same access_grant guard that covers any other change to reach, prints the resolved set before and after for every expression it touched — the same report D-104 adopted — and names audience_operand in redacted_tables, because a field that lists which tables the erasure reached is worth nothing if the one table where the person still appears is left off it. The nightly orphan scan in 05d-invariants-and-seed.md §11 is the backstop for this, and is not the control: the control is the erasure path itself, because a scan that reports a grant denying everybody every night is a report somebody stops reading.

    Indexes

    • A person's redaction history.

    This log is the answer to "prove you honoured the erasure request". It records what was removed, when, on what legal basis, and — importantly — what was deliberately retained and why. A redaction performed without a record is indistinguishable from a data loss incident.

    Two clocks, and this log is where the permanent one gets written down. An erasure acts in more than one shape, and the shapes differ in what they leave behind. It overwrites the person row's names and contact points, immediately and irreversibly; it deletes the person_attribute rows, so that redacted_tables naming that table is the only sentence left saying they existed; it empties an event_answer's value and keeps the row (§5, and D-140); and it scrubs that person's values out of the trail's payloads — out of rows which, since D-088, are scheduled to lose their payloads anyway (§2.2). Round 25 added one column-list to that sentence, and it is the emptied shape rather than the deleted one: membership.notes, membership_request.notes and payment.notes are emptied and their rows remain (D-215). Free text beside a row the organization keeps is the event-answer case and not the attribute case — the row is a fee, an ask or a membership, and the note is a remark on it. What an erasure does not do is touch the facts in membership, fee_obligation and payment, or this row's person_id — a period, an amount or a date is untouched, and only the free text beside them goes — and the reason is now a stated justification rather than nobody having decided when those rows end. That difference, between a rule nobody defended and a rule with a reason attached, is exactly what retained_reason carries.

    One body of text stands outside that whole list, by decision and not by oversight. A message, its body, its recipient rows and their opened-moment are neither emptied nor deleted (D-211), and the reason is on the record because the argument against it was available: D-191 deleted person.notes on the ground that free text about a person, sitting outside erasure, makes erasure a lie — that argument applies to a message body word for word, and it was put and refused, on the ground that a message is correspondence, an act this organization performed on a date, and not something it deletes on request. The consequence is stated rather than discovered: an erasure removes the person and leaves every message addressed to them standing, its recipient row pointing at the tombstone 01b leaves behind. The columns this list does not name are named in §6 — a roster row's note, an account's status_reason and status_note, and the two values a field_conflict holds — and whether they join it is K-16. The answer that moved these three columns named three columns, and this page does not widen an answer while writing it down.

    This log is not subject to payload reduction, and that is a decision rather than an oversight. Reduction empties the payloads of rows past the window. A redaction_log row holds no values to empty — a person id, a legal basis, a list of table names, and the reasoning — and it is the evidence that a person's request was honoured, which is the class of statement D-088 chose to keep for the life of the system when it chose to keep the skeleton. Reaching this table with reduction would delete the proof of an erasure in order to apply a policy about history, and those are not the same act.

    For the descriptive part of a person's record this log is the only surviving record, by design. The attribute rows are deleted; the audit entries that quoted their values are scrubbed by the same act (§2.2's mechanism two, which is the only direction in which a trail that must not be rewritten can still be erased); and the codes they pointed at stay live because a code belongs to the organization and not to the erased person (09-attributes.md §6 draws the two directions: retiring a code is an act about the vocabulary and keeps values, erasing a person is an act about the person and removes them). So redacted_tables naming person_attribute is the whole of what the system can honestly say — this person had descriptive data and it is gone — and it is enough, because the sentence the organization is entitled to keep is that it held a record and removed it, not what the record said.

    5. Integrity checks¶

    Invariants to verify

    • No longer a count: an imported membership with no end date. Two counts stood here — a membership carrying an import_batch_id with no valid_to, and its sibling, one whose batch was an events export — and both die with the column and the batch (D-190). The rule underneath them is untouched. An imported membership is never open-ended and never inferred from an attendance row; it ends with the calendar year the person was last seen in, and the value written is 1 January of the following year because every period here is half-open (D-169, D-093). What is gone is the report, and a hole whose report is gone should be written down rather than quietly unstated. Its shape: a membership row with valid_to empty whose audit_log row names the load's key. That is authority and not untidiness — an open membership satisfies is_current_member() and so walks through the permission gate (D-039) — and the answer to "who would notice" is: whoever reads the load's output, on the one occasion there will be a load's output to read. Nothing reads it on their behalf, and nothing in this design would tell them if it did (D-164).
    • Imported people who were later merged away. Count people whose record_origin is legacy_import and whose merge pointer is set. Non-zero is probably fine and worth an eye.
    • No longer a count: a batch left in a non-terminal state. This bullet policed runs that had stalled midway through six stages, and there are no stages, no statuses and no queue (D-190). The two bullets in this list that named the pipeline are emptied rather than deleted, and a later editor should leave them standing: the checks below them keep the positions the rest of the corpus counts them by, and an emptied bullet is a visible hole where a renumbering is an invisible one.
    • Audit rows whose actor names nothing. The trail has three ways to say who did this: a person, a registered integration (05e-keys-and-callers.md §14), and — for the writers that have no row of their own — a label from the list in 00-conventions §7a. The report runs over all three columns, because a version of it that looked only at actor_person_id and actor_label would count every integration write as a finding until whoever reads it stopped reading. What it no longer counts as a gap is the shape it was built to count. A row naming no person and no key is not a row that failed to answer who did this: §7a reads it as this system acting on its own, and the renewal sweep, payload reduction, the access trail's age-out, the bank statement pull, the messaging run and a named migration write rows in exactly that shape, correctly and by design (00-conventions §7). The messaging run is the newest of those writers (D-212): it authored what used to be called a renewal notice, because one label per kind of act is why the renewal sweep did not get the job — it expires memberships, and it now sends nothing. The age-out's row is new rather than always having been there, and it is one row per run rather than one per deleted row (D-184): before it, system:log-reduction was a name on a vocabulary and on no row at all. Two things are reported instead. (a) A row whose actor_label is not one of the labels §7a lists, including a row naming no person, no key and no label at all — the vocabulary is a documented list and not a table, so nothing in the database refuses a label from outside it and this count is the only thing that ever notices (D-125). A row that reaches the trail with nobody and nothing in it has not joined that list, which is the failure the list exists to catch. (b) A row on a write path that owed a person and named none, in the two shapes a reader can actually test: a transaction whose request context carried a current_person_id (06 §2.1 step 1) while actor_person_id stayed empty, and any audit row about one of the rows §7a names as having no empty to interpret — api_key.created_by is required and a grant's granted_by names a person, so a business row that cannot lawfully be authorless cannot have been written by a writer that is. What this check cannot catch, said because two rows a reader would want are not among them. A row naming a key and a person — the row a forensic reader most wants flagged — is never reported here. It is illegal, and the rule carried by the two columns is what refuses it (05e-keys-and-callers.md §16, D-121); a count over empty columns cannot see a row whose columns are full. A row naming a key that no longer exists is stopped before any count runs: audit_log.api_key_id is an ordinary reference and not one of the two reference-less keys, and no key row is hard-deleted (00-conventions §4). What stays uncounted, and uncounted everywhere in this section, is the pair no rule compares — a write audited against a key whose valid_to or revoked_at fell before the row's occurred_at, which leaves every column filled and this check, which reads emptiness rather than dates, silent.

    That silence is now a ruling rather than an oversight, and the reason matters more than the ruling (D-133). The window is not the obstacle and it was never honest to say it was: the three columns such a check would compare are api_key.valid_to, api_key.revoked_at and this table's own occurred_at, every one of them accounting skeleton, every one outliving the payload by decades. A report could be written over them tomorrow and would work in year thirty. What was decided is that it will not be written, which leaves the design without a guarantee it had been reading as though it had: nobody will be able to demonstrate that no write was ever audited against a key that had already ended, and no count anywhere will ever answer that question — not after 24 months, and not before them either. What this section does hold, and what the sentence above should never be read as replacing, is narrower and worth restating while it is being narrowed: every audited write left a row; that row's envelope — actor, table, record, moment — is intact for as long as the row exists; and the payload is emptied on schedule rather than lingering. Those three are checked. The fourth was wanted by nobody who read it, so it is stated as a rule with no report beneath it, which is the only remaining honest state — and 00a now says so in the same words this page does, because a register row claiming a report that no page has is the defect D-129 exists to catch. A reader who needs the fourth guarantee needs a different instrument than this table: the trail is a record of what happened to people, and what an ended key did after it ended is tracked on the key's own page (05e-keys-and-callers.md). - Tables whose write path does not audit. Take every table this system has, set the two trails aside — audit_log and access_log are the only tables the obligation excludes — and report any table that has a write path and produced no audit row. There is no maintained list of audited tables left to compare against: D-184 turned the obligation into a rule about writes, so one half of this comparison is the schema and the other is the code. Read this one carefully, because its meaning got worse rather than weaker. While a trigger existed, a table was audited by being named in a trigger definition, and forgetting one was a decision somebody made in a migration and could see. Now a table is audited because the code that writes it calls the audit entry point, so the rule and the code are two artifacts that must agree and nothing forces them to — which makes this report the entire control over the completeness of the audit trail, and the one check here whose finding must not wait for the monthly run. If it ever reports a table, the finding is not "some history is missing"; it is "every change to that table since it was added is unrecorded, and that period cannot be reconstructed later." Because the obligation is now a rule rather than a list, the assertion carries one enumerated list still: the paths that may delete. §2.1's carve-out names two, and a table-name comparison yields neither — erasure, which deletes person_attribute rows and audits nothing per row, and the access trail's age-out, which deletes from a table the obligation excludes and yet writes the one summary row. A delete reaching any other table is this report's finding even when that table audits its inserts and updates perfectly, and it is the finding a test should assert rather than a query, since nothing in the schema refuses the delete. - Redacted people with surviving personal data. Count redacted people whose names are not the redaction marker and who still have a current contact point whose value is not redacted; non-zero means an erasure was left half done. The count now reaches three more places, and the three fail in two different shapes. First, person_attribute: a redacted person with any attribute row is an erasure that did not happen, since those rows are deleted rather than overwritten (00-conventions §4). That is check 5 of 09-attributes.md §8 read from this side — the same assertion, not a second one. Second, and its shape is the opposite on purpose: event_answer. A redacted person with an answer row still carrying a value is an erasure that stopped short of the event tables. Those rows are not deleted — nothing about an event goes on a schedule, and an answer outlives its event indefinitely — so erasure overwrites: value emptied, and answered_at, answered_by and the row itself kept, because they were asked and they answered is the event's fact while this is what they said is the person's (D-140). value is the whole of the content here, since an answer is one typed value rather than a column per type (08-events.md §7) — there is no second field that survives an emptied value by holding the answer in another shape, which is the trap a check like this would otherwise set for itself. event_registration is counted by nothing here, and that is not an oversight: whether a person came is organizational history rather than something to withdraw, 00 §8's item 2 says so by name, and a check that reported surviving registrations would be reporting the design working. Third, and a different assertion, which only exists because values are logged on purpose: for a redacted person, no audit_log row about one of their person_attribute changes may still carry content. Content means value, value_option_id or recorded_note inside old_values or new_values; person_id, attribute_definition_id and source are not content and survive the scrub deliberately, because they are the only way this check can still find the rows after the erasure deleted the business ones — an audit row keeps its table_name and record_id when the row they named is gone, and its person_id is what ties it back to the person who asked to be forgotten. Read this check correctly and it has exactly one meaning: the scrub did not run. Before an erasure, a payload full of descriptive values is the design working (§2.2); afterwards it is the thing the person asked for the system to stop having. Note what the check structurally cannot see — a copy that is present, readable, and perfectly legal, held by somebody whose permission is graded below its contents — because that failure leaves no rows to count; it is the guard, and the guard is reviewed by a person. This check, check 5, check 7 and check 8 are the four here whose findings cannot wait for the monthly run — three because a value that outlives the rule about it is not something a schedule notices, and check 8 because it is the same failure wearing the other trail's clothes. Nothing in this design sends any of them to anybody, so "cannot wait" names a duty to open these four rather than a message that arrives on its own (D-164). A redaction property that is reported once a month is a redaction property that fails between the reports, and unlike a missing audit row, a value that outlives its erasure is not something anyone notices until a regulator asks what the table holds. - An audit row past the payload window that still carries a payload. Count audit_log rows whose occurred_at is older than the retention window and whose old_values or new_values still holds anything. This is check 6's mirror, and merging the two is the way to lose both. Check 6 asks whether an erasure ran, fires on one person's rows, and fails as a right not honoured; this one asks whether the calendar ran, fires on everybody's rows alike, and fails as a policy not applied (§2.2's three operations, which fail in different directions). A system passes one while failing the other in both directions, so they are two queries, not one query with two clauses. Its finding cannot wait for the monthly run, and the reason is the one §2.2 gives: reduction's failure mode is not running. Nothing about the trail looks wrong from inside it — a reduced row and a row that escaped reduction both still answer who, what, when, which is the half that was supposed to survive — so the only difference between them is a payload nobody looks at until somebody with audit.view opens a history screen and finds a child's allergy list still sitting there past the window it was meant to have left. Count the rows, do not sample them: a job that stopped after the newest partition keeps the oldest rows full and looks entirely healthy to a sample. And read the window from the same place the job does, because a check holding one value and a job holding another reports clean forever, which is the exact failure this check exists to prevent and the one it can cause by itself.

    • An access_log row older than the window that is still there. Count rows whose occurred_at predates the window and which still exist. This is check 7's sibling and deliberately not its copy. Check 7 counts payloads that should have been emptied out of rows that stay; this one counts rows that should be gone, on the other trail. Same clock, opposite operation — and merging them into one "anything past 24 months" report is how both are lost, because the two findings are repaired by two different jobs, one of which is an update and the other a delete, and because a single count cannot say which half of the retention policy stopped. The direction of failure is the same, and it is the reason this finding cannot wait for the monthly run: a job that stopped leaves a trail that looks entirely sound from inside it, since nothing about a growing table reads as a promise broken. The only difference between it and a healthy one is rows accumulating past the date the design said they would not exist, and the person who notices is the one asked what the system holds about them. Count, do not sample, for exactly the reason check 7 gives: a job that stopped after the newest partition keeps the oldest rows and passes any sample drawn from the top. And read the window from the same configuration the job reads — this is the second check that can be permanently satisfied by a disagreement between two copies of one number, and it is the failure mode each of them would cause alone. What it cannot do is demonstrate that anything was ever deleted. It counts what remains, which is the only thing a count over a table can see, so a job that deletes from the wrong table passes it; the standing guarantee here is not this report but the one on the other side of it, that the change trail is never pruned (05c §9). This delete is one of the two named exceptions to 00 §4, and the only one driven by a calendar rather than by a person asking — and since D-184 it is also the only delete in the system that leaves a row in the other trail: one summary row per run, naming system:log-reduction, the count and the window.

    • A change the trail never saw: an audited row whose newest audit row predates its own updated_at. For every table 06 §2.1 obligates, take each row's updated_at and the newest occurred_at among the audit rows naming that table and record_id, and report the rows where the change is newer than the trail's knowledge of it. It exists because of the one write this design performs outside the application: the recovery in the break-glass procedure un-suspends an account or re-issues a credential by hand, produces no audit row, and leaves a row that is live, referenced and invisible to every other check in this section (domain/02-actors.md §3, D-126). It can see the recovery at all because account is a table the obligation covers, and every table the application writes owes an audit row on every insert and update (06 §2.1). The set it walks is no longer a recommendation waiting to be confirmed: §6's first bullet is closed, the scope is every table the application writes except the two trails, and nothing stands between that rule and this query. What is left as its ceiling is this bullet's own sentence about updated_at, which is a property of the application rather than of this document. Nothing in the schema distinguishes a legitimate recovery from somebody editing a row they had no business editing, so this is a report and not a guard, like every item above it. What it cannot catch. An edit that also inserts its own audit row by hand — the trail is append-only to the application, not to whoever holds the database. And an edit that leaves updated_at alone, which is the quieter half of the blind spot: 00-conventions §7 is explicit that nothing maintains updated_at unless somebody says so, so this report is exactly as wide as whatever maintains that column, and an application that sets it only on its own screens narrows it to those screens. What it does not lose to payload reduction: reduction empties old_values and new_values and keeps the row (D-088), and a reduced row keeps its occurred_at — so 24 months after the values went, this check still has both timestamps to compare. Coverage outlives content by design, which is the one part of this list that gets no worse with age.

    Check 5 is worth automating as a test, not just a nightly query — a test that reads the table list in these documents and asserts, for every table except the two trails, that whatever writes it reaches the audit entry point, since that is the nearest thing left to what asking the database used to answer. The assertion lost its list and kept its shape. It no longer asks is this table on the audited list — there is no such list to be off — it asks does every write path to this table write the row, and it carries the two enumerated delete paths beside it, because no enumeration of table names yields either. A new table whose writer was never wired is a silent, permanent hole in the audit trail — and it will be discovered only when someone needs the history that was never recorded.

    6. Open issues¶

    • ✅ Closed — which tables must be audited: every table the application writes (D-184). The bullet asked for somebody to read a list and say yes, and the answer instead retired the list: one audit_log row per insert and update, on every path, from the day a table exists, with the two trails themselves the only tables outside it — audit_log and access_log are the record, and a record of a record would have to be recorded in its turn. A confirmation was worth asking for even though §2.1 already claimed the answer, because a scope written as everything except the list we never finished is a scope a new table falls out of by nobody noticing, and check 5's assertion that the list and the write paths agree had nothing correct to agree with. The cost is storage, and the growth is in the skeleton of a small fixed row rather than in a payload, since payloads empty at 24 months (D-088).
    • ✅ Closed — retention period for audit_log: rows forever, payloads 24 months, and nobody is left to rule on it (D-088, owned as a policy by D-205). It used to read "retention period before archival to cold storage; legal input required", which got the question wrong in two ways at once: it assumed the answer was a storage tier rather than a shape, and it assumed one clock rather than two. The policy is rows forever, payloads 24 months, 12 months as the floor a window may be set to, enforced by the job and the check in §2.2 and §5 rather than by an archive nobody queries. The half this bullet kept calling open is not open, because nothing is going to rule on it: there is no legal review of this design and there will not be one. Whether the indefinite retention of named membership and fee_obligation rows is lawful is therefore decided the way everything else here is decided — chosen by the person designing the system because the alternative falsifies the organization's own history — and the risk sits where the basis used to sit: this is the one class of row here that outlives every safeguard in 00-conventions §8, and nothing outside the design vouches for it. Five policies stand as decisions of the person designing the system, and they are the five D-205 lists: these rows kept with no end date; the audit payloads emptied at 24 months; the access_log rows deleted at 24 months (D-145); that a child may hold an account with no guardian's answer recorded anywhere (D-166, domain/02 §2); and what an invoice must contain and how long it is kept (D-198). The second and third are this system's own clocks; the first and fourth are the two a data subject asks about, and the fourth is the only one of the five that this design answers by not holding a record at all; the fifth is decided by saying what the row is — an invoice row is the record that a document exists, and whatever must be printed beyond what this design holds is met outside it.
    • Whether audit_log should be partitioned by month from the start. Recommend yes, and the reasons changed when the payload window arrived. The old reason was "the write volume and the multi-decade horizon", which is still true of the rows — they are the permanent half — but the bulk of a row is its payloads, and those are now on a 24-month clock. Monthly partitions serve the reduction job too: a partition entirely past the window can be reduced as one unit rather than as a sweep across a table that grows for the life of the system. The open part is which the deployment does first, not whether the horizon justifies it.
    • ✅ Closed — the end-date inference rule for imported membership periods (§3). An imported membership ends with the calendar year of the last period in which the person appears: their last day as a member is 31 December, and the value written into valid_to is 1 January of the following year, because every period here is half-open and current membership is read as valid_to after today — a literal 31 December in that column would end every imported row one day early and look correct in every report that does not name the day (D-169). The boundary was never missing, and that is the part worth keeping: D-047 put it in membership_type as expiry_month and expiry_day, and that row's own note says the organizational year runs with the calendar — the four words this bullet asked for were sitting in the row the date is read from, which is also why the day is read from those two columns rather than compiled into a job as a literal. Sharper, not smaller, since D-093 put memberships back on the receiving end of it: the inferred end date is not the end of a record nobody reads, it is the valid_to of a row that gates authority, so the year boundary is the value standing between the import and the permission gate. (When this bullet was written the row in question was a unit_participation; D-097 removed that table, which sharpens the point rather than softening it — there is now exactly one period an inferred date can be ending, and it is the consequential one.) What the pipeline's removal did not touch is the date itself. It is a rule about the rows a script writes now, not about a parse step inside a stage, and the value is read from expiry_month and expiry_day on the membership_type row wherever the load computes it. What died with the stages is the report that would have noticed a load ignoring it (§5's first bullet), so this bullet is the only place left where the date is written down in full.
    • ✅ Resolved — whether the import should create a membership at all for a historical period, or only a unit_participation (§3). Settled by D-090 on the shape of the corpus rather than on the merits of the two options — the corpus carried no payment data, so the exception arm of "participation unless there is payment evidence" had an empty set to fire on — and then narrowed by D-093, which is what this bullet now records: the corpus turned out to state membership periods, so a bounded membership may be imported, both ends dated, data_confidence = 'low'. What the resolution actually settled was never "no imported memberships" but "no inferred ones", and that half still holds in both directions: never open-ended, never from an attendance row. Superseded here and kept visible: this bullet used to end "participation only, always; membership rows come only from the current register."
    • ✅ Closed — exact redaction scope: which fields are personal data vs. organizational history (§4). The bullet read "requires legal review", and that review is not coming (D-205), so the line this page already draws is the policy — written by the person designing the system, with its risk named in the retention bullet above rather than a basis claimed below it. D-083 removed the category question (there is no specially-graded subset of the person record), and D-088 drew the line the design keeps: a person's names, gender, contact points and identifiers, every person_attribute row and every event_answer.value are erased; membership, fee_obligation, payment, event_registration and the anonymised person row are organizational history and survive. Nothing is left asking which of those two lists a field belongs to: a new column about a person joins one of them at the design table, and redacted_tables in §4 is where the choice becomes visible — once an erasure has run and the table it reached is named there.
    • ✅ Deleted — whether person.notes should exist at all. It does not, and the burden this item was written about turned out to point the other way (D-191). The column was kept off the redactor's list, because naming a column as an exception is the field-name rule D-083 removed — and the consequence nobody drew was that a redaction left the notes intact: erasure overwrites names and contacts, deletes every person_attribute row, empties an event answer, and did not touch the free text this same page admits may hold a diagnosis. Free text about a person is now an attribute definition's value, and an attribute row is the one thing this design deletes on erasure, so the deletion made erasure stronger rather than weaker. What still exists is free text about a person that is not a fact of them, and the page says so rather than implying the record is now clean of it: a roster row's note, an account's status_reason and status_note, and a field_conflict holding the two values a merge could not choose between. The boundary is that the person row holds no free text, while plenty of rows about a person do. Round 25 then ruled on three rows of that kind and no more: membership.notes, membership_request.notes and payment.notes stay where they are and erasure empties them, in the emptied shape §4 now lists (D-215). The four named just above are not among them — the answer named three columns, and this page does not widen an answer while writing it down — so they stay named here, and whether they join is K-16.

    The register holds seven questions, K-11 through K-17, and one of them is this page's own — K-16, whether the free-text columns named in the bullet above join the three that erasure now empties. That is the only item above pointing into the register, and the habit it does not change: round 24 emptied the register and left the asking on this page, so an item here closes by having its answer written into the bullet above, never by being handed back — which is exactly how the person.notes bullet and the three notes columns above were closed, in place, with their decisions named.
