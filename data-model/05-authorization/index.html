    Data Model — Authorization¶

    permission, role, role_holder, selector_type, audience, audience_operand, access_grant, access_log.

    This is the direct answer to your question about system-wide and per-item permissions. Design rationale: ../domain/06-authorization-model.md.

    1. The shape of the answer¶

    permission ─────────────── the atomic capability.       'membership.request.approve'
                            + where it may be granted:  applies_to  unit | unit_and_item
                            + read or write:            right_kind

access_grant ───────────── THE ONE TABLE THAT GRANTS.   grantee (person | audience),
                                                       scope ('unit' | exactly one item),
                                                       one code, effect (allow | deny),
                                                       period

role ───────────────────── an office, conferring nothing.     'treasurers'
role_holder ────────────── who holds it, for a period.        person X, 'treasurers',
                                                              2026-03-01 → open

selector_type ──────────── the language: every operand that   'ROLE', 'MEMBERS', 'PLACED',
                          may appear in an expression.          'AUDIENCE'
                          Seeded, is_system, read-only.

audience ───────────────── a set a grant aims at, nameable     MEMBERS * ROLE(treasurers)
                          by code from another audience.
audience_operand ───────── one node of that expression        (a tree, not a string; an
                          — an AUDIENCE leaf doubles as       AUDIENCE leaf is also the
                          the reference edge to another tree)  edge to another audience)

guardianship_permission ── authority over a PERSON.     (04-relationships.md)
access_log ─────────────── what was decided, and by what authority.

    One table grants. access_grant is the only place authority is written (D-005), plus one table over a single person: guardianship_permission (§10, channel guardianship). Everything else on this page decides whom a grant reaches, which has never been the same thing — a role holder, a group member and an audience operand are all sets of people, and being in one is worth exactly what the grant aimed at it says. That sentence was already true of audiences before this round; round 10 made it true of roles too, which is the whole of D-100. One of these tables is gone: the hand-typed names that used to sit in audience_member are written in the expression, as PERSON(a) + PERSON(b), in the place every other set is written (D-105).

    And the rule that answers every check (D-099):

    allowed when some live allow applies, and no live deny applies.

    That is the entire precedence rule. "Live" means the row has started as of the checked date, has not ended on it, and has not been revoked; "applies" is decided per channel in §10. There is no level to reach, no rank to compare, and no nearer grant that beats a further one — the ladder and locality were deleted in the same breath, because locality only ever ordered allows against each other and a boolean answer has nothing to order. What survives of the old ordering is deny's absoluteness, which was D-032's first half and is now its entire content.

    The rule that used to govern all of these tables, and where it went. This section opened with "authority over a unit originates at that unit and cannot be claimed from outside it — not by a parent unit, and not by the organization", and named scope_exposure as the only crossing. Withdrawn by D-097, and the withdrawal is precise: D-031 was not weakened, it was deprived of its subject. There is one unit, it is the organization, and it is not a row — so there is no "outside it" to be stopped from, and every permission is held either at the unit or on an item. The sentence is kept here, struck through in spirit, because a reader who arrives from an older draft will look for the rule and should find the reason it is gone rather than an empty space. What of its reasoning still holds is carried by two things that were never about units: deny is absolute (§10), and the ownership and guardianship channels answer for a person rather than for a body. What is lost is stated once in §5 and in D-097, and is not argued away there or here.

    The two tables that used to be one idea at two granularities, and what they are now. D-010 read role_assignment and access_grant as the same mechanism at different sizes and gave them a shared scope model and a shared resolver. Both halves are reversed — the scope model because D-097 left one scope and role_holder therefore has no scope columns at all, and the shared resolver because there is no role channel to share (§10). What they have in common now is the temporal pattern, like every dated row in this system:

    role_holder access_grant
    Says this person holds this office in this period this grantee may, or may not, do this one thing here
    Carries authority No — confers nothing (§3) Yes; it is the only thing that does
    Scope None. A role is held at the unit, because there is nowhere else The unit, or exactly one item
    Decides Whom a grant naming ROLE(x) reaches Everything, for one code
    Typical row "is the treasurer, from March" "may read this event's answers"
    Rows per broad capability One One per code — a broad office is a couple of dozen of them

    The row count is the price, and it was paid deliberately (D-099). One grant per code is what buys "what exactly does this person have?" answered by reading rows, and it costs the question "what does manage give me on this event?", which has no answer because there is no such word.

    2. permission¶

    Fields

    Field Kind Notes
    id integer identity Surrogate key.
    code text, required, unique The stable identifier — membership.request.approve. The code never changes; display_name can.
    resource text, required What the permission is about — membership_request.
    action text, required What may be done to it — approve.
    applies_to enum: unit, unit_and_item New, D-099. Where this code may be granted at all: at the unit only, or at the unit and on an item. Checked when the grant is saved, never at check time — see applies_to below.
    right_kind enum: read, write New, D-099. Exists for exactly one customer: the guardianship channel's none / view / write (D-074). Say the one customer out loud — see right_kind below.
    namespace text, optional system, or absent. What it still does, and what it stopped doing, is its own subsection below.
    description text, required What it means, for whoever has to read it.
    display_name text, required Human label for the permission editor.
    is_system boolean Default false. True: seeded by the platform, cannot be deleted or have its code changed.
    is_assignable boolean Default true. False: a permission no grant may name directly — a deprecated code kept only so historical audit rows still resolve to something readable. Before D-068 this also flagged wildcard .admin codes; there are none now.
    created_at timestamp, audit time

    Deleted from this table by round 10: requires_scope. Its job — "a unit-level permission cannot be granted organization-wide by mistake" — was about which unit a permission was held at, and D-097 left one unit and a handful of item types. The question it asked ("may this code be granted on an event at all?") is real and is answered by applies_to, which is the same idea with the hierarchy taken out of it. Nobody enforces requires_scope now, because nothing enforces it: applies_to does its job and does it at a different moment, on the save rather than in the resolver.

    Rules

    • code is well-formed: two or more dot-separated segments of lowercase letters and underscores.
    • resource equals the first dot-separated segment of code, so the code's prefix is never a second, drifting copy of the resource column. One exemption, deliberate: codes beginning system., whose resource is the thing being administered rather than the word system. There used to be a second exemption for the dependent namespace, whose codes had two meaningful segments (dependent.medical.view); those codes are deleted (D-074) and the exemption went with them, so nothing may be added under a namespace prefix again without reopening this rule.
    • code is unique.
    • applies_to and right_kind are both required. A required enum with two values is cheap; a nullable one whose null-ness silently means something is how the guardianship channel would have acquired an answer that depends on a value nobody typed.
    • A code seeded as system. is seeded applies_to = unit. This is not decoration; see What namespace does now.

    There is no wildcard permission¶

    A check for X.y is satisfied by an exact allow of X.y, or by system.admin, which is checked outright and is the only permission meaning everything.

    A check for permission 'X.y' is satisfied by:
    - an exact allow of 'X.y', OR
    - an allow of 'system.admin'   (NULL-namespace permissions only)

    There are no <resource>.admin codes. The convention that every resource should expose one, matched by prefix in the resolver, was reversed by D-068, which also removed the resolver branch that implemented it. "Can do everything about events" is an office composed from the thirteen event.* codes — which is to say, a role some grants name, each grant naming one code (D-052) — because that composition is runtime data the organization edits, and a wildcard was the code guessing at it instead.

    The cost is the one D-007 existed to prevent, and it is accepted: a new narrow permission is not automatically covered by the offices that should imply it. Integrity check 13 (permissions no live grant names) is the net the wildcard used to be. It fails closed.

    is_assignable = false exists for permissions that only make sense as part of a bundle — a deprecated narrow permission kept for historical audit readability, for instance. With levels gone, "part of a bundle" means part of a role's set of grants, and nothing else bundles.

    applies_to: where a code may be granted, and when that is asked¶

    This column is your own observation written as data: event.create and membership.manage are nonsense on an event. unit means the code may be granted at the unit and nowhere else; unit_and_item means it may be granted at the unit or on one item of a registered type.

    It is checked when the grant is saved, not when a permission is checked (D-099). A grant of event.create written on one event is refused, with the reason in the error: an event cannot be created twice, so a creation permission attached to a thing that already exists answers no question — and a refusal at save time is one human conversation, where the same shape discovered at check time is a rule inside the resolver that every future reader has to re-derive.

    What enforces it is the write path, and the direction of its failure is worth knowing. No database rule spans permission and access_grant columns, so integrity check 3 (§11) reports any row that got through — a row there means the check was skipped, not that a constraint was disabled. And because the rule sits on the save, a check that names an item for a unit-only code is not refused by it: the mismatched shape cannot be stored, so the check simply gets the unit's answer. That is a caller bug rather than a data bug, and §13 registers the question of whether the API should refuse it out loud.

    right_kind: a column with exactly one customer¶

    Saying so is cheaper than discovering it later (D-099). D-074 fixes a guardian's rights at three values — none, view, write — and with the level ladder gone the guardianship channel had nothing left to compare a checked permission against. This column is that thing, and it has no other consumer anywhere in the design:

    Guardian's right_level Covers
    none Nothing.
    view Codes marked read.
    write Codes marked read and codes marked write.

    The alternative was to store permission codes on the guardianship row, and D-074 refused it on the grounds that a volunteer cannot maintain a 74-code checklist per child. A two-valued column on the catalog side keeps that refusal intact: the organization grades each code once, when it is written, rather than per child, forever.

    A new code is seeded write unless someone marks it read, because write is the narrower guardian reach: an unconsidered new code is then unreachable by a guardian with view, which is the direction this model fails in everywhere else. This is a seeding convention derived from D-074's fail-closed posture rather than a ruling in D-099, and §11 has no check for it — a code mis-marked read is visible to a human reading the catalog and invisible to the database, which is worth knowing before trusting it.

    What namespace does now, and what it no longer does¶

    namespace What it means Example
    absent An ordinary code. membership.request.approve
    system A code about the system itself. It is seeded applies_to = unit, so no grant on an item can carry it, and the resolver no longer branches on the prefix. system.permission.edit

    There used to be a third value, and this subsection was about it. dependent. prefixed every guardian capability, and the resolver dispatched on the prefix: a code beginning dependent. went down the guardianship channel, everything else went down the ordinary path. D-074 deleted all eighteen of those codes, and with them the only thing the namespace column was being used to route on.

    So the namespace column no longer selects a channel, and the guardianship channel is now selected by something the namespace could never express: whether the action is about a different person from the one performing it. A parent editing their child's address is checked under person.address.edit — an ordinary, un-prefixed code — and reaches the guardianship channel anyway, because the subject of the act is not the actor. See §10. The old prefix rule silently missed exactly that case: a guardian acting under a person.* code looked like a person acting on themselves.

    What the column was also guaranteeing, and who guarantees it now. The surviving sentence in the old design read "a system. permission is resolved against role assignments and never against an item grant", and it was load-bearing: no one could hand someone authority over the permission system by sharing an item with them. Its mechanism died twice over — there is no role channel (D-100) and no item-scope path it needs excluding, because applies_to = unit already refuses the grant at save time. So the guarantee survives as a seeding rule plus integrity check 3, and the layer is named because the difference matters: it is no longer structural, and a system. code seeded unit_and_item by someone in a hurry would put the old hole back with nothing in the schema to catch it. A new namespace remains a data change rather than a resolver branch; it just has one tenant and no routing left.

    3. role — an office¶

    Fields

    Field Kind Notes
    id integer identity Surrogate key.
    code text, required, unique among roles, and unique across roles and groups registrar, treasurers, counselors. The cross-table half of the rule is §8's namespace rule; a single constraint cannot span two tables, so the write path carries it (§8, Names are one namespace).
    name text, required What the office is called on screen.
    description text, optional
    is_system boolean Default false. True: seeded — cannot be deleted, and its code cannot be changed. False: an ordinary office the organization created and may delete.
    sort_order integer, required Default 0.
    created_at timestamp, audit time
    updated_at timestamp, audit time
    updated_by reference to person Who last changed it.

    Deleted with their mechanisms, in one list, because D-081 requires each one to be answered:

    Gone With it Who answers the question now
    scope_required "is this office held org-wide or at a unit?" Nobody — the question has no subject. D-097 left one scope, so a role is always held at the unit (§4 has no scope columns).
    requires_membership the membership gate, default-on, enforced in the resolver (D-039) The expression, per grant: MEMBERS * ROLE(x) gates on a current membership, ROLE(x) alone does not (§8, §10). The posture reversed from default-on to opt-in, and §11's report is the control.
    max_term_months the prefilled term, and with it the "expiry is the default" nudge Nothing. Nothing forces a holder row to end and the report is the control (§4, D-071); the prefilled date was doing a job the report also does, and it went with the scope columns it was calibrated against.
    is_delegatable the refusal to let an office's authority be delegated (domain/06 §9) Nothing about roles. Delegation of authority survives only on a grant row (is_delegated + delegated_from), where the grant's own expiry is the control (§6).

    role is runtime data, not a fixed vocabulary. The organization creates offices, names them, fills them, and deletes the ones nobody ever held. Two rows ship with the platform, and the list is deliberately short: counselors, marked is_system (D-100; a permanent, organization-wide population is the opposite of D-098's definition of a group, and the table is what decides the sensitivity that protects the set). §12 states what is seeded and what is starter material.

    role has no valid_from / valid_to, deliberately (D-053). A role is a definition — a named office — and a definition does not come into and out of force. It is valid all the time, for as long as the row exists.

    The period belongs on the holder row. role_holder.valid_from / valid_to is where "this person holds this office from March until December" lives (§4). The distinction is between what the office is and who holds it: the first is not a historical fact, the second is.

    This removes the whole retirement mechanism. There is no role.retire permission, no resolver check on the role's period, and no report of roles expiring while still held. A role the organization no longer uses is one nobody holds any more.

    role_permission is deleted, and the office confers nothing¶

    The codes an office carries are the live grants whose audience names ROLE(x) (D-100). That is not a relabelling; it moves the definition of an office's power out of a bundle table and into grant rows that are individually visible, individually dated, individually auditable, and individually revocable. What it buys is the question the old design could not answer cleanly — "what may a treasurer do?" — which is now a query over rows a screen can show rather than a walk through a table nobody reads. This is where D-068 was already going — roles are the bundle, said of <resource>.admin — and it went the rest of the way: roles and audiences are the bundle, and nothing else is.

    Three things the old section argued, and where each stands:

    • ~~"Editing a role's permissions takes effect immediately, and that is the sharpest edge in the whole authorization system."~~ Withdrawn by D-100, and the de-escalation is worth naming rather than skipping. role.edit used to be a bulk privilege change wearing the clothes of a configuration screen, defended only by the audit trail because there was nothing to check before the fact. It now edits a name, a description and a sort order. The sharp edge moved to role.assign: adding one person to treasurers inherits every grant that names ROLE(treasurers), including ones written later, by other people, for reasons the person doing the assigning has never seen. That is the same invisibility §8 records for composition, arriving through the office door instead of the expression door, and it is why role.* stays the most sensitive code family in the catalog (🔒🔒) and why §11's report of ROLE(...) grants written without MEMBERS is worth reading.
    • ~~"A role that has ever been assigned cannot be deleted."~~ Same rule, new table, same three reasons — role_holder.role_id is a foreign key and D-003 forbids taking the history with the row; "what authority did this person have in 2019?" must survive the office being abolished, which is exactly when someone asks; and an is_system role must survive regardless, because deleting the row some grants name would silently widen or narrow whatever they aim at. The guard runs row by row, in this order, and both refusals are absolute:
    • any role_holder row references the role — refuse, with a message in terms the operator can act on: this office has holders, end the holder rows instead, so the history survives;
    • otherwise, is_system — refuse outright. A role that clears both was created by mistake and never held, and deleting it is legitimate.
    • ~~"No permission inheritance between roles."~~ Kept, because the reason was never about bundles. A unit_leader does not get the assistant_leader permissions by being implicitly senior: inheritance chains produce authorization answers nobody can predict by reading the configuration, which is what makes such systems undebuggable. Composition is available and is visible instead — an expression says ROLE(x) + ROLE(y), in the grant, where whoever reads the grant sees it (§8).

    role is registered in grant_scope_type, so an office is an item (D-100): "these two people may manage the counselors roster" is a grant of role.assign on one role, which is D-098's move applied symmetrically. Without it the only way to say that is a grant at the unit, which reaches every office — the exact over-reach this table's sensitivity exists to avoid.

    Why a role and a group are two tables when they are technically the same shape is answered where the difference bites — D-100 puts it in one line: the difference decides who may edit the membership, and one table would merge those two permissions into one. role.* is the most sensitive family in the catalog (🔒🔒); writing a group's roster is person_group.member.manage (⚠). One table with a kind column gives a single code that both adds a child to the Thursday group and puts the holder into the office whose grants reach everything. The groups themselves are person_group, in 02-organization.md — this document describes only the selector that reads them.

    4. role_holder — who holds an office¶

    role_assignment becomes role_holder (D-100). The rename is the small half; the deletion of its scope columns and of the resolver's role channel is the half that changes how an authorization question gets answered (§10).

    Fields

    Field Kind Notes
    id integer identity Surrogate key.
    person_id reference to person, required Who holds the office. One person, never a rule — F-12 is closed by D-100 and its answer is no, for the reason the register always gave: a derived holder list cannot answer "who was treasurer in 2023", and an office record that cannot answer that is not an office record.
    role_id reference to role, required
    valid_from date, required Default today.
    valid_to date, optional Absent = open-ended. Nothing forces it to end — see below.
    granted_by reference to person, required Who appointed this person. Kept, and not the provenance column this section deletes elsewhere — see What granted_by records and what audit_log records, immediately below. The arrow of this column goes to person and never to audit_log: it is a fact about the holder row, readable by anyone who may read the office, and reconstructing it from the trail would put the answer behind audit.view.
    granted_at timestamp, audit time Set when the row was written. Redundant with created_at in the ordinary case and kept because an appointment can be entered retrospectively, which is the only case where the two differ and the one where the distinction matters.
    note text, optional Why, in the writer's words. The only place the reason survives the audit trail's payload window (D-088).
    created_at timestamp, audit time
    updated_at timestamp, audit time
    updated_by reference to person

    Gone with the table's rename: scope_type and scope_id (D-097 — "a role is always held at the unit; there is nothing else to hold it at"), delegation_of and the re-validation of a delegation's source (§10), and the ended_by / ended_at / ended_reason_code / ended_note quartet. The last of those needs its "who answers now", because it is history, not configuration: the audit trail records who closed a holder row and when, which is the same question the columns answered, and note carries the why on the row that opens. What is genuinely lost is the typed reason code — a ended_reason_code vocabulary that could be counted. Nothing counted it, and D-098 refused the same shape one table over for the same reason: eighteen ticks without a receipt.

    What granted_by records, and what audit_log records — the distinction that keeps one column and deletes four. The two look like duplicates and are answers to different questions, and this section initially got it wrong in the direction of deleting too much, so the line is worth drawing properly. audit_log records an act: that a particular person, in a particular session, from a particular address, asked to appoint someone, and whether they were allowed to. It is the record of the writing. granted_by records a fact about the row: who appointed this person, which is a property of the office holding in the same way valid_from is. Who is entitled to say is the audit's question; who said it is the row's.

    Two reasons the fact cannot live only in the trail, and the first is decisive.

    • The trail's payloads do not last (D-088); the before-and-after images are emptied after 24 months. "Who put her in charge of the money?" has to be answerable in year five, from the same screen that shows when the appointment started, and an answer that requires audit.view, a date range and an intact payload is not an answer to a question about the current officer.
    • The row's readers are not the trail's readers. role_holder is read by whoever administers offices; the trail is read by whoever holds audit.view 🔒🔒, and D-086 graded that deliberately. Making the ordinary screen reach into the audit trail to show an ordinary field either widens audit.view or leaves the field blank, and neither is acceptable.

    The same argument already decides access_grant.granted_by the same way, and nobody proposes reconstructing that one from the trail — which is the tell that this is a pattern rather than a local call, and the reason ended_by / ended_at / ended_reason_code / ended_note can be deleted while granted_by cannot. What separates them is not "an actor column" versus "a provenance column"; it is whether the value is a property of the row that still exists. An ending describes a row that nobody is looking at any more, and the question "who closed this" is a question about an act, which is the trail's subject. An appointment describes a holding somebody is looking at right now. Consistency here is about which question is being asked, not about how many actor columns a table has.

    And one consequence for the diagram, because it was got wrong once. The relationship drawn from granted_by points at person. There is no edge from role_holder to audit_log and there must never be one: the trail is written about the act, by the same transaction, and it is not something the holder row refers to or depends on.

    Rules

    • valid_to is either absent or strictly after valid_from.
    • At most one open holder row for a given person and role. There is no scope term left to distinguish two concurrent holdings, because there is no second scope. A person may have been treasurer three times, and be treasurer once.
    • Rows for the same person and role may not overlap, counted over all rows and not only the open ones, half-open so a row ending on a day and one beginning that same day do not collide. Dating rows imports this invariant set whether the author wants it or not — it is D-098's argument for the identical rule on person_group_member, D-002, and F-10 shape 1, which says a hand-written migration must emit it and the ORM will not.
    • A person who left the office in 2020 and returned in 2024 is two rows, never one row edited in place, because the edit destroys the answer to "was she treasurer in 2019?" — and that is the question this table exists to answer. Moving from one office to another closes one row and opens another.

    Indexes

    • over open rows only, by role — the read that answers "who is in ROLE(treasurers) today", which is now an operand of a grant rather than a tier of the resolver, and runs on every check that names the office;
    • over open rows only, by person — the read that answers "what offices does this person hold", for the screen where someone is about to be given one more.

    Nothing forces a holder row to end, and the report is the control¶

    This is D-071's posture, transferred intact to role_holder, and the transfer is honest only because the gate it used to lean on is gone. The old argument ran: nothing forces an assignment to end, but the membership gate means a lapse silently withholds it anyway, so the leaver-with-access report was tidying rather than defending. That second half no longer exists. A holder row that nobody closes now authorizes for as long as a grant naming the office lives, and the only thing between an overdue treasurer and a written authority is:

    • the report — open holder rows, oldest first, with the grants each office is named by, which is the same reading that used to be "assignments that should have been closed and were not";
    • role_holder.valid_to where a human typed one, and §13 keeps the question of whether it should be mandatory for high-authority offices; and
    • the audience expression, which is where a lapse now matters: MEMBERS * ROLE(x) stops reaching a lapsed member the day the membership ends, and ROLE(x) never noticed.

    The second failure mode is the one D-100 named rather than buried, and it is the mirror image of the first. A holder row that outlives the person is a person with authority they should not have; a grant written as ROLE(x) with no MEMBERS authorizes a lapsed member silently, forever, and fails closed-looking so nobody notices. Integrity check 6 (§11) is the standing report of exactly that shape, and the seeded grants carry MEMBERS so that the path of least repetition is the safe path.

    5. scope_exposure — deleted by D-097¶

    This section is deleted. Its number is kept so that §6 and onward keep the anchors other pages link to, and its content is not replaced by anything: this is the one place in this document where the honest summary is lost, not replaced.

    What the table was. scope_exposure was the only mechanism by which authority crossed a unit boundary downward, and it existed to implement D-031 — authority over a unit originates at that unit and cannot be claimed from outside it. The row was keyed on the covered unit, which made the covered unit the grantor, and pointed at a specific assignment rather than a role, so a unit approved a person-in-a-role rather than a role that any future holder would inherit. scope.expose was deliberately a narrow code with no wildcard above it, because "may administer this unit" silently including "may hand a parent unit authority over this unit's records" was, in the words of the section that shipped it, the single most consequential act available at unit scope carried as an implication.

    What it protected, in the one sentence worth keeping. Access to a pack's children had to be granted by that pack — by people who recruited the leader, who can be asked, and who chose the person being let in. That property is gone and nothing in the remaining model reproduces it (D-097). A permission held at the unit reaches every member, and person.view was already the widest read permission in the system (D-083, D-086). What is left of the safeguarding story is the same three things D-095 was left with — a permission held by a named adult, an audit trail whose payloads expire after two years (D-088), and a report of who holds the write — minus the part where the named adult was somebody the body chose.

    Why there is no replacement, rather than an unfinished one. With one unit there is no "outside it" to be stopped from: every permission is held either at the unit or on an item, and the unit's people are the only people there are. A mechanism that lets a subordinate grant access to itself has no subordinate to run it. The route back is a decision with a reason attached — most plausibly "the organization has grown past one center's visibility" — and that decision would have to re-argue D-031 from its own premises, because the table that made it work no longer exists.

    Deleted along with the table, each with who answers it now. scope.expose and scope.exposure.review leave the catalog; the direction-of-authority check leaves the write path, and nothing replaces it because there is no direction left for a row to have; the nightly exposure alert leaves the schedule, and it was that check's only detector — which is worth remembering as a shape rather than this table's fate: a write-path rule with a report as its only guard, and a report that stops running, is a rule that quietly stops existing. org_unit, unit_participation, unit_ancestors() and unit_descendants() are gone with the hierarchy, and so is every rule about paths, depths, nesting, and reparenting (the whole of data-model/02-organization.md now describes groups).

    One lesson outlives its subject, and it is kept here because it is the corpus's best argument for reviewing prose rather than code. Until D-072 deleted the SQL, the unit-grant tier carried a second branch that looked like it consulted exposures. It did not: it pinned the grant to the unit being checked, which the branch above had just tested, so it could never succeed where the first branch failed. It was dead code, and it had survived every review because it read correctly. Nothing here changes what the system decided — a dead branch decides nothing — but the reason the prose said two routes when one existed, and the fact that removing the SQL is what surfaced it, is the reason this document states one route for everything it describes and says why a tempting second one is absent. See D-078, whose rule — an exposure extends an assignment, never a grant — now has no subject: nothing extends a holder row at all, because a holder row has no scope to extend.

    6. access_grant — the per-item layer¶

    This is the "per-item permission" table. You specified it means a capability on that item: "person X may act on item Y". After round 10 it is also the unit-level table, because the unit is now one of the scope types rather than a different kind of thing, and there is no other grant table to hold the difference. The heading is deliberately unchanged — it names only half of what the table is now, and it is kept because pages across the corpus and in the decision log link to this anchor.

    One grant is: a grantee, a scope, one permission code, an effect, and a period (D-099).

    • Exactly one of person_id and audience_id is set. A grant to neither is meaningless; a grant to both would be ambiguous — any person in the audience, or only this one?

    • scope_id is absent exactly at unit scope — and that is the whole of what the rule says. It constrains the absent case alone; grant_scope_type.item_table is what states which table an id belongs to.
    • effect is allow or deny. There is no third value and no ordering between them: deny is absolute and is checked first, which is D-032 entire surviving content.

Integrity check 11 (§11) reports an open-ended one.

    Inheritance, stated once¶

    A grant at the unit reaches every item of every type. A grant on one item reaches that item and nothing else. That is the whole of it (D-099), and it follows from two deletions rather than from a new rule: after D-097 there is no intermediate scope left between the unit and an item, and after D-102 there is no ownership relation to test.

    This is D-006 with the unit tier left standing — per-item permissions are capabilities on the item — and it deletes can_event() outright, along with the unit-authority rule that nearly every event permission in this corpus was reached through. What that rule was doing, so that its absence is noted rather than assumed: it asked whether the actor had authority over the event's owning unit before honouring an event grant. There is no owning unit, so there is nothing to ask, and the question "who is connected to this event?" is answered by the operands of §8's language instead — ORGANIZERS, PLACED, CONFIRMED — each of which is a set the item itself defines.

    A unit grant of system.admin therefore reaches everywhere on its own. The old resolver needed a special clause for that ("system.admin qualifies whatever the checked scope is"); it existed because organization scope and unit scope were different tiers. With one unit scope and plain inheritance, the clause has nothing to fix.

    The uniqueness rule has to be careful with audiences¶

    The obvious index — one live grant per (grantee, scope) — never worked, because a person can legitimately hold a personal grant and be covered by an audience grant on the same item, and those are different rows with different lifetimes. Round 10 adds a second reason it cannot work: one code per row, so one person legitimately holds a dozen live allows on one item. A broad office is a couple of dozen rows (D-099), each a different code, and a rule that permitted one live grant per grantee and item would refuse the second of them.

    Rules

    • At most one live allow per (grantee, scope, permission) — counted over the rows that name a person and are open, unrevoked, and effect = allow.
    • At most one live allow per (audience, scope, permission), counted the same way over the rows that name an audience.
    • A person and an audience are different grantees, so the same code on the same item may exist once for the person and once for a set they are in. It grants nothing extra and it is not an error; it is two people's decisions about the same capability, which is what the grantee pair has always meant.

    Indexes

    • person, audience and scope lookups, each restricted to open rows;
    • a deny-only index over open rows with effect = deny, keyed by grantee, scope and permission — the deny lookup runs on every check, at every scope, and it is the first thing the resolver does.

    Denies are excluded from both unique indexes, so a deny may coexist with an allow on the same grantee, scope and code — which is exactly what makes an explicit deny meaningful. A personal deny beats an audience allow, because the deny check runs first and is scope-independent (§10).

    effect, and why a - in an expression is not a deny¶

    Two mechanisms that both subtract, and are not interchangeable (D-099):

    effect = deny - in an audience expression
    Narrows Every grant that applies to the person, including ones written elsewhere, later, or by somebody else One grant's reach
    Says "she must not have this, whatever else she is given" "this grant is not for the people in that set"
    Beatable by Nothing. Checked first, absolute, scope-independent Yes — any other grant that reaches her
    Lives on A grant row An operand of the grant's audience (§8)

    The first cannot be expressed by the second, which is why denies stay — that is D-013's whole reason for existing. And denies are legal at the unit as well as on an item, since "exclude her from what the unit grants everyone" is exactly the case an expression cannot serve: subtracting her from one grant leaves every other grant aimed at the same set intact.

    A deny is a real row and can therefore be wrong in a way an allow cannot be. A live deny whose grantee, scope and code match no live allow is dead weight, and a deny that denies nothing is how a stale restriction survives its reason — integrity check 5 (§11) reports the shape, as D-099 requires.

    Live-grant uniqueness excludes denies — deliberately¶

    A deny must be able to coexist with an allow on the same scope, because that is the only way to express "this person has access to the unit's events, except this one". So the rule that keeps one live grant per grantee, item and code — carried here as uq_access_grant_live — covers allows only; a deny row is outside it by design, and the resolver checks denies first.

    This is the rule's shape, not an implementation detail of an index (D-072), and the distinction is load-bearing here rather than pedantic: "unique among live allowing grants" is a conditional rule, so the migration must emit a partial unique index whose predicate excludes deny. A plain unique constraint over the same columns is a different and wrong rule — it would refuse the deny beside the allow, which is precisely the pair the domain requires. See open question F-10, shape 2.

    The condition also excludes revoked_at, which is easy to write wrongly in the other direction. A revoked grant must not occupy the unique slot — that is what lets a grant be revoked and re-made rather than edited. So the predicate is "open and unrevoked and allow", three tests, and a partial index that gets one of them wrong is invisible until the day someone cannot save a grant they should be able to.

    What is refused when a grant is saved¶

    Five checks, one transaction, each with the layer named — because none of them is a database rule, and D-081 forbids writing them as if one were:

    1. The code may be granted at this scope. permission.applies_to permits the scope shape (§2). Refused with the reason in the message, per D-099's example: a grant of event.create on one event is refused because an event cannot be created twice.
    2. The code is assignable. is_assignable is true (§2's deprecated-code case).
    3. Every bound operand in the grant's audience may bind here. An operand whose selector_type.binds_to_item is true is legal only on an item-scope grant, and only where selector_type.argument_type matches that grant's item type. PLACED on a person_group grant is refused because that item is not an event; ORGANIZERS on a unit grant is refused because at the unit there is nothing to bind it to, with the operand named in the message (D-102).
    4. The audience's expression is well-formed and acyclic (§8) — and "acyclic" is measured over the expanded tree, so this check follows the AUDIENCE leaves into the audiences this one names (D-103). Saving a grant is not where a cycle is created, but it is where a person is standing to be told about one, and a grant must never be saved pointing at an expression that cannot be evaluated. There is no longer a depth limit to check here: the height bound was deleted along with its parameter (D-105), so this check carries no size rule at all and the cycle walk is the only structural refusal left in it.
    5. Every AUDIENCE leaf names an audience that exists and that has a code (D-103). Both halves read a second table, so neither is a constraint on this row: the dangling case is the orphan check of §11 item 2, and the null-code case is that item's neighbour in item 12. A leaf pointing at an unnamed audience is refused rather than resolved-at-read, because resolving it later would make a grant's reach depend on whether somebody had typed a code into a different screen.

    Why the save path and not the check path, said once for all five. A grant that cannot mean anything is a data-entry mistake, and a data-entry mistake is refused where the person making it is standing. The resolver's job in the same situation is the opposite and is already fixed: an operand that does not resolve is the empty set, so anything that slips past these checks denies rather than widens (§8, Evaluation) — except through -, where an empty operand widens, which is why these are refusals and not warnings. Checks 3 and 4 (§11) report what got through, and a row there means the write path was skipped — there is no constraint to have disabled.

    A sixth refusal lives on the other screen. Ending or deleting an audience is refused while a live grant, or a live audience reachable from one, names it (§8, Rules). It is listed here only so that a reader of this section does not conclude the grant save is the whole write path.

    No foreign key on scope_id — the accepted trade-off¶

    scope_id has no FK because it is polymorphic. This is a deliberate choice, and D-101 extended it to audience_operand.selector_arg_id, which is polymorphic for the same reason and takes the same four mitigations:

    Polymorphic scope_id grantable_item supertable
    Adding a grantable type One row in grant_scope_type New table + FK from supertable + migration
    Referential integrity None — can point at a deleted item Full
    Query cost Direct Requires a join
    Risk Orphaned grants after an item is deleted Migration churn on every new feature

    Polymorphism is what makes a new grantable thing — an event, a group, an office, a document that does not exist yet — a configuration row rather than a schema change, which matters a great deal given how much of this system is not yet specified.

    The mitigations are mandatory, not optional:

    1. grant_scope_type validates the scope_type value (FK above).
    2. Deleting an item must close its grants — an application obligation, and tested.
    3. A nightly orphan check per scope type, and the same scan over selector_arg_id (§11, check 2).
    4. scope_id is BIGINT and ids are never reused.

    To overrule this, add a grantable_item(id, item_type) supertable and give every grantable entity an FK to it. Tracked in ../open-questions.md — it is a real fork and worth a deliberate decision.

    Why valid_to is discouraged here too¶

    An item grant is typically for one event, so an open-ended item grant is nearly always a mistake. Application validation should require valid_to for item-scoped grants, with access_grant.grant_reason explaining any exception — and the sentence has to stay an application rule, because D-071's posture applies here too: nothing in the schema forces a grant to end, and the report of long-lived open grants is the control. What changed in round 10 is that the nudge lost its machinery. The prefilled term (role.max_term_months) is deleted, and the membership gate that used to make a forgotten row harmless is deleted; a forgotten grant is now simply live.

    7. permission_level — deleted by D-099¶

    This section, and the table it described, are deleted (D-099). The number is kept so §8 onward keep their anchors.

    What the table was, and the one job it actually did. permission_level mapped (permission, scope type) → the level a grant had to reach for that permission at that scope kind. It existed so that one access_grant.level could serve many codes without a hardcoded mapping in application code, and its virtue was reviewability: without it, every permission check would need to know its own level — a mapping in code, invisible to the organization, and impossible to review. "What does manage on an event actually let someone do?" was a query, and now there is no such question to ask, because there is no such word.

    Its two jobs moved to two columns on permission itself, which is D-099's exact accounting:

    The table's job Now
    Which scope kinds this permission means anything at permission.applies_to (§2), checked on save
    How much of this permission a given grant carries Nothing — a grant is one permission. Either the row exists or it does not
    (and the comparison the level made possible) permission.right_kind, used by exactly one channel: guardianship (§10)

    What is genuinely lost, said once. The ladder was a bundle with a name, and a bundle is something you can ask a question about: contribute on this event, for this person, meant a computable set of codes and the audit trail could say "she held contribute". Now the answer to "what did she have in 2024?" is the set of grant rows that were live then, which is more precise and less quotable. A broad office is a couple of dozen rows, and that is the price of the question "what exactly does this person have?" — D-099 paid it deliberately, and D-068 had already refused the same idea once when it deleted <resource>.admin and said roles are the bundle. Round 9 was half the thought: roles and audiences are the bundle, and nothing else is.

    Levels can come back as names for sets of codes with no comparison defined — which is what a role already is, so no new mechanism would be needed to approximate one. A real ordering coming back means re-importing this table, the rank rule (D-012), and locality's tie-break (D-032's deleted half), all of which were deleted with their only consumers. That is the two-way door, and it is narrow on the ordering side and wide on the naming side.

    8. audience — grants that target a set of people¶

    A grant can name a person or an audience (D-042). An audience is now an expression over named sets, stored as a tree (D-101): MEMBERS * ROLE(treasurers) - GROUP(schuzka2026-1) is one grant's grantee, and the three-arm shape it replaces — a rule arm, a manual arm, an inclusion arm, unioned — is deleted. D-091's outcome survives the reversal: composing "the kitchen crew, plus whoever leads this pack" in one grant is what round 9 bought and round 10 keeps, and the intersection and difference round 9 could not express at all can now be written.

    Fields (audience)

    Field Kind Notes
    id integer identity Surrogate key.

(1) Since D-101: a stable handle for the seeded rows (everyone, all_members) and for reports — an audience is aimed at by a grant through access_grant.audience_id, a reference, and that has not changed. (2) New, D-103: the only thing another audience can name. AUDIENCE(<code>) resolves through this column, so naming an audience is what makes it composable, and an unnamed audience is simply not one. Naming stays opt-in — there is no reason to force a handle onto a grant's private addressee list — and the leaf may not point at a null code, which is a check reading another table and therefore not a constraint (§11, check 12). code now joins the shared namespace of roles, groups and reserved words; name does not, and the two are different columns serving different questions.
    name text, required What a human calls it in the editor.

    Rules (audience)

    • The expression is the audience. An audience with no root operand row is an empty set, and a freshly created one is exactly that — not a misconfiguration, and how an expression gets built one node at a time. What is not legal is a grant aimed at one being reported as granting something, which is the editor's job (§The cost of composition) and integrity check 7 (§11).
    • An audience is aimed at by reference and named by code, and those are different relations. A grant points at audience.id; another audience points at audience.code. What changed in this round is only the second half — D-101's sentence, "an audience is referred to, never named, and that is why the name collision problem does not reach this table", was true of a grammar whose only names were GROUP() and ROLE(), and D-103 retires it. The name collision problem now does reach this table, and §Names are one namespace is where the rule lives.
    • Bare names do not resolve to audiences. MEMBERS * COUNSELORS names a role or a group, exactly as it did before, and the audience arm has to be written AUDIENCE(veterans) (D-103). This is a backward-compatibility rule and it is worth more than the four characters it costs: letting a bare name reach a third table would silently change what stored expressions mean, which is the one change this round's own canonical-rendering rule exists to prevent.
    • code is unique among the rows that have one, and joins the shared namespace (§Names are one namespace).
    • An audience may not be ended or deleted while something live names it — a live grant aimed at it, or a live audience reachable from a live grant through any number of AUDIENCE leaves (D-103). Refused at save, and the reverse lookup is one indexed query over audience_operand rows whose selector_kind is AUDIENCE, so the machinery the reference needs is the machinery the protection needs. Without this, "ended" is a way to empty an expression from a screen nobody associates with it — and under - an empty operand makes the answer wider, not narrower. No foreign key does this job: the reference is a code inside a polymorphic column, not a foreign key (§The expression tree).
    • Naming an audience requires the right to read it — audience.view over it, in the catalog's vocabulary — and grants nothing to the people it contains (../appendix/permission-catalog.md). Fail closed, and see what you name is already D-091's requirement. AUDIENCE(veterans) in a grant someone else saves is how one person's composition decision becomes another's authority, and the read permission is the only place that asymmetry is visible to the writer.
    • The row carries no size rule. There is no depth limit, no width limit and no node budget on an audience: D-105 deleted the bound, and nothing replaces it on this row — the tree is stored exactly as the writer wrote it. What a large expression costs is a report's job (§The cost of composition), and a report with nothing behind it is a convention and not a guard (D-081).
    • Where both ends of the period are set, valid_to is later than valid_from.

    Deleted from this table: resolver_key, scope_parameter, include_depth_limit and selector_depth_limit. The first two are absorbed into the selector_type registry (§The selector registry) — which operands exist, whether they take an argument and what type it is was three hard-coded lists (the enum written out in prose, the reserved-word list, and the "meaningless without a resolver_key" rule) and is now one seeded table. audience_include, the inclusion arm, is deleted outright (§What each operand costs). selector_depth_limit is the newest deletion and it has no successor at all: it bounded the height of the tree over the expanded expression, and D-105 removed both the number and the check that read it, so the column's own justification — D-048's rule that a number somebody may want to change belongs in a configuration column (D-048) — outlived the parameter it was written to place. There is no bound left to place anywhere. ~~kind, required, builtin exactly when resolver_key was set~~ — deleted by D-091 one round earlier, and the deletion is kept visible because three rounds of prose rested on it and its successor (resolver_key) is now gone too: an audience has no kind and no rule key, only an expression.

    Author in sets. Evaluate per person.¶

    This is the split that makes the notation question smaller than it looks. p ∈ (A ∩ B) ∪ C is by definition (p ∈ A ∧ p ∈ B) ∨ p ∈ C, so the two notations considered in D-101 are the same function read from two ends. The choice decides what gets stored and what a writer is allowed to say, not what the machine computes:

    • Sets are the source language, and they are the closed one. - is complement against EVERYONE and * distributes over +, so the set form is no weaker than a boolean one — it buys zero expressive power and everything in the way of safety. D-060 refused a rule engine, and a per-person boolean language is the door to one. That half of the argument survived round 12 untouched: +, - and * are the only operators the language has, and it has exactly one spelling of each. What did not survive is the second half of the same sentence, the test it offered for what an operand may be: ~~an operand must be a named, materializable set of people. An attribute is not a set of people. Guardianship is (GUARDIANS(x) is a set), age is not, and the line falls exactly where D-060 already drew it.~~ That test is wrong, and D-107 deleted it rather than repairing it.     Grammar, precedence, and the canonical rendering¶

    expr    := term ( '+' term )*
term    := operand ( ( '*' | '-' ) operand )*
operand := EVERYONE | MEMBERS
         | OLDER '(' n ')' | YOUNGER '(' n ')'
         | GUARDIANS [ '(' person ')' ]
         | ATTENDED | PLACED | CONFIRMED | ON_WAITLIST | ORGANIZERS
         | ROLE '(' name ')' | GROUP '(' name ')'
         | PERSON '(' person ')'
         | AUDIENCE '(' code ')'
         | '(' expr ')'

  OLDER(n) and YOUNGER(n) read person.birth_date AS OF THE DATE THE CHECK IS MADE,
  so `MEMBERS * OLDER(18) - YOUNGER(6)` reaches different people on different
  dates with no write, and can(..., on date) for a past date answers for that
  past date. Their argument is an INTEGER — the first operand argument in the
  registry that is not an entity reference (§*The selector registry*). There is
  no comparison operator anywhere in this grammar: no '>=', no '<', no bare AGE
  -- which is where D-060's refusal of a rule engine now lives.  See the prose
  under this block, and D-107.

  The operators did not change this round and there are no new ones: '+', '-'
  and '*' are the whole set, each with one spelling. What grew is the operand
  list, and nothing else — OLDER and YOUNGER are ordinary named operands,
  composed by the same three operators as every other row.

  Each of ATTENDED, PLACED, CONFIRMED, ON_WAITLIST and ORGANIZERS appears either
  bare — bound to the item the grant sits on — or written '(' event ')'.  A bare
  bound operand on a unit-scope grant is refused (§6, *What is refused when a
  grant is saved*).

  PERSON takes a person and names a set of one.  A hand-written set of people is
  therefore a sum, not a leaf: `PERSON(a) + PERSON(b)`
  ([D-105](../decisions/decision-log.md#d-105--there-are-no-lists-a-hand-written-set-is-an-expression-like-any-other)).
  AUDIENCE names another saved audience by its
  `code`; an audience with no code cannot be named, and a bare name never
  resolves to one ([D-103](../decisions/decision-log.md#d-103--an-audience-may-name-another-audience)).
    • + union, * intersection, - difference — three operators, and round 12 added none of its own. What it added was two operands the same three operators compose: MEMBERS * OLDER(18) - YOUNGER(6) is an ordinary term (D-107).
    • There is no comparison operator in the language. No >=, no <, no bare AGE: an operand is a registry row and AGE >= 15 has no row to be, which is the whole of D-060's protection now (§Author in sets, and D-107).
    • * and - bind tighter than +; * and - bind equally, left to right; brackets override.
    • The canonical rendering prints every bracket, always. The rendering is derived from the rows and is what every screen shows; if it is also kept as a column for search, it is a copy and the rows win (see §The expression tree).

    Printing every bracket is not a courtesy, and the example is the proof. Under this precedence, the unbracketed MEMBERS - GROUP(schuzka2026-1) * COUNSELORS means "everyone except the counselors in that group", which is not what its author meant — they meant "all counselors not in that group", whose stored form is (MEMBERS - GROUP(schuzka2026-1)) * COUNSELORS. The editor rewrites what is typed into canonical form before saving, so no expression in this system is ever read bare. The consequence is a nice property worth having: once everything is stored fully bracketed, changing what binds tighter later changes nothing already stored, which is why the canonical rendering is a requirement and precedence is the closed half of D-101's two-way door.

    The rule is unchanged by D-105, and its cost is now visible: a hand-typed sum of twenty people is a left-to-right chain, so it prints as nineteen nested pairs of brackets. That is what "storage and display both say what was written" buys, and it was asked for. If it turns out unreadable, the answer is that a set of people big enough to be unreadable is a person_group — not a flattening rule invented in advance.

    The selector registry¶

    selector_type is a seeded table, not an enum (D-101): the language becomes data. The parser reads its reserved words from it, the editor builds its dropdown from it, and the write path checks arity against it. Three hard-coded lists became one seeded registry in the shape grant_scope_type already has.

    Rules (selector_type)

    • Rows are platform-seeded. Adding an operand is a decision entry plus a seed row, never a row somebody types into a screen: each operand is a branch of the evaluator and a fact about stored data, and an editor that could invent one would produce an expression nothing evaluates — which then resolves to the empty set and denies quietly forever (§Evaluation).
    • The registry is organization-read-only, and saying that out loud is what makes "a closed language" true of a table rather than merely asserted. Every row ships with the platform, every row is is_system, no screen edits this table, and no organization adds to it (D-103). The language becomes data means the parser and the editor read one place instead of three; it has never meant the language is extensible at runtime. A code in this table with no shipped resolver arm is a deployment bug, and it surfaces as an empty set — an operand that reaches nobody, an allow that allows nobody, and a deny that on the - side denies everybody. That is the whole hazard of calling a closed set "data", and it is why the seeding of this table is release engineering rather than configuration. There is no third kind of row — the taxonomy is shipped and seeded, and "seeded, marked system, but not implemented" is not a category this model allows, however useful it would be as a roadmap marker. GRANTS was the candidate for that third kind, and it is refused on type grounds one clause below rather than admitted with a status flag that would put a broken operand inside the language and an empty set inside every grant that named it.
    • Every code is a reserved name in the shared role / group / audience namespace (§Names are one namespace).
    • argument_type is absent exactly when takes_argument is false, and names a real table exactly when it is true — one exception, new in D-107: the scalar domain integer, which OLDER and YOUNGER take and which names no table because it names no row. Amend the check rather than admire the rule: anything reading this column now has to know the scalar case exists, and has to know what the scalar is not — it is not a reference. That is why §11's assertion that exactly two polymorphic keys in this design go without a foreign key (access_grant.scope_id, audience_operand.selector_arg_id) is still true at fourteen rows, and true for the right reason rather than by luck: an 18 sitting in selector_arg_id points at nothing, so there is nothing for a merge to repoint, nothing for a cascade to find, and nothing for the orphan scan to walk. The check counts references, and 18 is not one (§A hand-named person is now a keyless reference, where the argument kinds are enumerated).
    • The four attendance tiers name columns of event_registration and nothing else. That is the requirement that keeps the writer's choice honest — picking between tiers only means something if the choices are the stored facts.

    Three operands are not in this table, and each absence is load-bearing — but as of this round two of them are decisions rather than type errors. Round 12 deleted the kind of argument those two were refused on. While the corpus claimed an attribute was not a set of people, a predicate over a column could be excluded by what it is; OLDER is a row, so that class of argument is gone and nothing may be excluded that way again. What remains are arguments about consequences, which are less tidy and are the real reasons they were always refused (D-107). Both answers are unchanged; only the grounds moved.

    • PRESENT(<event>) over was_present is refused, and this paragraph is the whole reason. The reason is the failure direction: was_present is NULL until somebody takes a register, so an operand built on it would revoke a child's access to their own camp photos because a volunteer forgot to tick a box. That column is a safeguarding signal, three-valued on purpose (D-067), and keeping it out of the selector language is what keeps it meaning "a person recorded this" rather than "this is who may see things". Say this wherever presence tempts a reader, and this is one of the two places that reader will look. What this round withdrew is one clause of the older version of this refusal, not the refusal: PRESENT(event) was previously held to be unspeakable here, and it is not — it is a predicate over a column, which is now an ordinary kind of operand. It is refused because of what it would do on the - side of an expression when a volunteer forgets a tick box, which is an argument about consequences and survives D-107 untouched.
    • GRANTS is refused, and its refusal is a different kind from the two above. It was wanted so that an expression could name "the people reached by these grants" — a bulk-editing affordance dressed as an operand. An operand must resolve to a set of people, and a set of grants is not one, which is not a judgement about usefulness but the closure property of §Author in sets doing the job it exists for: the moment an expression can name the grant table, MEMBERS - GRANTS(...) is expressible, and a language in which a grant can subtract a grant is a language in which the answer to "who may do this?" requires evaluating the whole expression tree of every other grant to answer one check. The bulk edit it was asked for is real and is an editor affordance — select the grants, change their grantees, in one screen, which changes rows and leaves every stored expression meaning what it meant. Refusing it here is what keeps that screen a UI feature instead of a resolver feature.
    • WHITELISTED is not seeded, and the reason is that there is nothing to evaluate. The nearest facts are source = staff (an organizer entered them) and promotion off the waitlist. Either it means one of those or it is a new column on event_registration, which is an events decision and not a selector decision, so it stays an open question rather than being implied by a word in a grammar. An operand that reads no state is not a vocabulary item but a syntax error wearing one's clothes, and this refusal is untouched by round 12 — what moved is the grounds it was previously given. F-20 is now a question about whether the feature is wanted, and no longer about whether the language could hold the word: since OLDER arrived, a predicate over a column is an ordinary operand, and WHITELISTED is out on its merits rather than on its type (D-107). PERSON(x) is in the registry, and this page is the reason anyone noticed it was missing. Two decision entries write it — D-100 has the waived-for-one-person case as MEMBERS * ROLE(x) + PERSON(y) and D-103 had the kitchen crew as (GROUP(kitchen) + LIST) - PERSON(a) - PERSON(b) — and the seeded table this page documents did not carry it, which this page read as "the language has no such operand" and the modules read as a licence to rewrite both examples as LIST. That reading was wrong, and the wrongness was a proof of less than it seemed. D-103's example used LIST and PERSON() in the same expression, so the two were being distinguished on purpose, and no substitution could work because LIST was then one set: + LIST added Honza and - LIST would have taken him back away again, so the two who left had to be subtracted through something that named only them. PERSON is that operand, argument_type = person, and the row now sits above. What the example proved then was that a set of several and a set of one are different kinds of thing; D-105 deleted one of the two it was telling apart, so the same expression, written the way it is written now, proves the opposite claim — that a hand-written sum of PERSON() leaves does the work of both. That is the interesting part, and it is not smoothed over here: the distinction this paragraph drew was real, and it was not worth a table. It is also why the deletion was a deletion rather than a redesign — an argument composes freely, which is the only property LIST lacked — so (GROUP(kitchen) + PERSON(honza)) - PERSON(a) - PERSON(b) parses today, unchanged in meaning.

    The other half of what this page objected to was right, and is worth keeping as the reason the row took a whole round to appear: putting a person's id into the language means an operand is no longer only the name of a set somebody else owns — PERSON(a) is a set nobody maintains, created by being typed. That is tolerable for exactly one operand, at the size of one person, in the one place where the alternative (a second grant aimed at that person, which stays legal and is arguably clearer for a permanent exception) leaves the exception invisible next to the rule it exempts. What was not tolerable is a control that does not parse. D-105 pushed this further than the paragraph expected: with the table gone, every hand-named person in the system is now one of these leaves, and a grant aimed at one person is no longer a curiosity but the ordinary expression of a temporary exception. The cost of that is not the typing, it is that a typed person is a selector_arg_id with no foreign key behind it — see §A hand-named person is now a keyless reference, which is where the requirement D-105 newly makes load-bearing lives.

    What LIST is, said plainly — withdrawn by D-105¶

    This section is withdrawn. LIST is not an operand and audience_member is not a table, so the question this section spent its length on — how does a grant carry its own hand-written exceptions? — now has the answer every other set in this language gives: write them in the expression, as PERSON(a) + PERSON(b) (D-105). The heading is kept because a decision entry links here and because most of what this section argued is not replaced by anything: it is the reasoning that decides where a hand-written set must not be put, and that reasoning outlived the operand it was written to justify.

    What was withdrawn, and the embarrassment the decision records itself. This page argued at length that LIST and PERSON were two operands and not one wearing two names. The argument was correct about the semantics and wrong about the conclusion, and the difference between those two turned out to be a table nobody needs. LIST was kept by D-101 on the stated ground that "a hand-written set of three people is not derivable from anything" — and D-101 was then amended, late in the same round, to add PERSON(x): the operand that names exactly one human being. That amendment removed the table's reason and the round did not notice, leaving two ways to write the same set of names. D-103's kitchen crew is the example the section was built around and it survives with one operand fewer: (GROUP(kitchen) + PERSON(honza)) - PERSON(a) - PERSON(b) — same meaning, one fewer kind of thing.

    What survives, because the hazard it guarded is real and unchanged. Naming three people inside a grant must not mean making them members of a person_group. A group's members are true when no grant points at them (D-098), so editing one to move one grant's reach edits a roster that other grants read — and it invents a group nobody talks about, which is the rule D-098 wrote. This is now the only argument left standing in this section, and D-105 keeps it deliberately: the answer to "plus Honza" is PERSON(honza), and the answer is not a roster edit. The second half survives too — an exception written this way is invisible to every other grant until somebody opens this one, it has no name of its own, and AUDIENCE(x) still resolves to another audience's whole expression and nothing less, so nobody can reach into the middle of it from outside.

    What is given up, written as lost rather than as managed. Per-person dates: audience_member carried valid_from / valid_to with the half-open no-overlap rule, audience_operand carries no period and gains none, so a June-to-August exception is its own grant with its own period — which is what D-099's shape is for, and the cost is exact: where there was one audience and one grant there are now two grants, and a reviewer asking "what does Honza have?" must find both. Per-name provenance: added_by named who put one person on one list, and what remains is created_by on a node, which — because saving an expression rewrites its rows — answers "who last saved this tree" and not "who added Honza". The list's past: "who was on this audience in 2026" is now a recomputation over audit payloads that expire after 24 months (D-088), so the question stops being answerable in 2028 — the same hole D-091 already accepted for nested audiences, one table wider. And two writers at once: adding a name used to be an insert, so two clerks could not collide; it is now an edit of one expression, so they can, and whoever builds the editor owes this an optimistic-concurrency check or a queue, because a silent lost update here quietly removes somebody's access and nothing in the schema will notice.

    The rename question is moot, not answered. This section's last paragraphs called LIST the weak part of the design and registered ADDED as the name to replace it. There is no operand to rename, so §13 deletes the question rather than closing it.

    The expression tree¶

    Each row of audience_operand is one node — the storage D-101 chose over a normalized union of terms, "because a binary tree needs one evaluator of about fifteen lines and keeps the writer's brackets instead of silently rewriting them".

    Fields (audience_operand)

    Field Kind Notes
    id integer identity Surrogate key.

    Rules (audience_operand)

    • Exactly one shape. A node has operator, left_id and right_id, and no selector_kind; a leaf has selector_kind (and selector_arg_id where the registry says it takes one) and no operator and no children. Expressible on the row, so this one stays a real constraint — the only rule in this section a migration can state by itself, which is worth noticing among the ones that cannot.
    • One parent, never a DAG. parent_id lives on the child. A side table letting two expressions share a node is refused: editing the shared node would silently widen every grant using it, which is D-091's inclusion hazard re-imported through the back door. Check 15 (§11) is a CI assertion that no such table exists.
    • Cycles are refused when the row is saved, there are two walks to run, and between them they are now the only structural guard this module has. Both survived D-105 deliberately, and they must not be relaxed alongside the bound they used to travel with: an expansion that never terminates is the failure the deleted height limit was never protecting against anyway, and with the bound gone nothing else stands between a saved expression and an unbounded one.
    • Inside the tree: walk the parent chain from the row upward — refuse a revisit. The tree shape makes a cycle structurally hard to build (a node cannot be its own ancestor if it did not exist when its parent was written) and it is refused rather than trusted for D-091's own reason: a hand-fixed row, a restore from before the guard existed.
    • Across audiences: follow every AUDIENCE leaf in the closure and refuse a cycle through them. This is the walk D-103 makes necessary, and it is the sentence D-101 got wrong, struck here rather than quietly corrected: ~~a tree cannot cycle, so the only acyclicity guard left is the cheap parent-chain one~~ — true of one tree, false of a forest of trees pointing at each other, which is what naming an audience creates. Half a is cheap; half b is the write-time reachability walk D-091 already required, over a graph built at save time from rows that already exist — and it is the walk that also counts the closure whose size the read path pays for, since it is already visiting every audience in it. The height limit that used to be measured on the same expanded tree is gone, and this is the exact spot where it was enforced. Application layer for both walks, and the layer is named because each spans rows rather than columns of one row, which is F-10 shape 3, so no migration can emit either.

    What did not come back is worth as much as what did. audience_include stays deleted: the edges are the AUDIENCE leaves, so there is no edge table to maintain, no transitive walk over rows at read time, and no include_depth_limit column. The machinery D-101 counted as deleted stays deleted; only its confidence was wrong. - The tree is stored exactly as the writer wrote it: one row per node, two operands and one operator, left to right, and no rebalancing (D-105). This is written as a rule because rebalancing was the tempting engineering answer the moment the height bound was removed — a balanced tree is cheaper to walk, and nothing here wants a cheaper walk badly enough to pay for it. A rebalanced tree is a different expression from the one a person wrote, which is the whole reason this storage exists rather than a normalized union of terms (D-101's choice, restated at the top of this section), and a quietly flattened tree is one whose canonical rendering no longer prints what its author saw. - Nothing bounds how big an expression may get, and this page calls that what it is: a convention, not a guard. D-105 deleted the parameter and refused to mint a replacement, so the size of an audience's expansion is now bounded only by acyclicity — a bound on height no longer exists and a bound on breadth was never asserted. What replaces the retired invariant is a standing report of the widest and deepest audiences and their cost per check, together with the operand cache of §10. Nothing refuses a save on size, no CI job fails, and the report stops being a control the moment nobody reads it. That is D-081's case exactly — the rule's name outlives the mechanism and the text must say which of the two happened — and D-105 counts this as the third control of that shape the authorization module now depends on. - Arity and binding are checked against selector_type on save: an argument present exactly when the registry allows one, and a bound (argument-less, binds_to_item) operand legal only on an item-scope grant whose type matches argument_type (§6, What is refused when a grant is saved). - An operand that does not resolve is the empty set — including an AUDIENCE leaf whose target has gone (D-103). A dangling selector_arg_id, a selector kind the evaluator does not implement, a role or group whose row has been deleted — each is the empty set, and an expression that cannot be evaluated reaches nobody (§Evaluation). Fail closed on the allow side, and note that the same rule is fail-open looking, which is why it is stated with its two halves rather than as a slogan: an empty operand reached through - makes the result wider, so EVERYONE - <nothing> denies everybody. The corpus's word for this was "loud", and round 12 retired it (D-106): - was never noisy, it is the only operator whose failed operand can grant, and naming a direction "noise" is how a rule gets read backwards. The sentence to keep is the directional one — a failed operand on the + or * side narrows the set, a failed operand on the - side widens it — because fail closed names a direction and not a safe state, and EVERYONE - PERSON(x) with a dead x is fail-closed and a lockout of everybody in one breath. D-103 kept the rule because a silent widening is discovered in an audit two years late; what round 12 added is a page, not a rule. An audience carrying a leaf that does not resolve is listed for its owner, in a second column of the report F-21 already asks for. No save is refused because a leaf is broken, and no expression is pinned to the set it resolved to when it was saved — pinning would make the stored expression a ceiling on truth, and a grant would freeze at the shape it had on the day somebody typed it. The report is a query with an owner: D-081's convention, not a guard, written as though it were a guard by the round that offered it. And the line D-106 draws is narrower than it looks: an operand that resolves fine and simply does not contain the person is not on that list. A person with no birth date is not a failed OLDER(18) — the operand resolves to the people who are that age on the checked date, and the person is not among them, exactly as a person with no membership row is not a failed MEMBERS. A missing attribute on the person being asked about is an ordinary answer, not a resolution failure, and a report that conflated the two would page about every incomplete import in the country. The protection above (an audience named by a live grant cannot be ended or deleted) is what keeps the wide case from arriving by ordinary administration. - The AUDIENCE leaves are the reference edges, and there is no edge table (D-103). Every question about nesting — does this cycle, how deep does this get, which grants name this audience, may this audience be deleted — is answered by selecting rows from this one table where selector_kind = 'AUDIENCE', which is why the round could delete audience_include and add nesting in the same breath. The two-directional index that makes that cheap is (selector_kind, selector_arg_id), and it is the second reason the reference is stored as a row rather than parsed out of a rendering: an edge inside a string answers none of those questions without a table scan. - A node belongs to exactly one tree, and to exactly one audience. Every row's audience_id equals its parent's; there is exactly one row with no parent; no row is its own descendant. Check 10 (§11) counts these. Nesting does not soften this: AUDIENCE(x) points a whole tree at another whole tree. It is not a node shared between two expressions, and the rule above about one parent and no sharing table is exactly what keeps AUDIENCE() from being audience_include again by another name. - The whole tree is written together. An expression is one act of authorship, so inserting a grant's audience inserts its nodes in one transaction, and deleting an audience deletes them. A node whose parent arrives in a later transaction is an orphan, and an orphan is check 10, not a transient state to be tolerated.

    Why a tree and not a string, and why not a normal form. A string would have to be re-parsed on every check and would make the arity and depth rules text-processing rather than row-writing. A union of terms (the normalization D-101's author offered and D-101 declined) needs the normalizer — distribute, dedupe, sort, survive blow-up — and would rewrite the writer's brackets behind their back. The tree needs one fifteen-line evaluator, keeps the brackets, and makes the depth limit the only complexity bound, since (A + B) * C costs nothing.

    What that costs, once. Two expressions with the same meaning are two different row sets, so "you already granted exactly this set on that row" no longer falls out of storage. The route back is a normalized fingerprint computed on save as a derived value used only for a warning — and if the expansion overflows, skip the warning rather than refuse the write: normalization informing a human, not gating one. Registered in §13 rather than promised, and the same reasoning is why "this selector reaches nobody" belongs to the editor preview that has to exist anyway (§The cost of composition) rather than to a syntactic check the tree cannot perform — a tree catches X - X on one node and nothing else.

    audience_member, re-roled as the LIST leaf — deleted by D-105¶

    This section, and the table it described, are deleted (D-105). D-101 had kept it with the words "it stays", and this section's whole job in the last round was to defend that keeping; a hand-written set of people is now written in the expression, as PERSON(a) + PERSON(b), in the same place every other set in this language is written. The heading is kept so that the pages linking here land on the deletion rather than on nothing.

    What the table was, and what died with it. audience_member held persons rather than rules: one row per person per audience, with valid_from / valid_to on a half-open period and a no-overlap rule counted over all rows, plus added_by naming who put that one person there. Its rules leave with it — the no-overlap rule, the refusal to add a person to a seeded is_system audience (adding somebody to everyone was a category error, and the refusal lived in the write path with the editor as its only detector), the editor affordance that rendered the operand as LIST (this audience), and one entry in the cache-invalidation trigger list of §10. The is_system refusal is a rule whose mechanism is gone and whose name should not survive it: there is no row left to refuse, so unlike the reports above this one retires completely rather than becoming a report.

    What survives, and it is the reason this deletion is not a reversal of D-098. The refusal to solve a grant's exception by editing a group roster stands untouched, because the hazard is real and unchanged: making three people members of GROUP(kitchen) in order to reach one grant edits a roster that other grants read, and it creates a row in the group browser that is not a group anybody talks about. D-098 is still the rule that says so — a group's members are true when no grant points at them — and PERSON(a) + PERSON(b) is now the answer that respects it. What is genuinely gone from that paragraph is its counterparty: this table was one of the two dated-member tables the design apologised for, the duplication D-080 would flag had two instances to explain, and now it has one. person_group_member is the only dated set of people the system keeps, and "the permission table must not become the system's roster" is no longer a rule with a live counterexample sitting beside it.

    The mitigation that no longer has anything to mitigate. D-091 required that the editor show which operand each person arrived through, and the requirement was interesting only because a person could arrive through a rule or through a list. Both are now the same kind of thing — a leaf in one expression — so the requirement reduces to "show the expression", which the canonical rendering already does. The two-writers hazard this table used to absorb is stated where its absence is felt: §What LIST is, said plainly — withdrawn by D-105.

    Names are one namespace, and reserved words are the other half¶

    Bare COUNSELORS resolves against roles and groups together, since ROLE() and GROUP() no longer have to disambiguate — which makes the name namespace load-bearing in a way it was not when a name was only a label (D-101). Five rules, and the layer each one lives in is the point:

    1. Names are unique across role, person_group and audience.code (D-103 adds the third table; audience.name is not in the namespace, only its code, because code is what resolves and name is what a screen displays). No single constraint spans three tables, so the write path carries it and integrity check 12 (§11) asserts the write path exists — D-081 wording, because a rule with no database backstop is a convention until a check says otherwise.
    2. Case, whitespace and separators are folded on write, so Schuzka 2026-1, schuzka_2026_1 and SCHUZKA2026-1 are one name and not three sets that look different in an editor.
    3. Every selector_type.code is reserved. A group called MEMBERS is refused at the save rather than silently rewriting every expression in the system.
    4. Bare names still resolve to exactly two kinds: a role or a group. An audience is reachable only through its own keyword, AUDIENCE(veterans) — naming the namespace a third way without widening bare resolution, which is what keeps stored expressions meaning what they meant when they were written (D-103; the audience rules at the head of this section).
    5. What is stored is always kind + id, never a name — so a rename never rewrites a grant. A role renamed still carries everything aimed at it, which is the property the old code-keyed designs kept advertising and never quite had. AUDIENCE obeys this like every other leaf: the resolution happens once, at save, into selector_arg_id, and renaming the audience afterwards rewrites nothing. Read the rule for exactly what it says: a rename rewrites nothing, because a rename leaves the row and its id where they were. A person merge is not a rename — it retires an id — and every hand-named person in the system is now a leaf holding one, which is the subject of the next section rather than an exception hiding in this one.

    What is given up: a name is now a security-relevant string. Two organizations that both call something leaders and one of them a group and the other a role get one namespace deciding which leaders a grant meant, and the answer is that they cannot, because the write path refused the second one at the save. Round 10 widened what that sentence covers: an audience code colliding with an office code was a cosmetic problem while nothing could name an audience, and is a silent grant once something can.

    A hand-named person is now a keyless reference¶

    This is the thing D-105 made load-bearing, and no page in this corpus required it before. PERSON leaves used to be a curiosity: the hand-typed people lived in audience_member, whose person_id was an ordinary foreign key, so its rows moved or vanished correctly on their own and nobody had to think about it. Now every hand-named person in the system is a selector_arg_id, and that column deliberately has no foreign key (D-011's accepted trade-off, extended to this column by D-101 and backstopped by an orphan scan rather than by the database).

    Requirement, and it is new: a person merge, and a redaction, must repoint every audience_operand leaf whose selector_arg_id names the absorbed or erased person, in all five argument kinds that name an entity. The merge reassignment table gains the row, the erasure path gains the scan, and the nightly orphan check (§11, check 2) becomes the backstop rather than the mechanism — a scan that runs the following morning is not a reassignment, and by the time it reports, an expression has already resolved wrong. It belongs to F-10's register for precisely the reason that register exists: the ORM cannot express it, because the column has no key to follow.

    Why this is not housekeeping. A leaf that resolves to nobody is the empty set, which narrows the result on the + and * sides and widens it on the - side — ~~quiet on the allow side and loud on the deny side~~, in the wording D-106 retired — and this module's own recurring worked example is a subtraction: EVERYONE - PERSON(x). Un-repointed, x stops subtracting anybody and the grant denies everybody — the failure the rules two sections back have been pointing at for two rounds, now reachable by an ordinary merge rather than by a hand-edited row. Repointing was not required before, and that was defensible only while the volume of hand-named people sat in a table that had a key to follow.

    Why all five kinds, and not just person. The scan is over one column, and the column does not know what it points at. A merge is the case that made the requirement, but an erased event, a deleted group, a retired office or an ended audience leave the identical orphan behind them, and writing the rule as "PERSON leaves only" would put the hazard straight back where it has been sitting for two rounds. The five kinds a scan walks are person, role, person_group, event and audience. ~~And they are the values selector_type.argument_type takes.~~ That clause was true until round 12 and here is its successor, because argument_type has gained a sixth value that is not a kind of thing a scan could follow: integer, the argument of OLDER and YOUNGER. (D-107). Six values, five of them entity kinds, and the scans walk the five. This is the one place to say why, because it looks like an omission and is not: an 18 in selector_arg_id is not a reference, so there is no row behind it to merge away, none to erase, none for a cascade to find and none for the orphan scan to report. The number this section is about — that exactly two polymorphic columns in this design go without a foreign key — is therefore still two, and still two for the reason it was two before: the count is of keys, and a scalar stored in a key's column is not one. Nobody has to add a person.birth_date branch to a merge script, and anyone who does has misread this paragraph.

    The distinction that used to be load-bearing — built-in versus named, rule xor list xor inclusion — is gone, and what remained worth comparing was whether a set is derived or kept. The right-hand column is now empty, and that is what this round deleted: LIST was the only operand whose members were rows somebody maintained, and with it gone every operand in the registry reads a fact that exists independently of the expression.

    PERSON(x) is not the occupant of that column, and pretending it is would lose the distinction. It is a constant: the answer is written into the leaf itself, so it reads no fact table and maintains no roster. It cannot rot the way a kept set rots — nobody has to remove anybody, because nobody is in it except the one person named. What it can do is dangle, which is a different failure with a sharper edge, and §A hand-named person is now a keyless reference is where that is handled.

    Prefer a derived operand where one exists. A grant to PLACED gets the right answer forever: a new registrant is covered the moment they are placed, and someone who withdraws stops being covered, with no rows to maintain. A grant listing names by hand does not do that, and the old warning against it — the same grant silently rots the moment the roster changes — survives as advice even though the operand it was written about is gone: a sum of PERSON() leaves goes stale exactly as a list did, it just goes stale one leaf at a time. What the two rounds changed is that choosing between derived and named stopped being a choice about the kind of set, so one expression can hold the derived set and the names it cannot reach, in one grant. The other half of the old argument is now stated as a size rule rather than a kind rule: a set of people big enough to be a roster is a person_group, and a set small enough to type is a sum of PERSON() leaves (D-105's judgement, registered where missing operands are registered in ../open-questions.md).

    The inclusion arm is deleted, and the second round had to give half of the answer back. D-091 let a group pull in other groups through audience_include, because a group could otherwise not be composed. Under D-098 a group's membership is its dated rows, and composition moved into the expression: GROUP(kitchen) + ROLE(troop_staff) composes as much as an edge ever did. What went with the edge table was a great deal of machinery — the transitive walk, include_depth_limit, the write-time cycle walk over a graph, and the CI assertion that the walk exists at all — and round 10 was right that a tree needs none of it within one tree.

    Where round 10's arithmetic was wrong, stated plainly because the sentence is still in D-101. It claimed the write-time cycle walk was dead because a tree cannot cycle, and it claimed "add the kitchen crew to everything the troop-staff rule is used for" was answered by naming the composite once and aiming grants at it. Under its own grammar the only names available were GROUP() and ROLE(), and both name a roster, not a rule — so the composite rule had no name to point at, and the "naming" answer could only be carried out by copying rows into a second audience, which is audience_include with the copy step done by hand. D-103 adds the missing arm, and with it the cheap guard stops being the only guard: one write-time reachability walk returns, across audiences rather than over an edge table (§The expression tree). What stays deleted is the table, the column and the read-time walk — the edges are leaves — so the machinery that came back is smaller than the machinery that left, which is the honest version of the claim. AUDIENCE() is also the only arm that names a decision about access rather than people, so it is the only one that can say "the same people that grant may see the fee ledger, plus the accountant".

    AUDIENCE is derived, and it is derived from something nobody maintains, which is its one genuinely new property. Every other derived operand reads a fact table someone is already keeping — a membership, a holder row, a registration. AUDIENCE(x) reads an expression. So the "maintenance: none, it cannot rot" row of the table above is true of it in the sense that there are no rows to maintain, and misleading in the sense that the thing it is derived from is edited by whoever holds audience.manage on it, for their own reasons. That is the hazard of §The cost of composition, and it is why this operand is graded as authority rather than as company.

    Evaluation¶

    An audience grant is evaluated as part of the person's own permissions — the resolver expands it, so a grant to an audience is checked as if the person held it directly. The expansion is a predicate, not a set (§Author in sets).

    A grant applies when one grant row satisfies all of these. These are conditions on a single row, tested together — their order carries no meaning:

    1. The grant names an audience; a personal grant is not this question's business.
    2. Its scope_type is the scope type being checked, and its scope_id matches the scope id being checked, with an absent id counting as equal to an absent checked id.
    3. Its effect is allow — denies belong to the channel that runs first (§10).
    4. It has not been revoked.
    5. It has started as of the checked date and has not ended on that date.
    6. Its permission_id is the checked permission — or is system.admin, the one code that means everything (§2). There is no permission_level row to consult and no rank to compare; the comparison this condition used to perform is the whole reason that table existed and it is gone.
    7. person_in_audience(person, audience, at, item) puts the person in that audience on the checked date, for the item being checked — because a bound operand means different people on different items (D-102).

    person_in_audience() is where the language lives. It takes the item so that bound operands have something to bind to, and it answers one question: is this person in the value of this expression, evaluated as a predicate?

    1. The audience itself must not have ended — no valid_to, or a valid_to after the checked date. An ended audience covers nobody, and this is the one test that gates the whole tree: the expression belongs to the audience that has ended, not to one of its operands.
    2. Evaluate the tree bottom-up, as a predicate. A leaf asks its selector; a node combines its two children with its operator; p ∈ A - B is p ∈ A ∧ ¬(p ∈ B), and so on for + and *. No set of people is ever built — the same fifteen lines answer every operand, and the cost of a check is one indexed read per operand, not one per member. AUDIENCE(x) is the one leaf that recurses (§Three named sets): it hands the same person, at and item to the other audience's tree and returns that answer, predicating all the way down (D-103). Nesting does not turn the evaluator into a set-builder; it turns one fifteen-line function into the same function with one more branch. The recursion is bounded by nothing any more, and the sentence saying it was bounded by the save-time selector_depth_limit is deleted with the limit (D-105). What still holds is the refusal that went with it: the read path gets no depth cap and no cycle check of its own. That refusal used to be a back-up for a guard that existed at save time, and it was argued as "the cap becomes the thing that decides who a grant reaches" — now it is the whole design, so the acyclicity walk at save (§The expression tree) is the only thing standing between a saved expression and one that never finishes evaluating.
    3. A leaf's selector is one read — except AUDIENCE, which is one read per audience in the closure. MEMBERS is the membership predicate at at; ROLE(x) is one open role_holder row for this person and this role at at; GROUP(x) likewise in person_group_member; PERSON(x) is the cheapest leaf in the language — it is the comparison x = p against a value already stored in the row, so a hand-written sum of twenty names is twenty of those and no fact table is consulted; PLACED is this person's event_registration row for the bound event with response = attending and is_waitlisted false; EVERYONE is true. OLDER(n) and YOUNGER(n) are the two cheapest reads in the language after PERSON: one row already in hand — person.birth_date — compared against n at at, no join and no fact table. They are also the only operands whose answer at changes on its own: every other leaf asks whether a row was live at at, and these two ask arithmetic about one, which is why a check for a past date has a right answer here (can(p, view, doc, on 2024-05-01, about q) computes the age that day) and why the set can move with no write to invalidate (D-107). A person whose birth_date is null is not in either set, and that is this rule answering, not failing — item 5 below is about leaves that resolve to nothing, and this is not one of them.
    4. A bound operand binds to the item under check. PLACED with no argument means the event in the check's scope. At the unit there is nothing to bind it to, and such a grant could not have been saved (§6's save-time list) — so an unbound-at-unit shape reaching here is a fault, and it resolves to the empty set rather than to "everyone at the unit", which is the direction a helpful implementation would get wrong.
    5. Failure is denial, in every direction. An operand whose selector_arg_id names nothing, whose selector_kind no evaluator implements, or whose tree cannot be assembled reaches nobody — the case this list used to carry as "whose depth exceeds its bound" left with the bound (D-105), and a leaf whose argument names a person who has been merged away is on this list too, which is why §A hand-named person is now a keyless reference makes repointing a requirement rather than a nicety. Nothing here falls back to "so it probably includes everyone", and nothing here guesses the half of a tree it could resolve: an expression that cannot be evaluated is an empty set, which is the direction this model fails in everywhere else — default deny (§10), deny absolute (channel 0), and an audience whose rule was quietly wrong reaching nobody rather than everybody. This supersedes the old fallback twice over: first, an absent resolver_key used to mean "so it is a stored list"; now an absent selector is a fault, not a hint. Second, the inclusion walk used to be the one place a cycle could make the answer depend on where the walk started, and round 10 said ~~a tree cannot cycle, so there is nothing left to fail on~~. That is true of a tree and false of a forest of audiences pointing at each other, and D-103 struck it. The consequence for the read path is deliberately nothing at all: a cycle through AUDIENCE leaves is refused where it is written, so an evaluator meeting one is meeting data that should not exist, and the rule that covers that is this one — reach nobody, do not recurse forever and do not guess. The reason the write-path check has to be a cross-audience reachability walk and not a parent-chain walk is exactly here (check 15): if the read path is ever the thing that notices a cycle, some grant has already been evaluated wrong.

    Note that ORGANIZERS and ROLE read their tables without themselves being authority grants. Membership in an audience confers nothing on its own — it only decides whether this grant, which someone already created deliberately, reaches this person. An appointed organizer does not become able to do anything by being on the roster; they become able to do exactly what the grant aimed at ORGANIZERS says, on that event. This is D-062 standing untouched, and it is the sentence D-102 leans on: the appointment is now aimed at by grants instead of being special-cased inside the resolver.

    What audiences do not do¶

    • They are not a permission. Being in an audience is not authority; it is membership of a set. All authority still comes from a grant.
    • They do not replace guardianship_permission. GUARDIANS lets an organization open something to a child's guardians; it does not let a guardian edit the child's record. That remains the guardianship channel (§10) and its right_level (D-074).
    • ~~They do not nest.~~ ~~Withdrawn by D-091; the mechanism was removed by D-101.~~ Nesting returns in the same round, through D-103, and the three-stage history is worth keeping because the diagnosis never changed. The sentence read "an audience cannot contain another audience — that is how you get evaluation cycles and answers nobody can predict". Round 9 refused the cycle when the edge was saved; round 10 declared the problem unsayable, because an operand is a leaf naming a set and not a pointer into another expression; and D-103 found that the second round had answered "reuse a rule" with "name the composite", while its own grammar offered no name for a rule — so the arm came back as AUDIENCE(<code>). What the ban was for survives intact, in a different place: cycles are still impossible to store, they are just no longer impossible to write, so the guard is a cross-audience reachability walk on the save path instead of a shape argument (§The expression tree, check 15). What the ban cost is also back, and honestly back: "the kitchen crew plus whoever leads this pack" was the complaint round 10 solved in one grant, and it stays solved — AUDIENCE() does not undo it, it adds the case round 10 could not express, which is reusing a composite rule rather than copying it.
    • Nesting is not sharing. AUDIENCE(x) evaluates the whole of another audience's expression; it does not splice one of its nodes into this tree. Two expressions never share a row, so editing a node inside veterans changes exactly the grants that name veterans and no others — which is the distinction that keeps this from being the DAG this corpus has now refused three times.
    • everyone is genuinely everyone, including people with no account and no membership. It is the right target for a public event page and the wrong one for anything else. And it is reachable by + from inside any expression, which is what the deletion of the inclusion arm changes: nothing stops an expression from unioning the seeded EVERYONE operand, and a writer with audience.manage can widen a grant to the whole world in one edit. That is not a hole in the model — holding EVERYONE directly is already legal and already alarmed (check 8, §11) — it is that a language with union in it makes the same reach available without even pretending to compose it carefully. The mitigation is the editor's expanded set below, not a rule about which operands may appear where.
    • A role cannot be held by a rule. role_holder names one person, and F-12 is closed by D-100, and its answer is no — a derived holder list cannot answer "who was treasurer in 2023", and an office record that cannot answer that is not an office record. The grantee pair F-12 contemplated is no longer even tempting, because grants now aim at roles instead of roles being filled by grants.
    • An expression cannot compute with a person's attributes. ~~An expression cannot say anything about a person's attributes.~~ That bullet stood for rounds and D-107 narrowed it rather than deleting it, because it was standing on the sentence §Author in sets has now withdrawn — that an attribute is not a set of people. OLDER(15) says something about an attribute and it is a row of the registry, so the negative that survives is about terms, not about subject matter: AGE >= 15 is not an operand, is not expressible, and there is still no open question asking for it, because there is no comparison operator for it to use and no way to add a row from a screen. What an author may write is what shipped; that, and not the type of the thing written about, is the closure property §Author in sets protects. Eligibility is capability (D-060) in the sentence that is now true of it: no rule is evaluated outside can(), no eligibility rule table exists, and the system gates nobody on its own — an age rule is a grant somebody wrote, visible in the grant list, revocable like one.

    The cost of composition¶

    A grant's reach stops being visible in the row that grants it. Derived membership has never been stored — D-042 accepted that, and these documents recommended it, because a rule does not rot the way a list does. Rounds 9 and 10 together widened how much of the permission answer is recomputed rather than recorded, and round 10 added a sharper wrinkle.

    • The editor must show the set a grant actually reaches, expanded — the people, not the expression's text — and it is a requirement on the screen rather than a nicety. Composition turns a three-deep expression into a silent widening, and the person saving the grant is looking at one row that mentions no person at all. This is the same posture as the cache question in §10: the answer exists, it is just not stored, so somewhere has to compute it in front of a human — and here the human is the only control there is.
    • And the preview is now per-item, which it structurally cannot be from the grant alone (D-102). PLACED means different people on different events, so "who does this grant reach?" is unanswerable as written when the expression contains a bound operand. The preview is therefore computed for an item and says so on screen — an honest screen names the event whose people it is showing — and the cache key for a bound operand must include the item (§10). This is the one place where D-102's convenience costs a screen an editor's design has to notice, and §13 keeps it open rather than assuming it away.
    • Editing an audience changes grants you did not make, and the screen has to say so before it commits (D-103). Add a person to AUDIENCE(veterans) and every grant naming it now reaches them — including grants their owners wrote for another purpose, and including grants this editor cannot see because they are not theirs. This is not a new failure mode and pretending otherwise would be the actual mistake: GROUP(x) has had the property since D-091, and the round that accepted it there accepted it for the reason that stands — a named set is worth being reusable, and a reusable thing has more than one user. The mitigation is D-091's, extended to the second user: saving an edit to a referenced audience shows which grants name it, and at what depth, before it commits. It is one indexed query over the leaf rows (§The expression tree), and it doubles as the report that partially closes the history gap in §Three named sets. D-103 registers this as an assumption rather than a measured fact — cheap at this organization's size because the edges are indexed rows in the table that already holds every expression, unverified at any size, and a slow save screen is a real risk if the import corpus's row-count estimates turn out wrong. §13 keeps it.
    • "Who could read this in 2023?" now has one fewer answer. The audit trail used to hold the change that made it true, and D-088 empties those payloads after 24 months; the derived membership was never stored at all. After two years the question is answered by re-running expressions against state that has itself moved on, and it is answered with today's confidence. F-7 is the route back — a materialized effective_permission snapshot kept as history rather than as a cache — which until round 9 read as a performance question and is now the only thing that could make the unanswerable question answerable. Caching is §10's subject; history is F-7's.
    • Something has to keep count of how big expressions get, because nothing refuses them any more. D-105 deleted the height bound along with the parameter that held it, and refused to mint a replacement, so the size of an audience's expansion is bounded only by acyclicity and "a check costs a bounded amount" is deleted from this system's properties — that property came from the bound and nowhere else. What replaces it is a standing report: the widest and deepest audiences, their closure size, and their cost per check, kept running rather than computed once at save. It is a report with no CI behind it — a convention, not a guard (D-081), and it is the third control of that shape this module depends on. Say it in those words wherever the old bound is missed, because the failure mode of a report nobody reads is that it stops being read and nothing else happens. The question of who reads it is itself open — F-21 asks exactly that, and this bullet is the mechanism F-21 is asking about.** The save-time walk that computes it is the same walk that checks acyclicity (§The expression tree), so the number costs nothing extra to obtain; what costs something is deciding to look at it.

    authorization_channel and authorization_ref_id are what make this an audit trail rather than a log. A record saying "person 100 changed person 200's address" answers what and who. A record that also names the guardianship_permission row that authorized it answers why they were allowed to — and whether that authority was valid at the time. When a custody dispute arises eighteen months later, that is the question asked.

    The channel enum got smaller in round 10 and the audit answer got better, which is worth saying in the same breath. item_grant and unit_grant are now the only two ways a permission is granted, and both name a row the auditor can open. Under the ladder, a row saying role plus an assignment id answered "by what authority" with a bundle — the auditor still had to expand manage into codes to learn what the person could actually do, and the table doing the expansion was permission_level. Now the row names the grant, and the grant names one code. That is the one place where deleting the levels makes the trail more useful rather than less.

    denied results are logged too. A series of denials is the signature of someone probing for access they do not have. Logging only successes discards the signal.

    before_state/after_state must be redacted, and the sentence is the reason this document is cited everywhere else. Copying a value out of a guarded record into a table guarded by different people, on a different path, for longer than the record is kept, produces a copy with less protection than the original — and the copy is the one that gets read by auditors, exported for reports and mirrored into backups. Redaction therefore happens before the insert, in the logging function, and never in a job that might not run — the ordering is the part of this rule with teeth, and it survives D-072 taking the trigger that used to enforce it.

    One clause of that sentence is now bounded, and the bounded half is not the half that mattered. For longer than the record is kept was written when this table's state columns and audit_log's payloads were both permanent. If F-8 is confirmed, the state columns expire on the window D-088 proposes for the change trail, which inverts that arithmetic: the copy becomes shorter-lived than the business row it was copied from. The conclusion is unmoved, because what the rule guards is the audience. A window decides how long a copied value is readable; it does not decide who may read it while it is readable, and the readers here are whoever holds audit.view, not whoever may see the record. A sensitive value copied into a less-protected table is a protection failure on the day it is copied, not only for as long as it then survives.

    How far the argument reaches, now that the person record has no sensitive corner. It is an argument about the copy's guard, not about the copy's contents, and reading it as an instruction to empty payloads is the mistake this section made once and corrected. D-083 removed the subset that a field list selects from — everything about a person beyond name, gender and age is a value in person_attribute (09-attributes.md) — which rules out name-keyed scrubbing of personal data for good: there is no list, and allergy is not on it. It leaves two conclusions, and they point in opposite directions from the obvious one:

    • The credential case stays, and it is the only one a name list can serve. Values where being in a log at all is the harm — secrets, tokens, credentials — are stripped on the way in, by key name, everywhere a listed key turns up (06-audit-and-import.md §2.2).
    • The person-data case is answered by grading the copy, not by thinning it. audit_log holds before and after images of a child's descriptive values, because D-085 made that trail the only history those values have. So "the copy must not be less guarded than the original" is a requirement on audit.view's sensitivity mark — the top tier, beside person.view — and not a rule about which keys may appear in a jsonb column. Written as a requirement in 00-conventions.md §8 item 7 and demanded of the catalog in ../appendix/permission-catalog.md §8; erasure reaching those payloads stays absolute (06-audit-and-import.md §4).

    Append-only enforcement:

    Any update or delete against access_log is refused row by row, before it happens, with an error saying the table is append-only. Nothing is left to the application remembering this.

    That sentence has two exceptions, and written as a blanket refusal it defeats the retention policy. Emptying a row's payloads once it passes the window below is an update, and so is scrubbing one person's values out of a row after an erasure. Neither is a correction: nobody is making a version of this table right, and the answer the row gives to who, what, when, by what authority is untouched in both cases. So both fall inside what "append-only" forbids in spirit and outside what it may forbid in practice. State the guard as a refusal of rewrites rather than a refusal of writes — the only actor allowed to change a row is the erasure or the reduction, the only columns either may touch are the state columns, and every other field on the row is immutable. The literal version of this rule is the more dangerous one, because it fails silently and what it fails is the policy. The same reasoning applies to audit_log and is stated with the operations themselves in 06-audit-and-import.md §2.2.

    Retention: the same window as audit_log's payloads, as a proposal rather than a decision. This paragraph used to read "never delete them — the partition retention policy is a legal question, not a technical one", and the second half of that is still exactly right. What has changed is that the decision which bounded the change trail's payloads (D-088) proposes the same window here — 24 months on the state columns, rows kept, 12 months as the floor — and records it as F-8, which still needs confirming. Until it is confirmed, that is a default awaiting a person and not this document's position.

    Why the question is weaker here, which is structural and not luck: this table does not hold the contents of reads. before_state and after_state are populated for write actions only, so a read records who asked, for what, under which permission, through which channel, and whether it was allowed — and stops. Recording what the read saw would copy every record a permitted glance touched into a table read by a wider audience than the record itself, which is the exact act this section argues against two paragraphs above. So the window here protects considerably less than it protects over in audit_log, and what survives it is everything this table was built to answer. The valuable fact about a read is that it happened and by what authority, and that is precisely the part no reduction touches.

    Consider partitioning by month once volume justifies it, and moving old partitions to cheaper storage. Rows are not deleted on either answer; the window is carried out by emptying the state columns, which is the job and the over-age check described for the change trail in 06-audit-and-import.md §5. If F-8 is confirmed, that check has to be written for this table too — a reduction with no check under it is a reduction that quietly stops happening, which is the failure mode §2.2 of that document names as the only one this operation has.

    Assumption: access_log may be queried by staff with a specific permission, but writing is automatic. If the organization needs human annotation of audit entries, add a separate access_log_note table rather than making this table mutable.

    10. The resolver¶

    Steps the resolver leans on¶

    Three helpers keep can() readable, and one of them is fifteen lines long.

    grant_satisfies(person, scope_type, scope_id, permission, at) — does the person hold a live allow of this code on this exact scope? Exact match only. It covers both a personal grant and one made to an audience the person is in. Any one grant row meeting all of these satisfies it:

    1. Its scope_type equals the checked scope type and its scope_id matches the checked scope id, with an absent id equal to an absent checked id.
    2. Its permission_id is the checked permission, or is system.admin.
    3. Its effect is allow — denies are handled by the channel that runs first.
    4. It has not been revoked.
    5. It has started as of at and has not ended on at.
    6. The grantee is this person, or an audience containing them at at for this item (§8).

    Two of the old seven conditions are gone, and both were the level. There is no permission_level row to join and no rank to compare, which means the helper now asks a question with a yes-or-no answer instead of one with a threshold. level_satisfies() is deleted with them — it existed only to compare a grant's level with the level a permission required at a scope type, and with no levels its arguments do not exist.

    person_in_audience(person, audience, at, item) — is the person in the value of this audience's expression on this date? It gained the item argument in this round, because a bound operand means different people on different items and there is no other way to tell it which event it is asking about (§8, Evaluation). An unresolvable operand is the empty set; an expression that cannot be evaluated reaches nobody.

    operand_holds(person, operand, at, item) — the evaluator's one recursive step: a leaf asks its selector, a node combines its children. It is written once and it is short; the requirement inside it is that every selector is evaluated as a predicate about this person and never as a set of ids (§8, Author in sets).

    One leaf does not ask a selector, it asks this function again. AUDIENCE(x) calls person_in_audience(person, <the audience x names>, at, item) and returns what comes back (D-103) — the same three arguments carried straight through, because a nested audience means the same people on the same date for the same item, and the same predicate shape, because nesting is the moment at which a lazy implementation would most plausibly decide to materialize the inner set and test containment. That is §11's check 16 and it covers recursion explicitly: an operand cache keyed on (person, selector_kind, selector_arg_id, …) already has a key for a nested audience, which is what makes the recursion shallow in practice and not merely shallow in argument. The recursion terminates by construction, not by a counter: a cycle through AUDIENCE leaves cannot be saved (§8), so a read-path depth cap would be a thing that decides who a grant reaches in exactly the case where the data is already wrong. D-105 did not change this sentence; it changed what the sentence is standing on. It used to be a back-up for a guard that existed at save time, and now the save-time cycle walk is the guard and this refusal is the whole design — which is why the walk must not be relaxed along with the bound it travelled with, and why "the recursion terminates by construction" is a claim about a write-path check rather than about a number.

    Deleted from this list: scope_exposure_covers_assignment(org_unit_id, role_assignment_id, at) (the exposure table is gone, D-097) and person_holds_gate_exempt(person, at) (the gate is gone, D-100). The second deletion retires a hazard with it. That helper had to be carefully kept out of can() — routed through the resolver, a lapsed holder of the exemption would be denied by the very rule it existed to lift, "a lockout with no error message, on the one day of the year it would hurt most". There is no such lockout any more, because there is no gate to lapse through. The special case is gone rather than fixed, and the lockout D-051 existed to survive is listed as retired below, in §12.

    The single authorization entry point. One implementation, used everywhere. Algorithm: ../domain/06-authorization-model.md.

    can(person, permission_code, scope_type, scope_id, at, owner_person_id) returns allowed or denied. scope_type and scope_id are optional, at defaults to today, and owner_person_id is supplied only where the ownership or guardianship channel is in play.

    The order of these steps is a rule for one reason and not the other. Each step either returns or falls through, so a step never gets to contradict an earlier one. The deny step must run first, and that is the only ordering with a consequence for the answer. The allow steps are alternatives producing the same boolean, so their order decides which channel the audit names and how quickly the answer arrives — never what the answer is. That is exactly what the deletion of locality means, and it is worth stating here because "the tiers are ordered and the first sufficient one wins" is a sentence this document repeated for nine rounds and no longer believes.

    1. Look the permission up by code. If no permission carries that code, return false — an unknown permission is a deny, and it is settled before anything else is considered.
    2. Resolve merge chains. Replace the person with the survivor of any merge, so permissions are never evaluated for a tombstoned person.

    3. Channel 0 — explicit deny. Absolute and scope-independent. A live deny grant of this code applicable to this person blocks access no matter what scope it sits at: a per-event grant can never restore access that a unit-wide restriction removed (D-013). A deny counts when it is itself unrevoked, has started as of at, has not ended on at, and applies to the person — where "applies" means it names them or names an audience they are in at at for the item being checked. A personal deny and an audience deny are equally absolute, and a personal allow cannot beat an audience deny, because channel 0 has already returned. Its scope applies when either of these holds:

    4. it sits at the unit, and then it applies everywhere — every item of every type, because a unit grant reaches every item and a unit deny is the same shape with the opposite effect;

    5. it sits on the exact thing being checked — same scope type, same id.

    There is no third bullet, and the second half of that sentence is the round. The old list had a third: it sits on a unit and the check is about that same unit, with a note that a deny on a unit covered only that unit because authority does not flow down. That case is now the first bullet — there is one unit and it is not a row — and the ancestor walk the note refused to perform has no function left to refuse it. Any such grant: return false. 4. Channel 1 — a grant at the unit. grant_satisfies(person, 'unit', NULL, permission, at). Because a unit grant reaches every item of every type (§6), this one step is the tier that used to be three: the organization grant, the unit grant, and the exposure branch that connected them. 5. Channel 2 — a grant on this exact item. grant_satisfies(person, scope_type, scope_id, permission, at), reached only when both a scope type and a scope id were supplied. This is the step can_event() used to precede, and nothing precedes it now: a unit grant reaches the item, an item grant reaches the item, and there is no owner to ask (D-102). 6. Channel 3 — ownership. If an owner was supplied and that owner resolves to this same person, return true. Your own data. 7. Channel 4 — guardianship. Consulted when the check is about someone other than the actor; answers with an allow only. The test is a comparison, not a name: an owner was supplied, it resolves to a real person, and that person is not the actor. The question is whether the actor holds a live guardianship over that subject at at whose right_level covers the checked permission — and this is where permission.right_kind is read, by its only customer: none covers nothing, view covers codes marked read, write covers both. If such a guardianship exists and covers the permission, return true. In every other case — no guardianship, none, or a right that does not cover this code — the channel returns nothing at all and the resolver continues. It does not deny. Defined in 04-relationships.md §4; the reasoning for both halves is D-079.

    Two properties of this channel survive, and the third changed shape:

    • Entered by shape, not by namespace (D-079, following D-074). A parent correcting their child's address is checked under person.address.edit — an ordinary, un-prefixed code — and still routes here. A permission added next year for a field a guardian must edit is covered the day the field exists, provided it is marked write, which is the seeding default (§2).
    • Silent rather than final. Under the old prefix rule this channel gave the last word. It still does not take it, and with a boolean answer there is no longer even a shortfall to fall through: a guardian whose right does not cover the code simply does not answer, and the actor's own grants get their turn. A real prohibition is still possible and still absolute — it is a deny row, step 3.
    • ~~Gate-exempt.~~ Withdrawn by D-100, and this was the property that made the channel need explaining. A guardian whose own membership lapsed had not stopped being the child's guardian, so the gate had to be declared not to apply here. Now nothing is declared, because no channel is gated: MEMBERS is an operand some writer typed into some expression, and guardianship is not an expression.

    The rest of this channel's older commentary survives unchanged and is not repeated here: the guard must not be weakened into "the subject is a minor" (the existence of a guardianship row is the whole test, and an age lookup would put a branch in the resolver that D-070 and D-077 rule out), and personal attributes reach this resolver through no new door — everything beyond name, gender and age is descriptive data read by person.view and written by person.edit through the channels above, with no attribute.* read permission, no fifth channel, and no branch on what a value is about (09-attributes.md §4, D-083). The temptation that paragraph refuses — "a leader may see allergy data, but only for the children in their unit" — has lost half its grammar in round 10: there is no "their unit" to refer to, and what remains of the refusal is the older and better reason that a branch on content is the privileged medical subset returning through the resolver after D-083 refused it through the permission list. 8. Default deny. Return false.

    There is no membership gate anywhere in this function, and that is the deletion to notice while reading it. Nine rounds put one clause in the role channel — AND (NOT r0.requires_membership OR is_current_member(p_person_id) OR person_holds_gate_exempt(p_person_id, p_at)) — and called it the thing most likely to be "optimized" away. All three disjuncts are gone: the first with the column, the second into the MEMBERS operand, the third with the permission (D-051 deleted entirely). The predicate is_current_member survives inside the evaluator, evaluated live at at like every other operand, and the three old shapes map exactly:

    Old mechanism Expression now
    The gate applied to a role with requires_membership = true MEMBERS * ROLE(x)
    The gate waived for a whole role ROLE(x) — written without MEMBERS
    The gate waived for one named person MEMBERS * ROLE(x) + PERSON(y) — the exception inside the expression that granted the rule, so it is read next to the thing it exempts. A second grant aimed at that person alone is equivalent and arguably clearer for something permanent; what was never allowed is a row that does not parse (§8, PERSON)

    The posture reversed from default-on to opt-in, and the new failure mode is named rather than buried (D-100). Forgetting MEMBERS is now invisible, silent and permanent, and nothing in the resolver can see it. Three controls, none of which is the resolver: the standing report of ROLE(...) grants with no MEMBERS (check 6, §11); seeded grants carrying MEMBERS so that the path of least repetition is the safe path; and import completeness — MEMBERS * COUNSELORS is only as correct as the counselor roster, so an import that misses 300 counselors silently un-gates them, and the reconciliation report of D-090 must count people marked as counselors in the corpus against role_holder rows written. It fails closed — nothing breaks, which is exactly why nobody notices.

    Cache invalidation, revisited¶

    The answer cache and the operand cache are two different things, and round 10 separated them because D-101 made operand results reusable in a way the old union-of-arms model never was.

    Fields (permission_cache)

    Field Kind Notes
    person_id reference to person, required
    permission_code text, required The code, as checked.
    scope_type text, optional
    scope_id integer, optional
    allowed boolean, required The answer that was computed.
    computed_at timestamp, audit time
    expires_at timestamp, required How long the answer may still be used.

    Rules

    • One row per person, permission code, scope type and scope id — where an absent scope type and an absent scope id each count as a key value of their own, not as "any scope".

    An operand cache beside it, keyed (person, selector_kind, selector_arg_id) for an operand that does not bind, and (person, selector_kind, selector_arg_id, item) for a bound one (D-102: the cache key for a bound operand must include the item). A bound operand cached without the item in its key is a wrong answer waiting for the next event — PLACED is different people on every event, and a cache that does not know which event it answered will answer for all of them. The win is that an operand result computed for one grant is reused by every grant that names the same operand, which the three-arm model could not do because its answer was a walk rather than a value.

    An AUDIENCE leaf is an ordinary operand for keying purposes and an extraordinary one for invalidation (D-103). Ordinary, because (person, AUDIENCE, <audience id>[, item]) is exactly the shape above and needs no new column. Extraordinary, because the answer under that key depends on rows belonging to a different audience than the one being evaluated — which is the paragraph below, and why it had to be rewritten in this round rather than left alone.

    Indexes

    • on expires_at, for the expiry sweep;
    • on person_id, because a membership transition must invalidate by person, fast — MEMBERS is now an operand, so every cached answer that depends on it is this person's.

    Invalidated on any write to role_holder, person_group_member, access_grant, audience, audience_operand, guardianship_permission, a person merge — and on every membership transition, including the yearly expiry sweep, which touches every member at once. Read the audience and audience_operand entries as wider than they look: a write to one audience's tree invalidates every audience that can reach it, not only the one written — see the hard case below. The list lost one entry when D-105 deleted audience_member, and it is worth naming the loss precisely rather than letting it read as a tidying: an insert into that table used to be an invalidation event that could not collide with anybody else's insert, whereas adding a name today rewrites the expression and therefore rewrites audience_operand rows — the same trigger, reached by a slower and less concurrent route, which is the two-writers hazard stated in §What LIST is, said plainly — withdrawn by D-105. The person merge entry is now load-bearing in a way it was not (§A hand-named person is now a keyless reference): a merge must repoint the audience_operand leaves that name the absorbed person, in all five argument kinds that name an entity, and invalidating the cache afterwards is not the same act — a repointing that is missed does not invalidate anything, because nothing was written, and the cached answer and the truth agree with each other while both name nobody.

    The shape of the hard case changed, and the lesson did not — and the case itself is now two. Under D-091 the dangerous write was audience_include: one edge three levels down silently changed every group above it, and the code saving the edge had no list of what depended on it, so these documents assumed the blunt answer — invalidate the audience-derived entries wholesale.

    Round 10 said the graph was gone and the hazard with it, and D-103 half-took that back. A write to veterans now changes the value of every audience whose expression names AUDIENCE(veterans), and of every grant aimed at those — and the code saving veterans still has no list of what depends on it. The hazard came back in a genuinely smaller form, and the difference is worth naming rather than glossing: the edges are rows in the table that already holds every expression, so the direct referrers are one indexed query on (selector_kind, selector_arg_id), and D-103 already requires that query for a different purpose (refusing to delete a referenced audience, §8). The reverse closure is therefore computable where it once was not — which is a real improvement and still not a reason to compute it inside an invalidation path, because "computable" and "cheap enough to run on every write" are different claims and the second is the one that gets decided wrongly under load. The blunt answer stands: invalidate audience-keyed operand entries wholesale.

    The second hard case is a table, not a graph. Adding one person to an office changes every grant whose expression names ROLE(that_office) — including grants written later by other people for reasons the writer has never seen — and the code that inserts a holder row has no list of those grants either. Neither invalidation can be keyed on the rows that were written. Both are listed here rather than left to whoever implements them for the same reason the membership sweep always was: the expensive invalidation is the one that is easy to decide is unnecessary.

    Properties to verify when reviewing this function¶

    1. Deny is checked before everything, including ownership and guardianship. A restricted account's owner permissions are denied by a deny row if that is what the organization wants.
    2. at is threaded through every branch. Permission checks are time-travel capable, and an operand evaluated at "now" inside a check performed at a past date is a wrong answer that looks like a right one.
    3. There is no wildcard except system.admin. No prefix match, no implied capability. If a holder can do a thing, a row says so (D-068) — and now a row says which thing, because there is no level standing in for a set of them.
    4. resolve_person is called on entry, so a tombstoned person's id still resolves.
    5. A deny is scope-independent. Channel 0 matches a unit deny and an exact-item deny and does not walk ancestors — so a deny at the unit blocks every item, which is what makes a suspension absolute.
    6. There is no hierarchy to walk, and nothing left to assert about it. ~~unit_ancestors() must not appear in this function~~ — the function itself is deleted (D-097), which is a stronger guarantee than the CI assertion that replaced it in round 8 and then died with the SQL in D-072. The surviving time-bomb is a different one — no permission path may materialize a set of people — and it is check 16 (§11).
    7. Audience grants are evaluated through the same channels as personal ones, via grant_satisfies(). A grant to EVERYONE and a grant to a named person differ only in how grantee is satisfied, never in how the scope or the code is resolved.
    8. Expression evaluation is a walk over a forest that fails closed. An unresolvable operand is the empty set, and an expression that cannot be assembled reaches nobody (§8, Evaluation item 5). The failure this property forbids is a helpful implementation: a walk that returns what it managed to reach when something downstream breaks turns a broken expression into a widening, and a widening is the only direction in which this channel can do harm. Forest, not tree, since D-103 — the walk crosses an audience boundary at an AUDIENCE leaf, and a partially-evaluated outer tree over a failed inner one is precisely the shape that produces a widening.
    9. A check that supplies no item cannot answer a bound operand, and must not guess one. PLACED with nothing to bind to is not "everyone" and not "nobody at this unit" — it is a caller error, and the answer is denial with a denial_reason that says so. This is the new cousin of the unscoped-check caveat below, and it is enforced in the same place: the calling convention, not the data.
    10. right_kind is read in exactly one place. If a second comparison of read against write appears anywhere else in the resolver, that is a mechanism being built on a column D-099 documents as having one customer.
    11. A nested audience is evaluated at the caller's at and the caller's item, with neither re-defaulted (D-103). The recursion has to carry all three arguments through or it is not the same question being asked again, and of the two, the silently wrong one is the date: an inner audience re-defaulted to today answers "was she in it?" with is she in it, which is the exact substitution property 2 exists to forbid, one level down and invisible in every test that checks only one audience.

    A caveat on an unscoped check. "Can this person do X anywhere?" returns true if any grant grants it. That is correct for UI decisions like "show the admin menu?" but wrong for enforcement. Every enforcing call must pass a scope. Enforce this in the calling convention — make scope mandatory at the API layer for mutating operations.

    The cache definition and its invalidation rules are above, immediately after the resolver — they are part of the resolver's contract, not a separate concern.

    The person-merge case remains the easiest to forget — see 01-identity.md.

    11. Invariants to verify¶

    These were queries. They are now invariants to be verified by something, in whichever way the implementation chooses, and what follows states what each one counts and what a non-zero result would mean. They are renumbered for round 10, because several lost their subjects (requires_scope, the membership gate, system.gate_exempt, permission_level, the three scope_exposure checks) and the survivors were closed up rather than left with holes.

    Three of the old checks were CI assertions that read the text of the stored resolver to prove a rule was still inside it (14, 15 and 19 in the previous numbering). Nothing can read that text any more. Their successors below are written as assertions about behaviour rather than about program text, because that is the strongest form still available, and D-081 requires saying so: a prohibition nobody runs is a convention, not a guard.

    Invariants to verify

    • 1. Live grants for a person who has been merged away. An open access_grant whose person has been merged into somebody else. A row means authority is still attached to a tombstone; it should have been closed or moved at merge time.
    • 2. Orphaned item grants, and orphaned selector arguments. Per scope type, generated from grant_scope_type: a grant whose scope_id names no row in the item table it points at — and the same scan over audience_operand.selector_arg_id, which is polymorphic for the same reason and takes the same mitigation. A row is exactly the risk the polymorphic columns accept (§6, D-011, extended to operands by D-101), and this is that trade's compensating control. An orphaned operand is the quiet half: it resolves to the empty set, so the grant is inert rather than dangerous, and inert authority is the failure nobody notices.

    Since D-103 the scan covers one more target kind, and this is the one place its asymmetry has to be stated rather than assumed away. selector_arg_id now also points at audience, and an orphaned AUDIENCE leaf is not necessarily quiet: quietness is a property of the leaf's position, not of the leaf. Reached through + or * it removes people; reached through - it adds everyone the missing audience was excluding. So run the scan, and route its output to check 7's second report rather than to this one's — an orphaned ROLE leaf belongs with inert authority and an orphaned AUDIENCE leaf belongs with the grants that got wider, which is the same fact about the operator being the thing that decides the direction of a failure, said once in §8's evaluation rules and again here because a reviewer reading only this list would otherwise file all of it under "inert". In practice: one scan, two outputs, splitting on whether the orphan sits under an odd number of - right-hand positions.

    And since D-105 this scan is a backstop and not the mechanism, which is a downgrade in status and has to be said out loud. Every hand-named person in the system is now a leaf in this column with no key behind it, so a person merge and a redaction are required to repoint those leaves in all five argument kinds that name an entity (§8, A hand-named person is now a keyless reference). The scan cannot stand in for that requirement, for two reasons. It runs the following morning, and an expression resolves wrong from the moment of the merge. And it only fires when the absorbed person's row is actually removed — if the merge leaves the row in place, as any design that keeps its history will, the leaf still names an existing id, this check reports nothing, and the grant quietly keeps a person who no longer signs in. On the allow side that is inert — the failure narrows the set and stops there; under - the same failure widens it, and EVERYONE - PERSON(x) with an un-repointed x denies everybody. D-106 retired this bullet's earlier word for the second half — loud — and the replacement is worth the typographical trouble, because the two halves do not differ in volume, they differ in which direction a wrong answer moves access, and only one of those directions can grant. - 3. A grant at a scope its code forbids. An open grant whose scope_id is present while the permission's applies_to is unit — or any grant of a system. permission whose applies_to is not unit, which is the seeding rule §2 depends on. Zero expected, and a row means the save-time check was skipped, not that a constraint was disabled — no constraint spans those two tables. This is the report that stands in for a database rule, which makes it worth alerting on. - 4. A bound operand where it cannot bind. An audience_operand whose selector has binds_to_item = true and which sits in an audience aimed at a unit-scope grant, or whose selector_type.argument_type differs from the item type of every grant aimed at that audience. Zero expected for the same reason as 3: refused on save, unenforced by any constraint, and a row means the write path was bypassed. PLACED aimed at a group, or ORGANIZERS aimed at nothing, should be unsaveable, and this check catches the build in which they are not. - 5. A deny that denies nothing. A live deny whose grantee, scope and code match no live allow anywhere. Probably intended as a removal rather than as a restriction; verify the intent. D-099 requires this one by name: a deny that denies nothing is how a stale restriction survives its reason, and the resolver will never tell anyone it is dead, because a deny never needs to. - 6. A grant naming an office with no MEMBERS. A live grant whose audience expression contains a ROLE(...) operand and no MEMBERS operand anywhere in the tree (D-100's standing report). A row is not an error — writing an office without the gate is a legitimate act and is exactly how an appointed officer keeps serving while their own fee is unpaid — but it is the case worth a human eye, because it authorizes a lapsed member silently and forever. This is the successor to the old "leavers with access" and "holders of the gate exemption" checks, and it is deliberately narrower than either: the report has to be short enough to actually be read, which the two it replaces were not. - 7. A grant aimed at an audience that reaches nobody — or everybody. A live access_grant naming an audience whose expression contains no operand that can hold a person — a sum of PERSON(x) leaves naming people who have been merged away, a GROUP(x) whose group has ended, an operand whose selector_arg_id is orphaned, an AUDIENCE(x) leaf whose target has ended or gone (§8, and D-103), a tree that cannot be assembled. The grant join is what makes this check worth running: an empty audience on its own is a work in progress, but an empty audience a live grant points at is a permission configured, visible in an editor as granted, and reaching nobody — the same inert-authority shape the previous version of this document recorded for a rule that matched no person, and the one an administrator cannot see from the grant screen.

    Run the same scan for the mirror-image case and report it separately, because it is the more dangerous of the two: an empty operand reached through - makes the expression wider, so EVERYONE - AUDIENCE(x) with x gone reaches everybody — an allow that meant to exclude now includes, and a deny that meant to exclude now falls on everyone. D-103 kept fail-closed evaluation because of exactly this case, and this check used to call the case "loud" — the word D-106 retired, because a widening is not a noise, it is a direction: a wide failure is only better than a narrow one when somebody is reading, which is what this check is for and nothing more. What D-106 added is a second column on the report F-21 asks for — naming which leaf failed, not merely that one did — aimed at the audience's owner, which is the same shape as this check: a listing with somebody's name beside the row. So the control here is report, twice over, and not a refusal: nothing in this round stops an audience whose leaf is broken from being saved, and the round's premise is why (D-107). MEMBERS - AUDIENCE(x) is the narrow version — ~~the quiet version~~, again in the retired word — and it is the easier one to miss: the exclusion is the grant's whole point and its target is gone, which reads on screen as a grant that still names the audience it excludes. The save-time refusal (an audience named by anything live cannot be ended or deleted) is the primary control; this check is the one that fires when the refusal was bypassed by a restore or a manual delete, and unlike the empty side it should alert rather than be read weekly. - 8. A grant whose expression contains EVERYONE. Live, and naming EVERYONE anywhere in the tree. Not a violation: it is what a public page is for, and holding it directly was always legal. It is on the list because a language with union in it lets one edit widen a grant to every person with no account and no membership, and the person who made the edit is the only control (§8, What audiences do not do). - 9. Holders of system.admin. Everyone with a live allow of it, however they came by it — through a personal grant or through an expression naming an office. This should be a very short list, and it should alert on change. - 10. Expression trees that are not trees. Any of: a node with a selector, or a leaf with an operator; a node with one child; a row whose audience_id differs from its parent's; a row whose parent_id names no row; an audience with zero or several root rows. Zero expected, and every shape here means the write path let a tree be assembled one row at a time. §8's first rule is the one check a migration can state; the rest of the list cannot be, which is exactly why it is here. Nesting did not relax any of it: AUDIENCE(x) is a leaf pointing at another audience's root, never at one of its interior rows, so a tree still has one parent per node and one audience per tree — and a row whose parent_id crosses an audience boundary is this check firing, not a feature. - 11. A delegation that cannot expire. A live access_grant with is_delegated true and no valid_to. The row rule forbids it and no constraint enforces it. Half of the old delegation check is gone: "or whose source assignment has ended" had an assignment to point at, and delegation_of is deleted (§4), so expiry is now the only guard against a delegation outliving its reason. - 12. Two names that are one name, and a reference that points at an unnamed thing. Three shapes, one namespace: 1. A role.code equal to a person_group.code after case, whitespace and separator folding; 2. either of those equal to a selector_type.code, or any of the three equal to an audience.code — the third table is in the namespace now, because AUDIENCE(<code>) resolves through it (D-103); 3. an AUDIENCE leaf whose selector_arg_id names an audience whose code is absent — legal on every column of both rows and unsayable as a constraint, because the check reads a third table (F-10 shape 3).

    Zero expected; no single constraint spans three tables, and no constraint at all covers the reserved-word half or the null-code half, so the write path carries all of it and this check asserts the write path exists. A row here is not untidy: a group called MEMBERS would silently rewrite the meaning of every expression in the system, and an audience sharing an office's code is the same accident one arm over — the kind of the name decides which set a bare expression meant, and after D-103 there are three kinds that can collide instead of two. - 13. Orphaned permissions. An assignable permission that no live grant names, at any scope. This check exists because of D-068 and survives D-100's deletion of role_permission by retargeting: the net was "no role grants it", and now nothing grants anything, so the question has to be asked of the grants themselves. Expected: empty after a seeding pass. A row means either a permission nobody has bundled into an office yet — fine on a roadmap, wrong in production — or a module was added and its grants were not. Note what it cannot see: a permission granted to an office but not to the office that arguably should have it. That residual is the accepted cost of D-068 and nothing closes it; it is a human reading the starter list in §12 against the catalog. - 14. Offices nobody holds, and holder rows pointing at an office that is gone. The first is expected and is not an error: a role nobody holds is either one about to be used or one stopped using and not deleted, and there is no third possibility, because a role has no period (D-053) and cannot be switched off, only held or not held. Delete the mistakes, keep the ones on the roadmap. The second should be zero — the delete guard on role enforces it, and the check exists because a restored dump or a manually disabled guard bypasses it, and the loss is unrecoverable. - 15. TIME-BOMB: an expression must be refused at the write, never survived at the read. The successor to the old inclusion-cycle assertion, same shape, and it asserts a guarantee rather than a set of rows. Four halves are listed, and the last is the one that fails silently — one of the four is now retired rather than active, which is what half 2 is doing there. 1. A cycle must be unsayable, and there are now two walks to try. Assert that saving is refused for both shapes — a node whose parent chain revisits a row inside one tree, and an AUDIENCE leaf whose target audience reaches back to the audience being saved, at any distance (D-103). The second half is the one this document got wrong, in D-101's words and therefore in its own: it claimed the write-time cycle walk had been deleted because a tree cannot cycle, and a forest of audiences naming each other is not a tree. Both are tested as a test that tries, not one that counts, because after the fact there are none to count. Zero is the expected result every time this runs, and a non-zero result is not bad data but a missing guard. 2. RETIRED, not answered: depth must be refused at the same moment, over the expanded tree. D-105 deleted selector_depth_limit, and with it the check this half asserted — so there is no save-time size rule left to test, and an assertion here would test nothing. The number is kept in the list so that the halves keep the numbers other pages cite, and because what this half said about the bound outlived it: a bound measured per-audience was a bound on nothing, since five nested audiences three deep are fifteen deep and each passed a local check; and the bound bounded height while an expression's cost is proportional to the number of audiences it reaches, which is breadth — fifty + AUDIENCE(...) leaves at depth one were legal under any depth bound and cost fifty fetches per check. Both observations stand, and now nothing bounds either shape at all. What survives operationally is not an invariant but the standing report of the widest and deepest audiences and their cost per check (§8, The cost of composition): no CI job, no refused save, nothing that fails if the report is not read. That is D-081's case stated in its purest form — the rule's name outlived its mechanism, and this line exists to say which of the two happened here. A retired invariant is not a passing test, so nothing here asserts anything about size: a build that gets an enormous expression is a green build, and F-21 is the open question about who reads what is left. Half 3 is therefore the whole structural story now: acyclicity at save, and nothing else. 3. No sharing table may exist, and the read path must grow neither a depth cap nor a cycle check. The tree's one-parent rule is worth nothing if a later round adds a side table letting two expressions share a node — editing the shared node would silently widen every grant using it, which is D-091's inclusion hazard re-imported. AUDIENCE is not that table and must not become it: it points at an audience's whole expression, never at an interior node. And a forest met at read time by a hop counter is survivable-looking, so whoever meets one adds a cap, and the cap becomes the thing that decides who a grant reaches — which is the reason the read path must depend on the write guard rather than back it up. This refusal used to be the back-up for half 2 and is now the entire design (D-105), which is why the temptation it guards against has got stronger rather than weaker: with no bound anywhere, a hop counter looks like the obvious way to make a check cheap, and the moment one exists it is deciding who a grant reaches. A build that passes half 1 and fails this one is a build where the write guard has been removed and papered over, and it is the specific shape D-103 makes easy to reach because nesting gives the read path somewhere to want a counter. 4. A referenced audience may not be emptied from the other end. Saving must refuse to end or delete an audience while a live grant, or a live audience reachable from a live grant, names it (§8, Rules). It is a save-path refusal like the others and it belongs to this assertion for the same reason: the data after the fact is perfectly well-formed — an ended audience and a leaf naming it are both legal rows — and the damage is that every expression doing X - <that one> silently got wider. Of the four halves this is the one most likely to be implemented as a warning, because a delete that pops a confirmation dialog feels like sufficient care and a refuse feels like an obstacle. It is the one where a warning is not enough.

    Why this is a CI assertion and not a report: three of the four guards are walks over rows rather than comparisons within one row, which means they live in application code and no migration can emit them (F-10 shape 3). A guard that lives in code is deleted by a refactor and the data looks fine afterwards — which is why D-072's removal of the build-time greps is recorded as a weakening rather than a cleanup. - 16. TIME-BOMB: no permission path may materialize a set. The requirement is that operand_holds decides membership for one person and never loads a set of ids to test containment in. An operand is a named set — a row of selector_type, which is the only test the language has — for the purposes of the editor's preview and the cache key; in the resolver it is a predicate, and D-101 writes the distinction down precisely because an implementation is one lazy join away from getting it the other way and paying three thousand rows per check. ~~An operand is a named, materializable set~~ for the purposes of this check: that was the wording here while §8 still claimed materializability was the test for what an operand may be, and D-107 deleted the test, so this bullet carries its third and last surviving instance. The assertion itself does not move an inch: OLDER(18) is precisely as materializable, and precisely as unmaterialized, as MEMBERS — a birth date read at the checked date and a membership row read at the checked date are both answers computed per person, and neither is allowed to become a list of ids. This replaces the old "no permission path may inherit" assertion, whose subject — the unit hierarchy — no longer exists, and it is the same kind of rule: a requirement about how the code computes, which no migration can state and no reviewer will notice missing until the first slow day.

    Nesting is where this assertion earns its keep, so assert on a nested expression specifically. A flat ROLE(x) is awkward to write as a set load and natural to write as a predicate; an inner AUDIENCE(veterans) inside an outer expression reads like a job for "fetch the inner set once and reuse it", which is the tempting optimization, is correct as an answer, and is the thing that makes a check slow at exactly the depth the expression happens to have — and since D-105 deleted the depth bound, that is now "at whatever depth somebody typed", with nothing above it. A test exercising only single-level expressions passes on an implementation that materializes at every nesting level. Assert that the recursion takes a person and returns a boolean, and that no path between two audiences hands over a collection. - 17. TIME-BOMB: .admin prefix matching, and any ordering comparison, must stay deleted. Two parts, both prose since D-072 removed the SQL that could be grepped, which that entry records as a real weakening. First, no step of the resolver may build a permission code by concatenating a resource with .admin, and no permission may have admin as its action except exactly one: system.admin. D-074 withdrew the four exemptions D-068 had allowed, so the allow-list is a single code and the check is an equality, not a set membership. Zero rows expected; the point of the entry is that the first version of the second half accepted five exceptions and the second accepts one, because an allow-list is where a wildcard comes back to life. Second, and new: no comparison of access_level ranks, no required_level lookup, and no "at least contribute" anywhere. D-099 notes that the bug D-012 existed to catch — 'admin' < 'contribute' read alphabetically — cannot occur in a system with no levels, which is a better fix than the guard. The one ordering this model still performs is right_kind containment in the guardianship channel, so a build where a second ordering has appeared has not reintroduced a bug; it has started reintroducing the ladder.

    Checks 3, 4, 5, 6 and 9 are the ones to wire to alerts, plus one half of 7.

    • 3 and 4 are the reports that stand in for the database rules that do not exist. A row in either is not data drift — it is a save-path check that stopped running, which is the failure mode §5 of this document used to need a whole table for.
    • 5 and 6 are the two reports round 10 created, one for each mechanism it simplified: a deny that denies nothing, and an office granted without the membership operand. Both are lists a human reads, and both are the entire remaining control on their failure.
    • 9 should email someone every time its result changes — a new holder of system.admin is either a planned change or an incident, and either way someone should know within the hour.
    • Check 7 is the exception to the rule that this list is read rather than alerting on. Its empty half — a grant reaching nobody — is inert and belongs on a weekly list. Its wide half, a grant that got wider because something under a - stopped resolving, is the only entry in §11 that describes authority increasing, and it should go to the same place check 9 does. Splitting 7 is not tidiness: a single report containing both halves has the wide row sitting between nineteen inert ones, which is how a widening gets read.
    • Checks 15 and 16 are CI assertions, not reports. They are the automated guards on the two rules most likely to be "simplified" away when someone optimizes the resolver: the refusal of a malformed expression at the write, and the refusal to compute a set where a predicate will do. Both fail the build rather than silently changing who can do what, and both live in code rather than in the schema, which is why neither can be a migration. D-103 moved weight onto 15 in particular — with nesting available, the write path is guarding a graph rather than a row, and since D-105 retired the depth bound it guards two things and not three: cycles, and not emptying something that is named. The acyclicity walk is therefore the only structural guard left on this module, and check 15's half 3 is the assertion that nobody quietly adds a second one inside the read path.

    12. What the organization starts with¶

    Starting catalog. Expect to edit this heavily; it is a proposal, not a specification.

    This section and ../appendix/permission-catalog.md are the same list written twice, and they drift. When this was reconciled, 22 documented permissions had no seed row — among them a code five of the catalog's own seed roles assigned, and one of the two codes D-068 exists to protect. A further 12 were the event.* set, added to the catalog when events were designed and never carried here. Under the old wildcard much of this was harmless: a role holding a resource's .admin reached a code whether or not the row existed, so the catalog could advertise a capability the database could not name and nothing noticed. The permission editor was the only place the gap showed. D-068 removed that cushion, and round 10 removed a second one: with role_permission gone there is no per-role list left to compare a catalog row against, so a documented code that nothing grants is now invisible in a way it never was. Integrity check 13 reads the grants for exactly that reason.

    There is no SQL check for this — one side is a Markdown file. The discipline is the check: when adding a permission, add it in both places in the same edit, and treat a catalog row that no grant names as a bug of the same class as a missing migration. The reconciliation is mechanical enough to script: a diff of the codes the catalog lists against the codes that exist. Anything in the catalog and not in the database is a permission that cannot be granted; anything in the database and not in the catalog is an undocumented capability. Both were present at the same time.

    Off the catalog now, and named rather than quietly dropped: the eighteen dependent.* codes (a guardian's authority is a right_level on the relationship, not a bundle of permissions — D-074); scope.expose and scope.exposure.review (the table they guarded is deleted — D-097); system.gate_exempt (the gate it waived is deleted — D-100); every position.* code and the appointment workflows behind them (D-100); and the codes that were about bodies rather than people — a unit's renaming, restructuring and closing, and participation in a sub-unit — all deleted with the hierarchy (D-097). role.assign and role.unassign survive but are retargeted at role_holder, as do role.create, role.view, role.edit and role.delete — and role.edit no longer reaches anyone's authority (§3).

    Roles are data, and the organization administers them at runtime. This section seeds the permissions — the vocabulary of things anyone might do — and one role. The role names used throughout the docs are worked examples of composing those permissions into grants, not a fixed list. Creating a role, naming it, filling it and deleting an unused one are all supported operations.

    What is seeded as a role, and it is one row: counselors, marked is_system (D-100). A permanent, organization-wide population is the opposite of D-098's definition of a group ("a temporary set of members that do events together"), and which table it lives in decides the sensitivity that every safeguarding convention in this area hangs from. It ships is_system so an editor refuses to delete it, and it confers nothing: this is a set, and being in it is worth only what a grant naming ROLE(counselors) says (D-005). Consistency requirement, and it is the import's problem too: MEMBERS * ROLE(counselors) is only as correct as that roster, so the import reconciliation of D-090 counts people marked as counselors in the corpus against role_holder rows written.

    Everything else is starter material the organization edits — including the system_admin office the catalog sketches, whose reason for existing is that someone has to hold system.admin before anything can be granted. What §12 must fix, and does, is the shape rather than the list: a system. permission is seeded applies_to = unit, so it can never arrive through an item grant. That is not a configuration choice — it is the structural guarantee that a shared item cannot hand someone authority over the permission system, and it is now carried by a column value instead of by a resolver branch (§2).

    What the organization starts with, in one list. The platform ships:

    • The permission vocabulary — each code marked is_system so it cannot be deleted or renamed — spanning people, accounts, membership, groups, audiences, roles, guardianship, item grants, audit, legacy import, reporting, the thirteen event.* codes, and messaging. The one complete list is ../appendix/permission-catalog.md; this document deliberately no longer keeps a second copy of it, because the two copies disagreed for exactly the reason the note above describes.
    • grant_scope_type: unit (the only row that is not item-scoped), event, person_group, role, and document (deferred).
    • selector_type: the fourteen operands of §8's language, every one of them seeded, is_system and implemented in the shipped evaluator, because there is no fourth combination and no third kind (§8, The selector registry). This registry is what both the grant editor's dropdown and the parser's reserved words read, which is what D-101 means by the language becomes data — a new operand is a decision entry, a seed row and a resolver arm in one release, never a row typed into a screen and never a row shipped ahead of the code that evaluates it. AUDIENCE is the newest operand anyone designed (D-103); PERSON was the last row to arrive for two rounds — it went in thirteenth, because two decision entries had used it for a whole round while this table did not carry it (§8, D-104) — which is the difference between a registry and a list of what somebody remembered. The round after, D-105 deleted LIST and the table came back to twelve: the row that was added last and the row that was deleted last are not the same row, and the count is stated here rather than inferred from either. And twelve is now history in the past tense, not the present: round 12 seeded OLDER and YOUNGER together, so the count moved by two in one decision and nothing was deleted, which makes it the first round since PERSON in which this table only grew (D-107). Their arms ship in the same release as their rows, on the rule two sentences above, and their argument is an integer — the first argument kind here that is not an entity reference, which is why the merge and orphan scans of §11 walk five kinds and not six (§8, A hand-named person is now a keyless reference). GRANTS is refused and therefore absent (§8).
    • Two audiences, both is_system, and both must have a code: everyone, whose expression is EVERYONE, and all_members, whose expression is MEMBERS. The code is not cosmetic any more — since D-103 it is the only way another audience can name them, so a seeded row without one is a piece of the resolver's vocabulary that nothing can compose with, and §11's check 12 is the net for the leaf side of that. Whether code should simply be required on an is_system audience rather than merely seeded is a question this document does not settle (§13). The parameterized seeded audiences are gone, and their three names are worth tracing because each became something smaller: unit_participants is MEMBERS (there is one unit), unit_leaders is ROLE(x) — and it is the case this document had to correct in round 6, because the stored rule matched nobody while every grant aimed at it looked granted, which is the cautionary tale §8's evaluation rules are written against — and guardians_of is the operand GUARDIANS(person), or bound as GUARDIANS on a grant about that person. Nothing needs a row per unit any more, because there is one unit and no per-unit audience to parameterize. What used to be said here — that a member row on either is_system row is refused — is retired rather than answered, because the row it refused no longer exists to be written: audience_member was deleted with the LIST operand, and its is_system refusal is a rule whose mechanism is gone and whose name does not need to survive it (§8, audience_member, re-roled as the LIST leaf — deleted by D-105). What of that sentence still stands is the category it was protecting: seeded rows are the resolver's own vocabulary rather than a set somebody curates, and editing everyone's expression is exactly as much a category error as adding a name to it ever was — the refusal just moved from a row nobody can write to the screen that should not offer the edit.
    • The counselors role, above.
    • One seeded grant, and it replaces a hard-coded sentence: event.answer.view on an event, aimed at an audience whose expression is ORGANIZERS — the code in the catalog's singular spelling, which is the registered one (D-102, amending D-061). "Answers are read by that event's organizers" used to be prose the resolver implemented; now it is a row a reader can find, narrow, or delete, and it is the worked example of a bound operand: item-scoped, argument-less, meaning the organizers of whichever event this grant is on — which is why it is one grant in the seed rather than one per event. D-062 is not contradicted: the appointment still grants nothing by itself, because an operand is a set and not a grant. It is written without MEMBERS and must not be "corrected" to include it: an organizer appointed for one weekend is not required to be a paying member, and D-062's appointment model assumes exactly that.
    • The effects and the enums. allow and deny arrive with the schema, as do the two values of applies_to and the two of right_kind. This is the same argument the five levels used to be given — a comparison the organization can edit at runtime is a security control with no owner — only now there is no comparison to edit, which is the point of D-099. The old sentence about revoked at rank 0 and admin at rank 40 is deleted with the ranks.

    A few of the surviving codes carry a decision in their shape, and those are worth naming:

    • person.merge and person.redact are separate codes, not flavours of person.edit. They are separate for reach, not for irreversibility, and the two words were used as one here until round 9. person.redact is irreversible — that is what an erasure is. person.merge is not: D-087 made reversing a merge a supported operation, with the undo written as its own audit entry. What both codes share is that they act on every row of a person's life rather than on one field, which is the reason they were pulled out of person.edit — and with no person.admin to imply them (D-068), a role that reaches them does so by listing them, visibly.
    • person.view reaches the whole record, and round 10 made it reach further still. Since D-083 there is no privileged corner of the record for it to stop outside: whoever holds it over a person reads their descriptive values — the allergy list, the insurance number, the note about who the child actually lives with — on the same grant that reads their name (09-attributes.md §4). And since D-097, a grant of it at the unit reaches every member in the organization, because that is what "at the unit" now means; the safeguarding property that used to narrow it — access to a body's children granted by that body — is recorded as lost in §5 and in D-097. It is therefore the widest read permission in the system, and the catalog's sensitivity mark is where that has to be visible (D-086): the honest mark is the top tier, worded around whose data a grant exposes rather than around a category of fields, because a category is what D-083 deleted.
    • audit.view is graded against what the trail quotes, not against what a log is usually for. audit_log carries before and after images, and since D-085 those images are the only place a child's past descriptive values exist; reading history has to sit in the same tier as reading a person. D-088 bounds the exposure to 24 months and fixes nothing, and the bound is the realistic control for an organization of this size rather than an argument that the mark is right — see §9 and AT-4 for why two years is not a short interval when the question is about a child.
    • attribute.define is the narrow half of a deliberate pair: values are open to anyone who may already edit the person, so that the fact gets recorded, and codes are held by few people, because the codes are what make the data queryable (09-attributes.md §2). An ordinary permission — no namespace, no resolver branch, no structural consequence.
    • person_group.member.manage is how "joining a group is somebody else's act" is said now (D-098, carrying D-095 across the rename): held at the unit, where it reaches every group, or granted on one group as an item. There is no self-scoped variant and none will be added — nobody writes themselves onto a roster. Its counterpart for offices is role.assign 🔒🔒, and the gap between the two marks is the whole content of D-100's "why two tables".
    • role.unassign is one of the only two things that end authority (the other is a holder row's valid_to), because a role has no period (D-053) and nothing forces a holder row to end (D-071).
    • membership.terminate expels, and is restricted as such; membership.lapse is an administrative end for non-payment. Neither now changes what a role grants — that used to be the interesting part of both, and it was the membership gate's business, which is gone. A lapse changes what MEMBERS means, which is a different thing, in a different place.
    • event.presence.mark stays a separate code from event.manage_participants on purpose, and event.register_self is the eligibility rule (D-060) rather than an expression of one. Under the ladder the first sentence meant "one rank below"; with ranks gone it means what it should have meant all along — a volunteer leader is handed the register without being handed the roster — and that difference is now two grant rows rather than a rung.
    • There is no wildcard at all, and that is worth noticing. dependent.admin was the last deliberate one, and D-074 replaced it with right_level = write, which does the same job without being a permission. The residue is the opposite problem: with no wildcard, nothing implies anything, so a role that should have a new permission must be told (check 13 is the net).
    • Reporting is seeded coarse, and left open on purpose. Only report.view and report.export are starters. Whether reporting is per-report or one permission with row-level filtering is still open (catalog §12). Do not ship both shapes; pick one when reporting is designed.

    system.admin is deliberately a single top-level permission, not a per-resource one. Holding it satisfies everything, and check 9 in §11 alerts on its holders. Keeping the most dangerous capability concentrated and alarmed is safer than spreading equivalents of it across every resource. Under the deleted ladder it was also the level called `admin, which is how this corpus came to have two unrelated things named "admin" and to need a paragraph telling readers they were different. That paragraph is now about one thing.

    13. Open issues¶

    • The complete permission catalog and real role definitions (§12) — needs your input. The question changed shape with D-100: it used to ask which roles are marked requires_membership = false, a per-row switch. There is no switch now, so the question is which starter grants are written with MEMBERS in the expression and which are not — and it has to be answered per grant rather than per role, with check 6 (§11) as the net and the seeded grants as the default.
    • Whether the API should refuse a check that names an item for a unit-only code (§2). applies_to is deliberately a save-time rule, and D-099 says so in as many words, so the mismatched grant cannot exist — but a caller that asks can(person, 'event.create', 'event', 1204, …) gets the unit's answer rather than an error. Assumption, not a ruling: the honest answer is a refusal at the API layer naming the code and the scope, because a caller that asks that question has a bug worth surfacing. Nothing in D-097..D-103 settles it.
    • ~~What the default selector_depth_limit should be (§8).~~ Moot, not answered, and deleted rather than closed (D-105). The bullet asked what number a parameter should hold; the parameter is gone, so there is nothing left to tune, and answering "is 3 right?" is not a possible act. What the bullet had discovered on the way is still open and is now the only half of it that is: it noted that a bound on height never bounded the thing an expression actually costs, which is how many audiences it reaches — a depth-3 limit allowed a wide flat sum of twenty AUDIENCE() leaves and priced each one at a fetch. With no bound at all now, neither shape is bounded, and the question "does expression size need a limit, of either shape, and who decides it?" is a real open question where "is 3 right?" was a fake one. It is registered in F-5's neighbourhood rather than answered here, on the same grounds this page always gave: inventing a parameter nobody asked for is not this page's call. The placeholder value of 3 is gone with the column, and the standing report of §8 and §11 half 2 is what a real number would eventually be argued from.
    • Whether an expression's fingerprint should be computed on save (§8). D-101 registered it rather than promising it: two expressions with the same meaning are two different row sets, so "you already granted exactly this set on that item" no longer falls out of storage. A warning, never a refusal — normalization informing a human, not gating one — and if the expansion overflows, skip the warning.
    • The per-item reach preview (§8) is a screen nobody has designed. PLACED means different people on different events, so the editor's expanded set must be computed for an item and say so on screen (D-102). D-091 made the expanded set a requirement; D-102 made it depend on an input the grant does not contain. Which item the editor picks by default — the grant's item, an event the writer chooses, the next event with registrants — is not decided anywhere.
    • WHITELISTED (§8): no stored state to name, so no selector. Either it means source = staff, or it means promotion off the waitlist, or it is a new column on event_registration — which is an events decision and not a selector decision. It is listed here so that it stays a question rather than becoming a word in a grammar.
    • PERSON(x) — closed by this round, and the way it closed is the open item. Two decision entries wrote PERSON() and the seeded registry in §8 did not contain it, so this page concluded the operand did not exist and read both examples as LIST. That was the wrong reading (it cannot express D-103's example), the registry was the side that was wrong, and the row is seeded now: argument_type = person, and the table that carries it holds fourteen rows today — thirteen while LIST existed, twelve for exactly one round after (D-105) deleted it, and fourteen since round 12 seeded OLDER and YOUNGER. The twelve is quoted here only because this bullet is where the count was recorded when it was true; a reader who takes it forward repeats, one round late, exactly the mistake this bullet exists to record. D-107 is the entry that moved it, and it moved the count by growing the table rather than by trading a row. The point of recording the number twice stands: it is cheap to check and it has been wrong before. The operand that was added to fix this drift turned out to be the one the other half of the drift was arguing for (D-101's amendment, D-104). What is still open is the class, not this operand: nothing in this module, or in this repository, compares the dotted tokens these documents write against the seeded registries they describe, so the same drift will happen again in whichever table is written next. D-104 adopts a hand-run report over the documents as the answer, and states plainly that a report nobody runs is a convention — there is no CI here to make it a guard, and no layer of this spec can tell a writer that PERSON( had no row behind it.
    • ~~Whether the LIST gloss is enough, or the operand should be renamed ADDED (§8).~~ Moot, not answered, and deleted rather than closed (D-105). D-103 recorded the name as the weak part of the design and refused the rename on cost grounds, and this bullet added that the cost rose every round it was deferred. There is no operand left to rename, so the question cannot be answered in either direction; the argument simply lost its subject. It is deleted rather than marked closed because "closed" would imply somebody weighed it.
    • Whether two people editing one audience is handled by a lock, a queue, or nothing (§8). Adding a name used to be an insert into audience_member, where two clerks adding two people could not collide. It is now an edit of one expression, so they can, and a silent lost update removes somebody's access without any row recording that it happened. D-105 states the obligation — an optimistic-concurrency check or a queue — and puts it on the editor rather than on the schema, because the table whose absence caused it no longer exists to carry the note. Nothing in this document decides which of the two it is, and nothing detects the failure after the fact: the audit trail records the write that happened, not the one that was overwritten.
    • Whether a person merge repoints every leaf that named the absorbed person, and who tests it (§8, §11 check 2). The requirement is D-105's and is not open; what is open is that no layer can express it. selector_arg_id has no foreign key (D-011), so the merge reassignment table has to be told about audience_operand the way it was told about every other person-referencing table, in all five argument kinds that name an entity, and the orphan scan that would otherwise catch it is both late and blind — late because it runs nightly, blind because a merge that leaves the absorbed row in place produces no orphan at all. It belongs to F-10's register for exactly that reason, and it is the first item on that register where the failure direction is a grant that denies everybody: EVERYONE - PERSON(x) with an un-repointed x.
    • Whether the reference listing is actually cheap (§8, §10). D-103 registers the assumption: "which live grants name this audience, and at what depth" is one indexed query over the leaf rows, cheap at this organization's size, unverified at any size. It is on the save path of a screen a person is standing at, and it is also what the wholesale cache invalidation is standing in for. If the import corpus's row-count estimates are wrong, the two decisions that follow — compute the closure on save, or keep invalidating everything — are one decision, not two.
    • Whether audience.code should be required rather than optional on is_system rows (§8, §12). The seeded audiences are the resolver's vocabulary and cannot be composed with by anything that lacks a code, which makes the code mandatory in every practical sense while leaving it nullable in the schema. A check is being asked to assert what a column could have guaranteed; making the column required-where-is_system would be the honest fix and is not this document's call to make.
    • Whether a nested audience's past reach should be reconstructable (§8, Three named sets). GROUP(x) answers "was she in it on 1 June 2024?" from its dated rows and AUDIENCE(x) cannot, because it is a rule and the trail that recorded its shape is reduced after 24 months (D-088). D-103 says the gap should be closed by a report rather than by pretending the question has an answer. The report is the same listing as the bullet above, kept over time — which makes this a question about whether to store history, i.e. F-7, and it is filed there rather than answered here.
    • Whether membership belongs in grant_scope_type (§6). It is not seeded, and that is an inference from what D-097 and D-099 list rather than a ruling; the old seed had the row.
    • Whether valid_to should be mandatory for high-authority holder rows and long-lived item grants (§4, §6).
    • Polymorphic scope_id vs a grantable_item supertable (§6) — a real fork, now covering selector_arg_id too.
    • How long a permission_cache entry may live (§10) — F-6, whose shape round 10 changed. It used to be a question about the membership sweep's TTL. It is now two caches with different keys — an answer cache and a per-operand cache, the second keyed on the item whenever the operand binds — and there are now two invalidations that cannot be keyed on the rows that were written, not one: role_holder (one holder row, every grant naming that office) and a write to any audience's tree, which since D-103 must also clear every audience that can reach the one written. The blunt answer — invalidate audience-keyed entries wholesale — is the recommendation, and the open question is whether it stays affordable (see the reference-listing bullet above).
    • Cache vs materialized effective-permission table (§8, §10) — F-7, and D-091 is the round that made it a history question rather than a performance one.
    • Retention policy for access_log — legal input required (F-8).
    • Whether access_log needs human annotation (recommend a separate table if so).

    Resolved and no longer open, kept visible so the reasoning survives: whether a role may be held by a rule — F-12, closed by D-100, and the answer is no; whether an audience may compose other sets (yes, three times over, and the third answer is the settled one: D-091 through an inclusion graph, D-101 through an expression naming only rosters, and D-103 through AUDIENCE() — the edge table stays deleted and the edges are leaves); whether a saved rule may be reused by name (yes, and only if it has a code, which D-103 settled after D-101 answered it in effect by accident); whether permissions depend on membership status (the gate is deleted and membership is an operand, with the posture opt-in per grant); the bulk exposure tool (removed — D-045), the exposure model itself (deleted — D-097), the level ladder (deleted — D-099), positions and their appointment workflows (deleted — D-100), and background-check permissions (removed — D-043).
