    Data Model — Identity¶

    person, account, contact points, identifiers, identity hypotheses, and merges.

    Every pattern referenced here is defined in 00-conventions.md.

    1. The central claim¶

    person   ── what the organization knows about a human. No status. No role. No type.
account  ── how that human logs in. Optional. Zero or more over a lifetime.

    There is no is_member, member_type, is_child, is_adult, unit_id, or role on person. Each of those is either a period in another table or a derived fact. This is the single most important property of the schema and the one most likely to be undone by a well-meaning future change.

    2. person¶

    Fields

    Field Kind Notes
    id surrogate id Internal only
    public_id external identifier, required Unique. Generated when the row is created. This is what URLs and exports carry
    given_name text, required Names are also stored as temporal rows in person_name (§3). These two columns are the current, canonical form, denormalised for display and search; history and legal-name variants live in person_name
    family_name text, required As above
    birth_date date, optional Nullable because legacy records frequently lack it. Never required — a 1994 roster with no birth date must still import
    birth_date_precision text, optional One of: exact, month, year, unknown. Because an imported birth date of 1990-01-01 often means "year 1990, month and day unknown". Storing the precision prevents false matches on a guessed day and prevents "happy 1 January birthday" emails
    gender text, optional One of: male, female, other, unknown. Included because youth organizations typically must report it. Remove if yours does not need it — do not collect what you cannot justify
    preferred_name text, optional What the person is actually called. Distinct from given_name so that the organization's records stay legally correct while the person is addressed correctly — "Alexandra" recorded, "Lex" displayed
    record_origin text, required One of: self_registered, staff_created, legacy_import. Default self_registered
    data_confidence text, required One of: high, medium, low. Default high. low for imported rows: it drives whether a merge may absorb this row, and whether the UI warns before displaying it as fact
    merged_into_person_id reference to person, optional Tombstone. When set, this row is absorbed and queries must follow the pointer. Never deleted (§8)
    merged_at timestamp, audit time, optional
    redacted_at timestamp, audit time, optional Set when a GDPR-style erasure was performed. Names and contacts are overwritten and the person's person_attribute rows are deleted; the row and its id survive so historical references still resolve (00-conventions §8)
    notes text, optional Free text about a person, and the only such text that lives outside the attribute vocabulary — see What is deliberately not on this table below. May contain anything, including a diagnosis sitting under a column nobody would call medical. It is logged with its contents, like every other field on this row, and it is deliberately not on the redactor's list — a column named as an exception is the field-name rule D-083 removed, and the protection is the guard on the trail (00-conventions §8 item 7). Whether the column should exist at all is argued below
    created_at timestamp, audit time
    created_by reference to person, optional
    updated_at timestamp, audit time
    updated_by reference to person, optional
    import_batch_id reference to import batch, optional Set on imported rows. Its link to import_batch can only be made once that table exists

    Rules

    • There is deliberately no deceased flag. The organization does not record whether a person has died. A membership simply ends; the reason for the ending lives in the generic reason-code fields (membership.termination_reason_code, membership.ended_note) like any other ending, and the organization's own vocabulary for such reasons is theirs to define. Do not add is_deceased or deceased_at back: it is not data the organization keeps.
    • public_id is unique.
    • A person may not be merged into themselves — merged_into_person_id may never name this same row, nor any row that resolves to it. The one-hop case is the degenerate form of the cycle banned in §8, not a separate rule beside it.
    • merged_into_person_id and merged_at are set together or both left null. A pointer with no date, or a date with no pointer, cannot be explained to anyone.
    • A birth_date_precision may be recorded without a birth_date only when it says unknown. Every other precision claims something about a date that is not there.

    Indexes

    • Person search by name, with both names compared case-insensitively.
    • Persons by birth_date, restricted to rows that have one.
    • Persons that have not been merged — a partial index, because every ordinary listing must exclude merged rows and it has to stay cheap.
    • Persons that have not been redacted — same reason.
    • Lookup by public_id.

    Notes on specific choices¶

    birth_date_precision. Without it, a legacy import that only knows the year produces 1990-01-01, which then matches every other year-only record born in 1990 on the same invented day, and sends birthday greetings on 1 January. Cheap column, prevents a whole class of matching noise.

    public_id alongside id. External references use public_id. Sequential internal ids appear in access_grant.scope_id and in URLs; exposing them would let anyone enumerate member records. See 00-conventions.md.

    merged_into_person_id and redacted_at are both on person rather than in side tables. They are read on every query (every listing must exclude merged and redacted rows from ordinary views), so they need to be cheap. They are also the reason the two partial indexes above exist.

    What is deliberately not on this table¶

    Everything the system knows about a person other than their name, their gender and their age is a descriptive value in person_attribute, and this table carries no column for any of it — no allergy, no medication, no insurance number, no shoe size, no preferred contact language, no diagnosis (09-attributes.md, D-083). That is not a gap waiting for the next module. An earlier design did put the medical facts on a shelf of their own, behind codes of their own, and the answer that produced D-083 refused the shelf: a drug dosage and a clothing size are the same kind of object — recorded by the organization, wrong in three years, unenumerable in advance — and a permission that reads a person reads the whole record whatever the shelf is called (domain/08-person-data.md).

    given_name/family_name/preferred_name, gender and birth_date are the only descriptive facts that are columns here, and they are the last the model will accept. The test for keeping one is not sensitivity — notes sits in the field table above and is a worse exposure than most of what moved out. The test is whether a rule exists that an open layer physically cannot hold. "One current name per person", "exactly one primary postal contact among the open rows", "a school id may repeat, a birth number may not" — each is a constraint over a known set of columns, and a key/value layer has no known set of columns, which is the whole of what it is for. Folding person_name, person_contact or person_identifier into the attribute layer would have relocated four working constraints into application code and been called uniformity (F-10 shape 3). Identity is closed because it was enumerated years ago and has not changed; description is open because nobody can enumerate it (D-085).

    The rule cuts both ways, and review is where it is applied. A fact with no such rule behind it does not earn a column here, however reasonable it looks — that is the sentence that keeps is_deceased's cousins out. And a table already in this document does not lose its columns because the new layer looks tidier — that is the sentence that keeps a well-meaning unification of "everything about a person" from quietly turning four enforced rules into four hopes.

    One consequence names itself. person.notes is the last free text about a person kept outside the vocabulary, and it is the column a reviewer should ask about: a sentence typed there is invisible to the curation queue, to any report on a code, and to the screen a first aider reads at 23:00, while the same sentence recorded as a value against a proposed code is visible to all three (09-attributes.md §2). Whether the column should exist at all is open and already argued (06-audit-and-import.md §6); what is settled is that it carries no protection the attribute layer does not carry, and less readability.

    The answer to "who is a member?" is never on this table¶

    To be unambiguous, the two facts:

    • Currently a member — the person has a membership whose valid_to is absent and whose status is active.
    • Was a member on 1998-06-01 — the person has a membership whose period contains that date: valid_from on or before it, and valid_to either absent or after it. Its status is one of active, completed, lapsed, terminated.

    Note the second fact includes closed statuses — "was a member in 1998" is true regardless of how the membership later ended. Getting this wrong is why "years of membership" reports are usually wrong.

    3. person_name — name history¶

    Fields

    Field Kind Notes
    id surrogate id
    person_id reference to person, required
    name_type text, required One of: legal, preferred, maiden, former, alias, imported
    given_name text, required
    family_name text, required
    middle_name text, optional
    valid_from date, optional Optional because an imported name often arrives with no start date at all
    valid_to date, optional NULL = current for this name_type
    source text, optional self, staff, legacy_import
    import_batch_id reference to import batch, optional

    Rules

    • valid_to is either absent, or valid_from is absent, or valid_to is later than valid_from.

    Indexes

    • A person's names.
    • Fuzzy search across all historical names — both names lowercased, family name first, and usable for prefix matching. This is what legacy matching reads.

    Why name history is a table. Three concrete cases:

    1. A member married and changed their family name. The 1998 roster has the maiden name; the 2026 registration has the married name. Legacy matching must find the link, and the historical record must keep the name it actually had.
    2. An imported name has a typo (Novak for Novák). Correcting it must not destroy the imported form, because that is what the paper says and someone will ask.
    3. A person goes by a different name than their legal one. Both must be storable, and which is used where must be deterministic.

    person.given_name/family_name are the denormalised current values and are what the UI displays. person_name is the source of truth for matching and for historical queries. This is a deliberate denormalization — the alternative is joining on every list view — and it must be updated in the same transaction whenever the canonical name changes.

    Assumption: display should use person.preferred_name ?? person.given_name. If the organization requires legal names on official documents, that rule lives in the document layer, not here.

    4. account¶

    Fields

    Field Kind Notes
    id surrogate id
    person_id reference to person, required Not unique. Uniqueness applies only to live accounts, under Rules
    username text, optional
    status text, required One of: pending_verification, active, suspended, closed. Default pending_verification
    verified_at timestamp, audit time, optional
    guardian_consent_at timestamp, audit time, optional Set when a guardian confirmed a minor's account. NULL for adults (domain/02-actors.md §2)
    created_by_staff boolean, required Default false
    last_login_at timestamp, audit time, optional
    failed_login_count integer, required Default 0
    locked_until timestamp, audit time, optional
    status_reason_code text, optional Why suspended or closed
    status_note text, optional The human explanation
    created_at timestamp, audit time
    created_by reference to person, optional
    updated_at timestamp, audit time
    updated_by reference to person, optional

    Rules

    • At most one live account per person: unique over person_id across the rows whose status is pending_verification, active or suspended. Closed rows are not covered, so a person may accumulate as many closed accounts as their history contains.
    • username is unique compared case-insensitively, over the rows that have one and are not closed. A closed account releases its username for someone else to take. It covers live rows only, not all rows.
    • An account may not reach active while verified_at is absent.
    • An account may not reach active while an unresolved identity_hypothesis names its person — a hypothesis sitting in proposed or needs_review, on either side of the pair. This is the second gate on the same transition and D-089 is why it is here. It is written as a gate on a status rather than as a rule against creating the row, because the row has to exist for the claim to be attached to anything, and because the queue a claim lands in already exists (§8) with its signals, its ordering and its reviewer. See the collision flow below for what that buys and what it costs.

    Indexes

    • A person's accounts.

    Why person_id is not unique. The partial unique index expresses the real rule: one live account per person, while permitting closed historical accounts. A plain UNIQUE (person_id) would make it impossible to close an old account and open a new one — which is exactly the returning-member case at the account level.

    Why guardian_consent_at is on account, not person. Consent is about the act of creating login credentials, not about the human. A person who was registered as a child and later reaches majority keeps the same person row; the consent record stays attached to the account it was given for.

    A colliding sign-up files a claim and brings nothing into the world that a human has not approved. This is the case the second gate above exists for (D-089): someone arrives at the sign-up form through a door the system did not know they had, and their details strong-match a person who already exists. The tempting answer was the convenient one — verify the e-mail, see that the signals are strong, link the two accounts — and it is D-004 wearing a different coat: a false-positive link is one human's descriptive data becoming another human's record, and a merge is the hard part to undo even with §9's reversal. The other tempting answer was the permissive one — let the second live account stand — which drops the uniqueness rule above and splits one person's consents, payments and attributes across two ids until somebody happens to notice, in exactly the records where nobody notices. So the system refuses, files the claim, and waits.

    What actually happens, in the order it happens. The sign-up's rows are created as any sign-up creates them and stay pending_verification; an identity_hypothesis is raised naming the two person rows, with proposed_by of person because that is what happened — a person asserted this is them — and its match_signals carrying which of them matched; the gate holds the account where it is; the person at the form sees a holding message. Nothing is merged, nothing is linked, nothing is activated. The address they typed is not thrown away: it stays on the pending account as the credential it was offered as, the identifier awaiting verification, rather than being echoed back to a browser and lost — which matters because the reviewer's second possible answer is "this is a different person", and that answer should leave the applicant finishing the sign-up they started rather than starting it again.

    The trigger is a strong match, and the threshold is the whole rule. A hypothesis is raised at sign-up on an exact legacy member number, or an exact birth date together with a normalized name match — the top of the band ../domain/03-identity-and-legacy.md §4 already weights, and nothing below it. The soft band must not raise one. Blocking a stranger's registration on a fuzzy score is the failure this rule would most deserve to have caused, and the two sides of that trade are not symmetric: the cost of letting a true duplicate through is bounded, reviewable, and caught by the nightly matcher anyway, while the cost of stopping a new member is a child who cannot sign up to a camp on the day the form closes.

    The holding message confirms nothing, and this is a security requirement rather than a tone choice. It says the request was received and will be checked. It says nothing about whether the name, the birth date or the member number matched anything at all, because a sign-up form that answers "yes, that person exists" is an existence oracle over a roster, and this system's roster is children (05-authorization.md is the document that spends its length on why that distinction is worth a resolver). The same form that legitimately helps a returning member find their account is the form that lets anyone with a birth date enumerate who is enrolled. So the message is identical whether the match was exact, partial, or absent, and it stays identical whether or not an account exists to be found.

    Resolving the claim is not a new permission and not a new queue. A reviewer confirms or rejects the hypothesis, which is person.merge and already sits with identity_reviewer; confirming it merges the two person rows under §9's rules, rejecting it lifts the gate and the account activates. No new code is involved on either side, for the same reason §9 gives for reversal needing none: undoing and correcting an identity are one act by one named human.

    Confirming the claim closes the placeholder account; it does not move it. This is the part of the flow that looks obvious and is wrong, so the wrong version is written out first: the sign-up's person row is absorbed, the sign-up's account still points at it, so repoint account.person_id at the survivor and the applicant keeps the login they were building. That write is refused by §4's own rule — one live account per person, unique over the pending, active and suspended rows — for the common case where the survivor already has an account; and in the case where it is not refused it quietly makes a login older than the person's own, carrying a consent timestamp, a verification and a credential history that belong to an act the survivor never performed. The rule is instead, in this order:

    1. Close the placeholder account — status becomes closed, with the reason naming the hypothesis that closed it. Closed rows are outside the uniqueness rule (§4), which is exactly why closing works where repointing does not, and the row survives as the record that someone applied.
    2. Move the credential, not the account. The typed address was verified as far as it ever was, and throwing it away punishes the one person in this flow who did the right thing. It is added to the survivor's live account as an account_credential row (§5) — credentials are many-per-account already, which is why this is an addition rather than an exception. Where that account has no live account to receive it, the survivor is given one, carrying the moved credential and the placeholder's verified_at: the address was verified, and making somebody click a second link to prove a claim a reviewer has already confirmed is bureaucracy with a security costume.
    3. A credential of the same type already live on the survivor's account is not overwritten. Two password rows do not both become current; the arriving one is left unverified and a field_conflict (§10's queue) is raised for the person to sort out, because this is the one branch where the system cannot tell whether it is looking at the same address typed twice or at two different claims to one mailbox.
    4. One audit entry names both accounts, both persons and the hypothesis, and it is written by the same transaction that closes the row. Not two entries, and not the merge's own entry doing double duty: the merge says two people became one, and this says a login changed hands, which is the question whoever reverses the merge (§9) will actually be asking.

    What this costs is the applicant's session, and it is worth naming. Someone mid-sign-up whose claim is confirmed does not continue in the account they were filling in; they log in to the one they already had. That is one extra login against the alternative of an account that belongs to two people's history at once, and the holding message (§4 above) is where they are told it. Two costs come with it and both are stated rather than absorbed. A genuinely new person who happens to match now waits for a human, so the blocking condition carries an age check shaped like field_conflict's thirty days below, and it alerts: a stalled identity queue stopped being a data-quality problem the moment it could hold a login, and a person who cannot sign up has no way to say so except by telephoning someone. And the reviewer deciding it is deliberately half-blind — the seeded role holds the merge and no read over the person record, so it decides from signals and names, which is the correct conservative shape and also the thing to revisit if reviewers start asking for context they cannot see.

    The everyday case is not this case, and naming it stops the rule being oversold. "I have a new e-mail address" has never been a second account and is not one now: account_credential (§5) holds many credentials for one account, so a new address is an addition to the login a person already has and runs past nothing here. The collision case is narrower than signing up twice — it is a person arriving through a door the system did not know they had.

    A suspended account does not suspend a membership. They are independent lifecycles. The schema enforces this by simply not linking them.

    5. account_credential¶

    Fields

    Field Kind Notes
    id surrogate id
    account_id reference to account, required
    credential_type text, required One of: password, passkey, totp, oauth, staff_link. Multiple methods per account. oauth supports a national Scout identity provider; staff_link supports a one-time link issued to a member who cannot self-serve
    secret_hash text, optional Password/OTP hash. Absent for passkey and oauth
    external_ref text, optional OAuth subject id, or WebAuthn credential id
    external_issuer text, optional OAuth issuer
    valid_from timestamp, audit time, required Default: now
    valid_to timestamp, audit time, optional NULL = current
    last_used_at timestamp, audit time, optional
    revoked_reason text, optional
    created_at timestamp, audit time

    Rules

    • A password, totp or staff_link credential must carry a secret_hash. A passkey or oauth credential must carry both external_ref and external_issuer. A credential with nothing to check against cannot exist.
    • valid_to is either absent or later than valid_from.
    • One current credential per (external_issuer, external_ref) pair among oauth rows that are still current. It covers open rows only — an expired credential does not hold its external identity back.

    Indexes

    • An account's current credentials — the open half of the period only.
    • The oauth uniqueness above, which is itself a lookup: given an issuer and a subject id, which account is this.

    Why credentials are not columns on account. Adding passkeys, an organizational SSO, or a one-time-link flow later must not require restructuring account and migrating every existing password. Keeping secret_hash off the account table also means the common "show me the user" query never touches credential material.

    valid_to on a credential is how password history and revocation are expressed. A revoked credential stays as a row, so "was this account ever accessed with a credential that has since been revoked?" is answerable.

    6. person_contact¶

    Fields

    Field Kind Notes
    id surrogate id
    person_id reference to person, required
    contact_type text, required One of: email, phone, postal, website, messaging
    purpose text, required One of: login, membership, emergency, billing, guardian, general
    value text, required Normalised per type: emails lowercased, phones E.164, postal as structured JSON
    value_detail free-form jsonb, optional For postal addresses: {street, city, postal_code, country}. Avoids six optional fields that only apply to one contact_type
    label text, optional home, work, school — display only
    is_primary boolean, required Default false
    verified_at timestamp, audit time, optional
    verified_by text, optional One of: self, staff, bounce_check, import
    valid_from date, required Default: today
    valid_to date, optional NULL = current. Addresses change; history is required
    source text, optional self, staff, legacy_import
    import_batch_id reference to import batch, optional
    created_at timestamp, audit time
    created_by reference to person, optional

    Rules

    • valid_to is either absent or later than valid_from.
    • At most one current primary per (person, contact_type, purpose). It covers open rows only: a superseded primary does not block the new one.

    Indexes

    • A person's contact points.
    • A person's current contact points by type and purpose — open rows only.
    • Email lookup on value lowercased, over current email rows only.
    • Phone lookup on value as stored, over current phone rows only.

    purpose is the column that makes this operationally useful. It lets the permission system distinguish "a unit leader may see emergency contacts for camp" from "only billing staff may see payment email addresses". Without purpose, every contact is equally sensitive and the only safe choice is to hide all of them.

    The partial unique index on the primary contact enforces "one primary email per person per purpose" without preventing a person from having four non-primary addresses. Note this index is what makes the merge conflict in §8 possible — resolving two is_primary rows is a review step, not a rule.

    The email index is functional — it indexes value lowercased because email matching in the legacy importer must be case-insensitive, and a plain index cannot serve that.

    7. person_identifier¶

    Fields

    Field Kind Notes
    id surrogate id
    person_id reference to person, required
    scheme text, required national_scout_id, legacy_member_no, legacy_member_no_oldsystem, government_id, school_id, insurance_no. Validated against identifier_scheme below
    value text, required
    issued_at date, optional
    valid_to date, optional IDs expire or are superseded
    verified_at timestamp, audit time, optional
    source text, optional
    import_batch_id reference to import batch, optional
    created_at timestamp, audit time

    identifier_scheme — the vocabulary of schemes.

    Fields

    Field Kind Notes
    code text, required The value person_identifier.scheme holds
    name text, required
    is_unique boolean, required Default true. True: a value may appear on only one person (national id, member number). False: values legitimately repeat (school id across schools)
    description text, optional

    Rules

    • Uniqueness is per scheme, and identifier_scheme.is_unique decides it. For a scheme flagged unique, (scheme, value) is unique over the identifiers that are still current — those with no valid_to. A superseded number may be reused; a current one may not be shared. The rule covers open rows only.
    • For a scheme flagged not-unique there is no such rule, and that is the point of the flag. school_id repeats by design: the same school issues the same number to many children, and each of them is a different person who genuinely carries it. is_unique = false means one value may appear on any number of people, and means it at enforcement time, not as a note in a description column.
    • Corrected here, and worth reading as a lesson. The rule as first written applied to every scheme and never read the flag; the flag and the rule were the same idea stated twice, once enforced and once decorative. The effect was that school_id could not in fact repeat — the second child at that school with the same number would have been rejected as a duplicate identifier, at import time, by a constraint whose own table said it should not exist. A column that no rule reads is not documentation, it is a promise. This is the same failure D-072 warns about from the other side: a rule nobody runs is a convention, and a flag nothing consults is decoration. Both read correctly on the page.

    Indexes

    • A person's identifiers.
    • The uniqueness lookup above.

    This table is the highest-value asset in the legacy import. If the old system issued member numbers, an exact (scheme='legacy_member_no', value=…) match is the strongest identity signal available — strong enough to short-circuit a fuzzy name comparison. See ../domain/03-identity-and-legacy.md.

    Why not put these on person as columns. A person may have zero identifiers (most self-registered children), one, or several; schemes appear and disappear over decades; and national_scout_id must be unique while school_id must not. Columns would mean a schema change per scheme and a nullable-unique mess.

    8. identity_hypothesis and person_merge¶

    Fields

    Field Kind Notes
    id surrogate id
    person_a_id reference to person, required The lower of the two ids — canonical ordering, so that (a,b) and (b,a) cannot both exist
    person_b_id reference to person, required
    confidence number between 0 and 1, required Inclusive of both ends
    match_signals jsonb, required A list of signals, each with a name, a weight and whether it matched — [{signal: 'birth_date_exact', weight: 0.4, matched: true}, {signal: 'name_normalised', weight: 0.3, matched: true}, …]. Stored so a human reviewer can see WHY the matcher proposed this
    status text, required One of: proposed, needs_review, confirmed, rejected, superseded. Default proposed
    proposed_by text, required One of: import_job, match_job, person, staff. match_job is the nightly batch matcher, as opposed to a person suggesting a match by hand. (Named background_match before background checks were removed from the design; renamed so the two never get confused.)
    proposed_by_person_id reference to person, optional Set when a person proposed it
    proposed_at timestamp, audit time Default: now
    reviewed_by reference to person, optional
    reviewed_at timestamp, audit time, optional
    review_note text, optional
    created_at timestamp, audit time

    Rules

    • person_a_id names a lower person id than person_b_id, and the two are never the same person.
    • A hypothesis cannot be confirmed or rejected without a reviewed_at.
    • One live hypothesis per pair: unique over (person_a_id, person_b_id) while the status is proposed, needs_review or confirmed. A rejected or superseded hypothesis does not block a new one, so the pair can be re-proposed later.

    Indexes

    • The review queue: status, then confidence highest first, restricted to the rows still waiting — proposed and needs_review.

    person_merge — the record that one person row was absorbed into another.

    Fields

    Field Kind Notes
    id surrogate id
    surviving_person_id reference to person, required
    merged_person_id reference to person, required The absorbed row. It keeps its id; person.merged_into_person_id points here
    hypothesis_id reference to identity hypothesis, optional The hypothesis this merge came from, when one did
    merged_at timestamp, audit time Default: now
    merged_by reference to person, required
    reason text, optional
    reversed_at timestamp, audit time, optional
    reversed_by reference to person, optional
    reversal_note text, optional
    created_at timestamp, audit time

    Rules

    • A person may not be merged into themselves: surviving_person_id and merged_person_id are never the same person.
    • A merge is refused when the chosen survivor resolves — by walking the chain — to the person being absorbed. That includes the one-hop case just stated and the case that actually happens: absorbing B into A when A has already been absorbed into B. D-087 puts this check on the merge and not on the read, and the placement is the substance of the rule: a cycle refused on the way in cannot be reached, whereas a step limit on resolve_person() achieves nothing except deciding which of two wrong answers to return — the tombstone, or wherever the walk happened to stop. There is no schema object that spans two rows of a walk, so this is a rule about an operation; the cycle check in §10 is a canary for it having leaked, not the guard.

    Indexes

    • Merges by surviving person, and merges by merged person — both directions get asked.
    • Merges by merged person that have not been reversed — the "who is this person now" query.

    The canonical ordering (person_a_id before person_b_id) is not cosmetic. Without it the matcher can create both (100, 847) and (847, 100), and a reviewer will confirm one while the other sits in the queue forever — or worse, confirm both. The ordering rule plus the partial unique index makes the pair identity unambiguous.

    match_signals as JSONB rather than columns, because the signal set will evolve as the matcher improves, and because these are write-once-read-for-review, never queried analytically.

    person_merge is append-only — reversed_at is set, rows are never deleted. An unmerge does not remove the merge record; it records that it was undone, so the history of the mistake is visible. See ../domain/03-identity-and-legacy.md.

    Reversing a merge is a required operation, not an accommodation. Those three columns exist because someone asked for the capability, and D-087 is where it was asked for; §9 explains that this is the reason chains were kept rather than the other way round. Two consequences sit under it, and both are easy to lose later.

    • Reversal needs no permission of its own. Unmerging is the same act as merging — an identity correction made by a named human — so it is person.merge, which identity_reviewer already holds. A separate code would grade undoing a mistake as safer than making one, which is precisely backwards: the mistake is the thing that needed the guard.
    • A merge can be undone; the merged period cannot. The reversal procedure lifts the tombstone and returns the rows created before the merge, but the rows created inside the window are flagged for a human rather than guessed at — and every access_grant from that window is flagged, not redistributed, because the authority exercised in there really was exercised by somebody the record can no longer separate. That asymmetry is the honest core of the feature: the row is restorable, the interval is not. Anyone who reads reversed_at as "as if it never happened" is reading the wrong column for that claim, which is why the reversal record is kept rather than deleted.

    Merge conflicts¶

    field_conflict — one field the merge could not decide.

    Fields

    Field Kind Notes
    id surrogate id
    merge_id reference to person merge, required
    entity_table text, required person, person_contact, membership, …
    entity_id surrogate id, required The row in entity_table. No reference — with the table named per row it would point at any table in the schema
    field_name text, required
    surviving_value text, optional
    merged_value text, optional
    resolution text, optional One of: kept_surviving, took_merged, manual, unresolved
    resolved_value text, optional
    resolved_by reference to person, optional
    resolved_at timestamp, audit time, optional
    created_at timestamp, audit time

    Indexes

    • A merge's conflicts by resolution.
    • A merge's conflicts that are still unresolved — the reportable set.

    Every field the merge could not resolve automatically lands here as unresolved. A merge with unresolved conflicts must be visible in an operational report — otherwise these rows accumulate silently and the data is quietly wrong.

    Conflict cases worth naming, because each needs a human decision and none has a safe rule:

    Case Why it cannot be automatic
    Two is_primary emails of the same purpose Which one does the person actually use?
    Two different birth_date values One is a typo; guessing produces a wrong legal record.
    Two overlapping memberships May be legitimate, or may mean the merge is wrong.
    Two different current names One may be a maiden name, or they may be different people.
    Two accounts, both active Which one is in use?

    9. Views¶

    Convenience views that encode the "always exclude merged and redacted" and "point in time" rules, so that application code cannot forget them.

    v_person — the person rows most queries actually want: a person who is neither merged away nor redacted. Both exclusions, not one. They are different reasons and they must not be collapsed: a merged row is a duplicate that points at the real answer, while a redacted row is a person who was erased, whose names and contacts no longer exist. Neither belongs in a list of people, and a listing that shows one and hides the other is worse than either, because it teaches the reader that the redacted child is a data-entry error.

    This is a correction, not a restatement. The view as this document first carried it excluded merged rows only, while §1 and §2 both said every listing must exclude redacted ones too, and §9's own opening sentence claimed the views encoded both. Three statements, one of them the code. The code lost. It is fixed here rather than noted as a discrepancy, because with the SQL gone (§1 of D-072) there is nothing left to disagree with the prose — the prose is now the only place the rule lives, so it has to be right.

    What is deliberately not excluded anywhere: the row itself. A redacted person is still a row with a still-working id, so historical references keep resolving (§2's redacted_at). Anything that must reach a merged or redacted row — audit, as-of reconstruction, resolve_person — queries person directly and says so. v_person is the view for "people", and people is not what an audit trail is asking for.

    resolve_person(id) — turns a possibly-merged id into the survivor. These steps run in this order, and the order is the rule:

    1. Begin at the person row carrying the given id.
    2. If that row has no merged_into_person_id, stop; this id is the answer.
    3. Otherwise move to the row its merged_into_person_id names and repeat from step 2.

    v_person_display — one row per person v_person admits, carrying id, public_id, a display given name (preferred_name where present, given_name otherwise), family_name, a display name formed by putting the two together with a space, birth_date, birth_date_precision and redacted_at.

    What v_person_display does not carry, and why that is a rule rather than an omission. It is a display row — the columns needed to name a person in a list — and it carries no descriptive values. It cannot: a view is a fixed list of columns and the attribute layer is the part of a person with no fixed list, which is §2's test arriving from the other side. Naming the boundary matters because the failure mode is silent. Anything asked about a person, rather than about what they are called, reads person and person_attribute together under the one person.view that reaches both (09-attributes.md §4) — the allergy list a first aider asks for, the record a guardian opens, the form a leader fills in. A screen built on v_person_display alone answers those questions with a name and a birth date, shows no empty field where an allergy should be, and looks entirely healthy while doing it (domain/08-person-data.md §3). The view stays narrow for the reason it exists — every picker and roster in the system reads it and none of them needs the record — and the obligation sits on the caller: do not answer a question about a person out of a view that only knows how to print one.

    resolve_person follows every hop because merges chain: A absorbed into B, later B absorbed into C. A single-level pointer lookup would return B, whose row is itself tombstoned. Every place that accepts a person id from outside must call this. Getting it wrong means a historical record links to a tombstone and the UI shows a blank name — or the wrong one.

    Chains are a decision, not an assumption, and this used to be an assumption. The sentence this replaces read "Assumption: merge chains are allowed rather than flattened at merge time", and it argued for chains on the ground that flattening makes unmerge nearly impossible — a defence resting on a capability nobody had asked for. Someone has since asked for it, as a requirement (§8), and D-087 is that request: with chains kept a reversal is a replay, with flattening it is a rebuild. So the direction of the argument is reversed and the trade is now asymmetric in a way it was not before — flattening is closed off, not merely slower, and "flattening is faster" no longer buys anything except the death of a feature somebody named.

    Two rules keep a permanent chain safe, and neither existed while this was an assumption.

    • A cycle cannot be created, and the place it cannot be created is the merge, not the read (§8's rule). A read-side hop limit is not a weaker version of this guard; it is a different and worse thing, because it always returns an answer.
    • Chain length is a signal, not an error. Resolution has no hop limit — a bound would silently hand back a tombstoned id, which is the single failure this function exists to prevent, and the argument for following every hop above is the argument for having no bound. Instead a chain longer than three hops is reported, because four merges in a row on one line of people does not mean the data is odd: it means the matcher is producing duplicates faster than a human can settle them, which is a statement about the queue and not about the row. Check 3 in §10, which used to treat an absorbed survivor as a breach, is corrected for exactly this: a survivor that has itself been absorbed is what a chain looks like when it works. Only the length means anything.

    And the rule immediately above now has a check under it. "Every place that accepts a person id from outside must call this" is a rule about application code, which application code can forget, and D-072 removed the database-level ways of catching the forgetting. The net is check 4 in §10 — live rows that reference a tombstoned person — which is the only form of this failure that is still visible after the fact, and the reason §9's prose is no longer the only thing standing between a foreign key and a tombstone.

    10. Integrity checks¶

    Checks that should run nightly and alert. These encode invariants that no per-column rule above can express.

    Invariants to verify

    • A person with more than one live account. Count account rows per person whose status is pending_verification, active or suspended; anything above one is a breach of the rule in §4, and the point of the check is to confirm that rule really does exclude the pending-plus-active combination.
    • A tombstoned person with no merge record. Count persons whose merge pointer is set but who do not appear as the merged person of any un-reversed person_merge row; non-zero means a pointer was written without the record that explains it, which is the case the append-only table in §8 exists to make impossible.
    • A merge chain longer than three hops. Walk each tombstone's pointer and count the hops to the survivor; report the chains over three. This is a report about a queue, not a finding about a row. It replaces a check that counted person_merge rows whose surviving_person_id has its own merge pointer set and called a non-zero result a breach — with D-087 deciding that chains stay, that count is the normal shape of the data and its expected value is not zero; a person absorbed twice has a survivor who was absorbed, by construction. What carries information is the depth: four merges in a row on one line of people means the matcher is producing duplicates faster than a human can settle them. It is read alongside the review queue's age, never on its own, and it is not alertable — there is nothing wrong to fix on the row, and an alert that can only be answered by "work faster" trains people to ignore alerts.
    • A live row that references a tombstoned person. Count rows in any table that names a person which point at a person whose merge pointer is set, where the referring row is itself live. This is the only net under §9's rule that every entry point calls resolve_person(), and it exists because D-072 took the database-level ones away. Getting that call wrong is invisible at the moment it happens — a blank name in a list, at worst the wrong name — and permanent afterwards, because the row is written and referenced by whatever came after it. This one alerts rather than logs: the gap is not reconstructable later, and unlike a missing audit entry it misattributes a person rather than merely forgetting them.
    • A cycle in the merge graph. Walk the pointers and look for a row reachable from itself. There should never be a single row here: §8 refuses a merge whose chosen survivor resolves to the person being absorbed, so a non-zero count is not untidy data, it is that rule having failed, and it is the one merge finding that should stop work rather than fill a report — resolve_person() does not terminate on a cycle, and every screen and every resolver hop that touches the affected people goes with it.
    • An account held below active by an unresolved hypothesis for more than 30 days. Count accounts whose status is not active or closed, whose person is named by a hypothesis still in proposed or needs_review, where that hypothesis is older than 30 days — the same shape as the conflict-age check below, for the same reason. It alerts rather than logs, and the reason is worth keeping in the check's own text: a stalled identity queue stopped being a data-quality problem the day it acquired the power to hold a login. Behind each row is a specific person who cannot sign up — often to a camp whose form closes this week — and no channel by which to tell anyone except a telephone call.
    • Unresolved merge conflicts older than 30 days. Count field_conflict rows resolved to unresolved and created more than 30 days ago; non-zero means the review queue is being ignored and the data is quietly wrong.
    • Redacted people still referenced by a live account. Count account rows that are not closed and belong to a person with redacted_at set; non-zero means an erasure left a working login behind.
    • Imported people with no membership period. Count people whose record_origin is legacy_import and who have no membership row at all. What makes this a check rather than a census is the ratio: a batch in which nobody has a period is a batch whose rows simply said nothing, and a batch whose stated-coverage figure disagrees with the periods written is a partially applied one. A row that created a membership its export did not state is a different finding, and lives in 06-audit-and-import.md §5. It has been reversed twice, and both reversals are on the record, because this row is really a claim about what an import is allowed to conclude. It began as exactly this check — imported people with no membership. ~~That was the ordinary condition of every imported row once a roster was held to make a participation and nothing else, so the check reported the entire legacy corpus as a failed batch, which is precisely how a check stops being read~~; D-090 therefore reversed it to no participation row. That reversal is now void twice over: the participation table is deleted (D-097), and the people export turned out to state membership periods, so the import writes bounded membership rows for precisely those people and nobody else (D-093). What the round-9 rewrite got right and is kept: a check whose trigger condition is normal for the whole corpus is not a check.
    • A redacted person with descriptive values still attached. Stated where the rows are, as check 6 of 09-attributes.md §8 — attribute rows belonging to a redacted person, and attribute values still present in audit_log. It is named here because the redaction checks above are the same erasure guarantee read from the identity side, and a half-finished erasure does not announce which table it stopped in. Of everything on either list, that is the one that should alert rather than log.

    11. Open issues¶

    • Erasure policy specifics: what must be redacted vs. retained. Smaller than it used to be, and not gone. The retention shape is decided — the trail's payloads expire on a window, the accounting skeleton has no clock at all (D-088) — so what is left is the question a decision log cannot settle, namely whether indefinite retention of named membership and fee rows has a basis. See 00-conventions.md and 06-audit-and-import.md §6.
    • Whether person.gender is needed at all; if so, what value set.
    • Whether the organization has (or will have) a national SSO identity provider — this determines how much of account_credential is actually used.

    Resolved, kept visible so that nobody reopens them by reading the tables alone. Whether merge chains are allowed or flattened at merge time (§9): chains stay, because reversing a merge is a required operation rather than a convenience, and the two rules a permanent chain needs are now written where they are obeyed — the cycle refusal in §8, the length report in §10 (D-087, B-2). And whether a person may hold two live accounts simultaneously: no, enforced by the partial unique index in §4 — with the case that made the question hard answered by a gate rather than by an exception, so that a colliding sign-up files a claim and activates nothing instead of becoming a second account or being silently linked (D-089, B-4).
