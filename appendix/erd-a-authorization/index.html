    Appendix — ERD: authorization¶

    The authorization model as one diagram, the tables behind it, and the expression tree an audience is stored as.

    Section numbers are shared across the three parts and never renumbered, because other pages cite them by number and their anchors are built from them: erd.md holds §1–§4, the diagrams of the people — identity and attributes, the unit's groups and offices, membership, guardianship; erd-a-authorization.md holds §5–§5b, the whole authorization answer, its tables and the audience expression tree; erd-b-inventory.md holds §6–§8, the temporal pattern, the table inventory and the foreign key summary.

   that — claimed (D-121).
   The allow steps are ALTERNATIVES producing one boolean: their order decides which channel
   the audit names, never what the answer is. There is **no locality rule**: locality could
   only ever order allows against each other, and a boolean has nothing to order (D-099).
   Step 0 is not an ordering either — it is a check that runs before every other.

    Key points:

    • One table grants. access_grant is the only place authority is written, plus the one table over a single person, guardianship_permission (§4, step 4 above). role_holder, person_group_member and every operand of an audience decide only whom a grant reaches — being in a set is worth exactly what the grant aimed at that set says (D-005, restated for roles by D-100). role_holder carries no scope columns, no level and no resolver channel; what it shares with access_grant is only the temporal shape every dated row has.
    • There is no wildcard permission. A check for X.y needs an exact grant of X.y, or system.admin (D-068). "Can do everything about events" is a role some grants name, each grant naming one of the thirteen event.* codes. Why admin is never ambiguous here: a grant carries no level, so the word appears in exactly one place — the code system.admin — D-099.
    • Deny is absolute. A live deny beats every live allow, with no tie-break, and the only thing that removes one is a person ending its period (D-013, D-099). That is why valid_to earns its keep on a deny row, and why a live deny matching no live allow is a reported shape rather than a harmless row — a deny that denies nothing is how a stale restriction outlives its reason. Why the ranking went: D-013 let a newer act outrank a denial, so a stale deny was one late revocation away from winning anyway. Deleting the ordering deleted the hatch.
    • The membership gate is opt-in, written in the expression. MEMBERS is an operand evaluated live on the checked date, so MEMBERS * ROLE(x) gates an office and ROLE(x) alone does not, and the "except this one person" case is written inside the same expression (D-100). The failure mode is a grant written ROLE(x) with no MEMBERS, authorizing a lapsed member silently and forever, failing closed-looking so nobody notices — the control is the Unusual section of the tab on the permissions page, which lists exactly that shape and refuses nothing, plus the writer's own habit that a grant template carries MEMBERS — no grant arrives already carrying it, and the only grants this system writes by itself, the twelve beside an event, must not (D-109, F-13). Ownership and guardianship are not gated and never were.
    • The guardianship channel is entered by shape, not by permission name (D-079): the check is about someone other than the actor, and the channel answers with allows only. A sufficient right_level returns true; no row, none, or too low a level returns nothing at all and the actor's own grants are tried. A prohibition is therefore a deny grant, never a low level. What the channel compares a permission against is now permission.right_kind — a column D-099 added and names as having exactly one customer, this channel.
    • There is no downward crossing, because there is no down. With one unit there is no outside it for authority to leak toward, so D-031 was not weakened, it was deprived of its subject (D-097). A grant at the unit therefore reaches every item of every type, and system.admin with it. What is lost and reproduced nowhere is the safeguarding property: access to a body's children had to be granted by that body — by the people who recruited its leader and can be asked. What remains is a permission held by a named adult, an audit trail whose payloads expire after two years, and a report of who holds the write, minus the part where the adult was somebody the body chose.
    • A grantee may be an audience, or a key. access_grant.person_id, audience_id and api_key_id carry exactly one value between them — never two, never none (D-116). An audience is an expression over named sets (D-101): every operand is evaluated live and nothing is stored as a roster, a hand-written set is written PERSON(a) + PERSON(b) like any other set (D-105), and two sets are composed with an operand rather than an edge between them. Author in sets, evaluate per person: a check compiles the expression to a predicate and never materializes a set, and an operand that does not resolve is the empty set (D-106). That is one of the two rules the stated scale names as surviving any growth — the other is that a listing is paginated — and it is why the system gets slow before it gets wrong (D-112). Why "fail closed" is the wrong shorthand here: a failed operand on + or * narrows the set and a failed operand on - widens it, so EVERYONE - PERSON(x) with a dead x is fail-closed and lockout-everybody in one step. The failure stays fail-closed and stops being silent: an audience carrying a leaf that resolves to nothing is listed on the tab on the permissions page (D-109), and no save is refused for it. One thing can still produce such a leaf: a redacted person. Nothing else a rule names is deleted any more, and the empty set a disabled operand yields is somebody's deliberate act, not a failure. A person with no birth date is not such a failure either: that operand resolves perfectly well, and they are simply not in the set.
    • A key is the third grantee, and it holds no expression. The seeded selector_type registry keeps its thirteen rows and none of them names a key, so there is nothing to compile for one and no set it could join — EVERYONE is every person, so a key is not even in the widest operand (D-117). What that keeps is this diagram's largest machinery unchanged: a grant naming a key is read as written, ruleset_version keeps the two tables that bump it, and api_key_id is a real foreign key, so the reference-less polymorphic keys stay the two they were. What it costs is that "grant this to the integrations" is not one sentence: twelve integrations need twelve grants, and the thirteenth is a write somebody performs.
    • Nothing owns an event (D-102). The people connected to an event are named by operands that bind to it — ORGANIZERS, PLACED, CONFIRMED, ATTENDED, ON_WAITLIST — each a set the item itself defines. An operand is a set, not a grant, which is why D-062's "an event_organizer row grants nothing" is the whole story: the row appoints, the operand resolves. The consequence worth remembering: a bound operand is illegal at the unit, so "every event's answers go to its organizers" is twelve item-scope allows written beside the event row by the act that creates the event, one set per event, in one transaction — and an event whose creation died partway through has nobody who can read its answers, which is what the events with an incomplete organizer bundle report is for — it counts every event, not only staffed ones, and names which of the twelve are absent. That failure has an author: one create, named in the trail, rather than a configuration step nobody took (D-151).
    • scope_id is polymorphic (no FK) so a new grantable item type is a config row, not a migration. Accepted cost, with four mandatory mitigations — the same trade-off and the same four mitigations hold for the second reference-less key, audience_operand.selector_arg_id (D-011, D-101).

    See ../data-model/05-authorization.md and ../domain/06-authorization-model.md.

    Key point: access_grant is a pentagon of references and grants exactly one thing — a grantee (person or audience or key), a scope, one permission, an effect, a period (D-099). Uniqueness follows the shape: at most one live allow per grantee, scope and permission — and because a unique index does not constrain a row whose grantee column is NULL, that one rule is three unique indexes, one counted over the rows naming a person, one over the rows naming an audience, one over the rows naming a key (D-116). Denies sit deliberately outside all three so a deny can sit beside an allow on the same item — that pair is the only way to say "this person has the unit's events, except this one". A person, a set and a key are three grantees, so the same code on the same item may live once for each of them, and no row there is another one's duplicate.

    No line leaves scope_id, and that is the diagram telling the truth. The edge that exists runs to grant_scope_type, the seeded registry (unit — the only row with is_item_scoped = false, event, person_group, role, document deferred) which is what states which table the unresolved id belongs to. The id itself is ⇒ polymorphic: no foreign key, validated by the registry plus a nightly orphan scan per type (D-011, §8). A unit grant carries no scope_id at all, which is the entire schema-level content of "the unit is a scope, not a row".

    A role is not a permission bundle. An office carries no codes of its own: what a treasurer has is the live grant rows whose audience names that office as ROLE(x), so "what may a treasurer do?" is a query over rows a screen can show (D-100). role_holder is a dated holder row — person, role, period, who granted it, note — and names one person, never a rule: an office that cannot answer "who was treasurer in 2023" is not an office record (F-12). role itself has no period: it is a definition, valid always, and the period lives on the holder row (D-053). The second arrow into person is granted_by, and it is drawn to a person because the column records who appointed — a fact carried on the appointment, readable five years later by whoever has to ask. The act of appointing is what audit_log holds, with its actor taken from the request context rather than from the row (../data-model/06-audit-and-import.md §2); the two are not one column's job, and every other actor column here (access_grant.granted_by, person_group_member.added_by) points the same way for the same reason. The first actor column in this appendix that does not point at a person is on a trail, not in this diagram: access_log.api_key_id and audit_log.api_key_id name the registered caller a request authenticated as, mutually exclusive with each trail's actor_person_id — a request is made by a person or by a key, never by both at once, so there is no actor column to disagree about and on_behalf is never set on a row whose actor is a key (D-121). A trail row naming neither is not a missing arrow: the empty column is this system acting on its own and a complete answer to who did this, and where the trail has a label column the writer's name comes from a fixed list rather than from free text (../data-model/00-conventions.md §7a, D-125).

    Names are one namespace. role.code, person_group.name and a set audience.code are unique together, case- and separator-folded, and every selector_type.code is reserved — so a group called MEMBERS is refused at the save rather than discovered later as an expression whose meaning moved. No constraint spans three tables, so the write path carries the rule and an integrity check asserts the write path still does (D-101, D-103, D-081). What is stored is always kind + id, so a rename never rewrites a grant.

    Five of these lines are ⇒ in the notation above, and not one of them is a foreign key. A leaf's selector_arg_id is one integer column pointing at role, person_group, audience, person or event depending on selector_kind — the same polymorphism, the same four mandatory mitigations and the same nightly orphan scan as access_grant.scope_id, the same trade-off accepted for both keys (D-011, D-101). Drawing it as five real edges would be the lie; drawing it as no edges at all would hide the fan. The registry is what resolves the ambiguity, and it is a real edge: selector_type says whether the code takes an argument, whether the argument-less form binds to the item being checked, and what type the argument is — the parser reads its reserved words from that table, the editor builds its dropdown from it, and the save path checks arity against it (D-101). It is seeded and organization-read-only — the whole table is written only by migrations and no screen edits it: "the language becomes data" has never meant the language is extensible at runtime, and a seeded code with no shipped evaluator arm is a deployment bug that surfaces as an empty set — which is why the registry holds no row waiting to be implemented.

    argument_type has six values and five of them are tables. OLDER and YOUNGER (D-107) take an integer — a whole number of years — so selector_arg_id holds 18 for OLDER(18), and this is the first operand argument in the registry that names no row. The five drawn edges are still exactly five, and the count this appendix keeps is unchanged for the right reason rather than by luck: a scalar sitting in selector_arg_id is not a reference, so there is nothing to repoint when a person is merged, nothing for a cascade to find, nothing for the orphan scan to walk, and the binding check that compares argument_type against a grant's item type simply does not apply to an operand that binds to no item. There are still exactly two reference-less polymorphic keys in what this part draws — access_grant.scope_id and audience_operand.selector_arg_id — and 18 is not one of them, because the check is about references. The shared inventory in §8 keeps the design's whole count, which is seven: the other five sit on the two trails and on a merge conflict, none of which is drawn here. The two operands read person.birth_date as of the date being checked, which is why they are a registry row and nothing more: they are ordinary named operands, composed by + - * like every other, and the set they name changes with no write at all.

    No leaf resolves to anything but people, and that is why there is no GRANTS(…). An operand resolves to a set of people, because a set of people is what a grantee that is a set is — the two grantees that are not sets, one named person and one registered key, name a single bearer and are reached without this tree at all (D-117); a set of grants is a filter over the grant list, and "these twenty rows, now also for that office" is something an editor does while finding the rows to act on, not something a stored grantee could ever be evaluated against. The bulk operation stays an affordance over the list, and the registry refuses the kind rather than leaving it unimplemented (D-101).

    One row is one node or one leaf, never both. A node carries operator (+ union, * intersection, - difference) with left_id and right_id, which are not interchangeable because - is not commutative; a leaf carries selector_kind and, where the registry says so, selector_arg_id. parent_id lives on the child, so a node belongs to exactly one expression and a sharing table is refused — editing a shared node would silently widen every grant using it. audience_id is denormalized onto every row so the read path fetches one audience's rows in one indexed read and assembles the tree in memory; it never walks a parent chain (D-101). OLDER and YOUNGER are two seeded rows of that registry and nothing more: the operator enum is + - *, an attribute adds no node kind and no column, so (MEMBERS * OLDER(18)) - YOUNGER(6) is stored with the shapes that already existed — a * node with a MEMBERS leaf and an OLDER leaf, under a - node whose right operand is a YOUNGER leaf (D-107).

    AUDIENCE(code) is the leaf that points back into this diagram (D-103): it names another saved audience by its code, which is why code exists and is optional, and why an unnamed audience is simply not composable. The AUDIENCE leaves are the reference edges, so "does this cycle", "how deep", "which grants name this audience" and "may I end it" are all one indexed query over selector_kind = 'AUDIENCE' rows. Why a saved expression can still cycle when every one of them is a tree: one tree cannot, but a forest of trees pointing at each other can. The save-time reachability walk therefore goes inside this tree and across every AUDIENCE(x) leaf, and it is the only structural guard there is — no cap on expression depth survives (D-105). The walk looks for cycles and nothing else: the tree is stored exactly as the writer wrote it, with no depth bound and no rebalancing, and nothing bounds what an expression may cost — affordable at the stated size of a few hundred people and a few dozen registrants per event, with a named trigger to reopen the question (D-112). Depth and width stay a convention, not a guard (D-081): there is no report of the widest and deepest audiences, and what the permissions page shows is the tab listing rules that name something deprecated, hidden or disabled or fail to resolve — silent about how wide any of them is (D-109). The protection that keeps the dangerous case out is that an audience named by a live grant may not be ended or deleted while that grant lives: under -, an emptied operand makes the answer wider.

    A hand-written set is an expression like any other (D-105), written as PERSON(a) + PERSON(b) in the same tree as every GROUP(x) and MEMBERS leaf. What that gives up is written as lost, not as managed: no per-person dates — a person is in the audience or not, and a June-to-August exception is a second grant with its own period, which is what D-099's shape is for — and no record of who added a person beyond who last saved the tree. Why a name in an expression was never merged into GROUP(x): a group's members are the organization's record and are true with no grant pointing at them, while a name written into an expression is meaningless outside the expression it completes (D-098). Naming people inside a grant must not make them group members, and (GROUP(kitchen) + PERSON(honza)) - PERSON(a) says the whole thing: the same set as any list spelling, one fewer kind of thing in the model, and no chance of the permission table becoming the system's roster.

    PERSON(x) is drawn as the person argument edge, and the edge has exactly one occupant. PERSON is one of the thirteen seeded selector_type rows — the seeded registry, not an evaluator's arm list, is the record of what the language accepts (D-107, D-104, D-113). A guardian is not a second occupant. Guardianship is a level over a child — none, view or write read off the guardianship row (D-074) — and not a set the expression language resolves, so an operand aimed at guardians would offer, one code at a time, a subset of what those people already hold (D-113). What the registry row adds over the storage fact — selector_arg_id may hold a person id (D-011) — is one fact of meaning: an expression may name a single human who is on nobody's roster. Since a hand-written set is a sum of these (D-105), the edge is load-bearing: every hand-named person in the system is a selector_arg_id with deliberately no foreign key. PERSON is the one argument kind the repoint duty still reaches (D-109): retiring an office, a group or an audience is one write to that row and no expression is edited, because nothing a rule names is deleted — it goes deprecated, hidden or disabled, and only disabled changes the answer. A person is not in that protected set, because D-088 lets a redaction require the row to actually go and erasure wins. So a merge repoints the PERSON leaves naming the absorbed person, a redaction leaves the rest resolving to the empty set, and a redacted person is the only remaining way to produce a broken leaf — shown on the permissions tab rather than in a report nobody opens, with the nightly orphan scan as backstop rather than mechanism. PERSON, ROLE, GROUP and AUDIENCE are the four codes whose argument names a person, an office, a group or another audience. The fifth drawn edge, to event, belongs to the item-bound codes — ORGANIZERS, PLACED, CONFIRMED, ATTENDED, ON_WAITLIST — whose argument is optional exactly because the item being checked usually supplies it (D-102).
